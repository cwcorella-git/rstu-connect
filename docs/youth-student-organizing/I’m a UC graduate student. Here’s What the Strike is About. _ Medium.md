A rally on Memorial Glade at UC Berkeley, one of the 10 UC campuses, which together on November 14 declared an indefinite all-grad student strike for a cost of living adjustment (COLA). [Image Source](https://48hills.org/2022/11/inside-the-uc-academic-workers-strike/)

The largest strike of 2022 is here, and again it is from education workers of California. 48,000 workers, primarily graduate students but also postdocs, some undergraduates, and other staff, have walked off the job only a few weeks before student finals at the end of the quarter. I am one of them. I think this is a significant fight that all progressive forces in this country should be aware of, for reasons I'll explain. We will focus on the graduate students, both because they dominate the strike's numbers and because I am more familiar with their situation, though some of this discussion is also relevant for the other bargaining units.

At first glance, the terms of bargaining are the same as any labor negotiation: the union's demand, a salary of \$54,000 per year for all grads is "unrealistically high" and will be bargained downwards. The university, for their part, is bargaining with small increases, offering first 3%, and now up to a 7% increase. Their offer hasn't yet beaten inflation, but surely they'll get there soon enough. An outside observer would think that we will soon enough reach a compromise, somewhere between our number and theirs. But unfortunately, that is not a realistic telling of what is going on.

The difference between the demand and the offer is not a minor break, but a chasm. Current wages range from around \$23K — \$30K per year (depending on how or whether you're funded in the summer), so the union's \$54K proposal is more on the order of a 100% wage increase; the single-digit percent increases offered so far, even if they were doubled or tripled, would not approach our demand. Indeed, the union argues that the gulf is so vast that the offers they have presented in recent bargaining sessions are not serious, and are done in bad faith. Does the split come from intransigent greed on the part of the UC regents, as some strikers claim? I don't believe so, at least not in the traditional, capitalist sense. Certainly, some of the overpaid, redundant top admins are probably aware that they siphon off a big chunk of student tuition and state funding, but even so, as individuals their salaries only account for a fraction of the demanded funds. Does the split come from laughable naïveté of graduate students, who have vastly overvalued their services, as the UC administration believes? No. There is a straightforward logic to the striker's demands. I believe the gulf between the offer and the demand comes from a fundamental disagreement about the meaning and value of education. Grad students aren't demanding a pay raise so much as a paradigm shift.

The union has dropped all abstractions about our hourly rate or percentage increases. Instead, we are talking about expenses. We say, "Pay us enough to live here!" which, simply means that we cannot afford to pay for housing and food and transportation and childcare, while rent takes up 50 percent, 60 percent, or more of our paycheck. [About 1 in 4 grads are](https://regents.universityofcalifornia.edu/regmeet/july16/f2.pdf) [food insecure, and about 1 in 20 grads are homeless.](https://regents.universityofcalifornia.edu/regmeet/july16/f2.pdf) We are demanding enough income that we can afford housing, food, and other basic needs. Specifically, we demand that rent for a single person, with their own room in a shared apartment, should not be more than 1/3 of their income; people paying more than this on rent are called "rent burdened". The union's proposal comes directly from this: the calculation is essentially to take the average housing cost in the California cities hosting

UCs (~\$1500 per month) and multiply by 3, giving a monthly salary of \$4500, and since we live here all 12 months of the year, this gives an annual salary of \$54000. While the details of this could be haggled over (the scale-invariant notion of "too high = rent burden = 33%" frankly still feels sloppy to me, and personally I'm not as attached to it as other grads are¹), the idea we will not budge from is that we are no longer talking about "appropriate compensation for our labor", but instead about "making enough to live". We at least need enough left over after rent to afford everything else.

However, if we look at the "official" breakdown of our work and compensation, we get a very different story about grad student pay. There are basically two graduate student units, paid very similarly² — the first group is Academic Student Employees, or ASEs, who generally have a title of "Teacher Assisistant" (TA) or "Graduate Student Instructor" (GSI), and the second group is Graduate Student Researchers, or GSRs, with a title of "Research Assistant" (RA). According to our contract, ASEs work 20 hours a week to deliver instruction to students. While they don't have a contract yet, roughly the same is true for GSRs, who, rather than working 20 hours a week on student instruction, work 20 hours a week on "their PI's research". Both groups of students are expected to perform an additional 20 hours a week of research and/or coursework for their own degree, depending on their program and the stage of their thesis. Thus, they are "full-time" students, but only "work" 20 hours a week. Their pay, for those 20 hours, is an apparently reasonable \$28.11 per hour (calculated by dividing my paycheck of \$2249 per month by [4

weeks per month x 20 hours per week]). On top of that, the UC notes, grad workers get their tuition waived,³ which, divided by a 20 hour workweek, we can calculate to be worth another \$18.26 per hour (by taking the quarterly "Graduate Tuition" fee remissions of \$4384, dividing by [3 months per quarter x 4 weeks per month x 20 hours per week]). Adding these together, we arrive at a "total compensation" of \$46.37 per hour. This is the story the UC is negotiating around, and presented this way, it looks like an amazing hourly rate!

Something doesn't add up. It seems like they're paying us really well, and we're claiming to only want a bare minimum to live. So how is our demand almost twice their offer? Why do these numbers not align? This is, of course, related to the fact that the cost of living has skyrocketed in college towns, which have as a rule not increased their housing stock in nearly proportionate numbers as students have been added to the universities, and to the more general housing crisis in California. (See my [previous article](https://medium.com/@re. Marx/what-makes-housing-so-expensive-abff0f72bc7d) on this topic)

However, the unaffordability of housing has only increased the immediacy of our demand. It didn't introduce the basic problem built into our higher education system. The union's "enough to live" demand leaves this contradiction implicit, but I think it is time to make it explicit. The problem is that **we're not part-time**. This is what we're really saying when we say we should in fact be able to survive on our wages, alone, without additional jobs. The positions of the administration and the grads on this are totally incompatible.

- Admin's view: In the university's estimation, graduate students are primarily students. In principle, they continue to pay checks to the UC Regents for their "tuition" to the tune of \$13,000 per year, in exchange for a 20 hour per week "education". On the "labor" side, TAs and RAs are paid ~\$46 per hour, for 20 hours per week. The \$54K salary we are demanding, with this perspective, works out to a totally bonkers \$86**⁴** per hour. The University won't stand for that! In their eyes, the university is doing grad students a favor by hiring them in these positions. This arrangement saves grads some hassle in finding other jobs, and saves admin the effort of finding qualified applicants for these positions. **According to the UC, grads are lucky that their (part-time) job TAing or doing research is enough to even pay their tuition, and it is no surprise that it doesn't also support them living a full adult life. If they want to do that, they ought to be externally supported by their parents or their partners, and barring that support they ought to take out loans, get a second or third job, or drop out.** Significantly, this is what we have all accepted undergraduates should have to deal with, with the difference being that there is no job whatsoever that will hire them while doing their undergrad and fully pay for their tuition. Why should grads be exempted from this? • Grads' view: In our actual experience, which is reflected in the union demands, graduate students are full-time, adult workers. Our "tuition" is a fiction which should be totally neglected. During the majority of a doctoral program, grads don't even take classes — and outside those, we provide at least as much value to the university in research as they do to us through mentorships and services. No grad student should either pay tuition or consider it "part of their pay". **Grad student wages ought to be calculated on the 40+ hours per week we actually put in, to both research and teaching (or "our PI's research" and "our thesis research", as the case may be), not based on the 20 hours specified in the contract. We should not need additional jobs, loans, or help from our parents to survive while in graduate school.** If we use the full 40 hours of our workweek, including working over the summer (which we do) to calculate current grad student wages, they are a pathetic \$10.54 per hour, well below the California minimum wage (the above \$28.11 per hour, divided by two to reflect 40 hour weeks instead of 20, and multiplied by 3/4 to account for the unpaid summer quarter). We believe, rather than being treated as part-time student workers lucky to have tuition paid, graduate students should instead be treated as early-stage career academics, and paid for all their work at a reasonable rate. If the administration were to give us the union proposal of \$54K a year, our hourly wage would work out to \$54,000 per year divided by [50 week per year x 40 hour per week] — A respectable, and moderate, \$25.96 per hour.

**So the UC thinks they already pay us \$46.37 per hour and the union is demanding \$86.09 per hour, while in our actual experience, we currently make \$10.54 per hour and with our union we are demanding \$25.96 per hour. The whole of the conflict, and the gaping chasm between the bargaining proposals, can be reduced to the difference between seeing grads as 20 hour parttime workers, paid in part with a tuition remission for**

**their schooling, and seeing them as they are: as 40 hour full-time academic workers, whose labor contributes tremendous value to the UC. If we have the first perspective, the current contract is quite good, and the union's demand is outrageous. But if we correctly take the second perspective, the current contract is pathetically bad, and our demand is the most basic of living wages.** Which view of grads is right?

The part-time view is an extension of the commodified view of education which remains pervasive in this country. In prior eras, education in general was a commodity, bought by the idle rich merely for their own self-fulfillment and enjoyment. As higher education has become more common, it has not been decommodified, but instrumentalized — the expensive degree is supposed to "pay off" through increased future wages. But in the last few decades, college degrees have only helped their holders in a totally perverse way, i.e. by neoliberal work culture increasing exploitation of those who don't get a degree, rather than rewarding the people who do.

In the eyes of the university and media/social infrastructure of this country, the grad student is nothing more than a supercharged undergraduate. Their degree takes longer, delays their entry to society further into adulthood, but it is supposed to be a tool which generates positively astronomical salaries when students finally finish. The extra costs of their debt and/or the extent of their continued support by others are (supposedly) proportionate to the benefit they gain by their advanced degrees.

Even if an advanced degree did provide ample benefits down the line, it would still be right to fight for a living wage for grad students. Decommodification of education can only be achieved by both lowering the "cost" of getting a degree, and also shrinking the value of the "returns" the degree confers. To lower the "cost" requires additional state investment into education, shrinking tuition for all and raising pay for student workers. Narrowing the "returns" gap is more complex, and the burden doesn't primarily fall on those with degrees. Those without them will have to fight for themselves as well — for strong unions and working class politics. Our grad strike works to decommodify the degree primarily on the "cost" side. But still, at the very least this fight will make advanced degrees more accessible for working-class families, and the increased ubiquity this brings to grad degrees will help to close the "returns" gap as well.

However, we don't even need to worry about this gap if it is already [slipping away](https://www.theatlantic.com/business/archive/2011/09/the-value-of-college-is-a-growing-b-flat-c-falling-d-all-of-the-above/245746/) of its own accord. The benefits of graduate degrees can be greatly overstated. Academia is essentially saturated, so degrees in fields with less monetizable potential give very little extra earnings except to a very lucky few, and in lucrative, technical fields, the graduate degree is not worth much compared to spending those same 7 years working full-time in the industry. But while our degree's commodity value can (and should) collapse, its social contribution, i.e. the value we create in doing it, remains strong and vital to our community and planet. If our degree no longer gives us the elite status it used to, we can make our peace with that — but in exchange, the paradigm must shift, and we must be paid to do our Ph. D. research. This means explicitly, we must be paid enough to live here, to have adult lives, families, housing, and so on, without taking on other jobs. The expectation that grads put in work outside their own research, be it grading, doing lab grunt-work, or teaching classes, doesn't need to be abolished. But the farcical notion that that's the only work they do, while their own research is just an experience that they're paying the university to have, does. And we will fight until the UCs, and all research universities, recognize this.

Some of these problems are the same for undergrads, who also pay outrageous tuition just to work long hours on hard assignments, or even research — so why is the strike a graduate affair? Frankly, it isn't because the injustice is greater; it's just because the grads are both more desperate to rectify it, and in a position of more power. The reason we are more desperate is because we are older than undergrads (on average, graduating at 33), and are more concerned with starting families and living our lives. Compared to our classmates from our undergraduate work, grads have already fallen behind in terms of savings and paying down student loan debt. The fact that a Ph. D. takes significantly longer than an undergraduate degree (which, again, we've already done on top of the grad degree) only exacerbates this frustration. And we are more powerful because the University needs our labor. If grad students don't work, the university can't run the majority of its class sessions, and almost none of its grading. However, don't get it twisted: our strike is the short-term leverage we have in this fight, which will make them notice us immediately, but it's not the long-term danger this part-time vs full-time contradiction poses to the UC. They aren't scared primarily because they rely on our grading services. While they do in the short term, it's easy to hire scabs to do that if the strike goes on too long. The real danger is that if a critical mass of us drops out, citing poverty, it hollows out their prestigious research capacity (i.e. our "unpaid" labor). There's no scabs for that.

Overwhelmingly, grads want to be here, and want to do their work, both on the officially paid and officially unpaid sides. But we must be treated with dignity. The university's story about "part-time" grads is already a transparent fiction, and the vast majority of UC grads (and, let's face it, grad students throughout the United States) rightly treat their studies as a real job. [Decades ago,](https://livinghistory.as.ucsb.edu/2020/07/30/graduate-student-organizing-timeline/) UC grads refused to accept paying tuition as part of the deal, and now, as the housing market obliterates the paltry income we make outside this, we can no longer accept sub-minimum wages either. Is the UC ready to only have as grads those wealthy enough to be supported by their parents into their 30s? I think not — so we will eventually reach some compromise. I don't know if we will get all the way to \$54K. But no offer will be good enough to end the strike until it pays us like a reasonably good full-time job, whatever bullshit accounting is necessary.

Our labor must be treated like the full-time scientific and social work it is. The UC is not unique in their "part-time" stance, but they will be the first large university system to have this position seriously challenged. This is due to the intersection of our pay being worse than our elite competitor private universities, and our housing markets being worse than our peer public universities in other states. But we should warn the administrations at all these other research universities: their time is coming, too.

If rent increases are decoupled from other costs and increase much more rapidly than inflation, which we usually argue they are (except when doing this specific calculation), the obvious result is that you can survive just fine at a higher proportion of your salary going to rent, as long as you have enough left over to cover everything else. Its much more significant in my view that the UC's wage increases haven't matched rent increases in absolute terms, i.e. if after a year your rent increases by \$100/ mo and your wages go up by \$50/mo, than that wage increases don't match rent increases in relative terms, i.e. after a year your rent increases 10% and your wages go up 5%. The first is actual ground lost in quality of life, the second is just a reallocation of your income into different bins. Fortunately

(well, unfortunately) for us, we are clearly losing ground by either metric. But I wouldn't be surprised if the UC balks at the arbitrary, relative terms of our wage demand. We're so far behind that even if we don't "get out of rent burden," what we need to live will still inevitably be looked at as outrageous.
