**French Americans** or **Franco-Americans** [\(French:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language) Francoaméricains) are [citizens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizenship_of_the_United_States) or [nationals of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_nationality_law) who identify themselves with having full or partial [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_people) or [French-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Canadian)[Canadian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Canadian) heritage, [ethnicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_group) and/or [ancestral ties.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancestor) [\[2\]](#page-36-2)[\[3\]](#page-36-3)[\[4\]](#page-36-4) They include [French-Canadian Americans,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Canadian_Americans) whose experience and identity differ from the broader community.

The state with the largest proportion of people identifying as having French ancestry is [Maine,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maine) while the state with the largest number of people with French ancestry is California. Many [U. S. cities have large French American populations.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_U. S._cities_with_large_French_American_populations) The city with the largest concentration of people of French extraction is [Madawaska, Maine,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madawaska,_Maine) while the largest Frenchspeaking population by percentage of speakers in the U. S. is found in [St. Martin Parish, Louisiana.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Martin_Parish,_Louisiana)

Country-wide, as of 2020, there are about 9.4 million U. S. residents who declare French ancestr[y\[5\]](#page-37-0) or French Canadian descent, and about 1.32 millio[n\[6\]](#page-37-1) per the [2010 census,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_United_States_census) spoke [French at home.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language_in_the_United_States) [\[7\]](#page-37-2)[\[8\]](#page-37-3) An additional 750,000 U. S. residents speak a [French-based creole language,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French-based_creole_language) according to the 2011

#### [American Community Survey.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Community_Survey) [\[9\]](#page-37-4)

Franco-Americans are less visible than other similarly sized ethnic groups and are relatively uncommon when compared to the size of France's population, or to the numbers of German, Italian, Irish or English Americans. This is partly due to the tendency of Franco-American groups to identify more closely with North American regional identities such as [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Canadians) [Canadian,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Canadians) [Acadian,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acadians) [Brayon,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brayon) [Louisiana French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana_French_people) [\(Cajun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cajuns), [Creole\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana_Creole_people) than as a coherent group, but also because emigration from France during the 19th century was low compared to the rest of Europe. Consequently, there is less of a unified French American identity as with other [European American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_American) ethnic groups, and Americans of French descent are highly concentrated in [New England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_England) and [Louisiana.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana) Nevertheless, the French presence has had an outsized impact on American [toponyms.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toponym)

Some Franco-Americans arrived prior to the [founding of the](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolution) [United States,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolution) settling in places like the [Midwest,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midwestern_United_States) [Louisiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana) or [Northern New England.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_England) In these same areas, many cities and geographic features retain their names given by the first Franco-American inhabitants, and in sum, 23 of the [Contiguous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contiguous_United_States) [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contiguous_United_States) were colonized in part by French pioneers or French Canadians, including settlements such as Iowa [\(Des](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Des_Moines,_Iowa) [Moines\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Des_Moines,_Iowa), Missouri [\(St. Louis\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Louis), Kentucky [\(Louisville\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisville,_Kentucky) and Michigan [\(Detroit\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detroit), among others[.\[10\]](#page-37-5) Settlers and political refugees from the [Kingdom of France,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_France) including [Huguenots,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huguenots) also settled alongside French-speaking [Flemish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flanders) [Walloons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walloons) in the Dutch colony of [New Amsterdam,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Amsterdam) the capital of [New](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Netherland)

[Netherland,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Netherland) which later became [New York City.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City) [\[11\]](#page-37-6)[\[12\]](#page-37-7) While found throughout the country, today Franco-Americans are most numerous in [New England,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_England) northern [New York,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_(state)) the Midwest, Louisiana, and [northern California.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_California) Often, Franco-Americans are identified more specifically as being of [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Canadians) [Canadians,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Canadians) [Cajuns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cajuns) or [Louisiana Creole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana_Creole) descent[.\[13\]](#page-38-0)

A vital segment of Franco-American history involves the [Quebec diaspora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quebec_diaspora) of the 1840s–1930s, in which nearly one million [French Canadians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Canadian) moved to the United States, mainly relocating to New England mill towns, fleeing economic downturn in Québec and seeking manufacturing jobs in the United States. Historically, French Canadians had among the [highest birth rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Revanche_des_berceaux) in world history, explaining their relatively large population despite low immigration rates from France. These immigrants mainly settled in [Québec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qu%C3%A9bec) and [Acadia,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acadia) although some eventually inhabited [Ontario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontario) and [Manitoba.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manitoba) Many of the first French-Canadian migrants to the U. S. worked in the New England lumber industry, and, to a lesser degree, in the burgeoning [mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mining) industry in the upper [Great Lakes.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Lakes) This initial wave of seasonal migration was then followed by more permanent relocation in the United States by French-Canadian millworkers.

## ![](_page_4_Figure_4.jpeg)

Map of [New France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_France) about 1750 in North America

Louisiana Creole people refers to those who are descended from the colonial settlers in Louisiana, especially those of French and Spanish descent but also including individuals of [mixed-race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiracial_people) heritage (cf. [Creoles of Color\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creoles_of_color). Louisiana Creoles of any race have common European heritage and share cultural ties, such as the traditional use of the French language and the continuing practice of [Catholicism;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholicism) in most cases, the people are related to each other. Those of mixed race also sometimes have African and Native American ancestry[.\[14\]](#page-38-1) As a group, the mixed-race Creoles rapidly began to acquire education, skills (many in New Orleans worked as craftsmen and artisans), businesses and property. They were overwhelmingly Catholic, spoke Colonial French (although some also spoke [Louisiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana_Creole) [Creole\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana_Creole) and kept up many French social customs, modified by other parts of their ancestry and Louisiana culture. The [free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_people_of_color) [people of color](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_people_of_color) married among themselves to maintain their class and social culture.

The Cajuns of Louisiana have a unique heritage, generally seeing themselves as distinct from Louisiana Creoles despite a number of historical documents also classifying the Acadians' descendants as Créoles. Their ancestors settled [Acadia,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acadia) in what is now the Canadian provinces of [New Brunswick,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Brunswick) [Nova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nova_Scotia) [Scotia,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nova_Scotia) [Prince Edward Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Edward_Island) and part of [Maine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maine) in the 17th and early 18th centuries. In 1755, after capturing [Fort Beauséjour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Beaus%C3%A9jour) and several other French forts in the region, British authorities demanded the Acadians swear an oath of loyalty to the [British](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Crown) [Crown,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Crown) which the majority refused to do. In response, the British deported them to the [Thirteen Colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirteen_Colonies) in the south in what has become known as the [expulsion of the Acadians.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expulsion_of_the_Acadians)

Over the next generation, some four thousand Acadians made the long trek to Louisiana, where they began a new life. The name Cajun is a corruption of the word Acadian. Many still live in what is known as the [Cajun Country,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acadiana) where much of their colonial culture survives. [French Louisiana,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana_(New_France)) when it was [sold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana_Purchase) by [Napoleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon) in 1803, covered all or part of fifteen current [U. S.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U. S._states) [states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U. S._states) and contained French and Canadian colonists dispersed across it, though they were most numerous in its southernmost portion.

During the [War of 1812,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_1812) Louisiana residents of French origin took part on the American side in the [Battle of New Orleans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_New_Orleans) (December 23, 1814, through January 8, 1815). [Jean Lafitte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Lafitte) and his Baratarians later were honored by US General [Andrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Jackson) [Jackson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Jackson) for their contribution to the defense of New Orleans. [\[15\]](#page-38-2)

In Louisiana today, more than 15 percent of the population of the [Cajun Country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cajun_Country) reported in the [2000 United States census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2000_United_States_census) that French was spoken at home[.\[16\]](#page-38-3)

Another significant source of immigrants to Louisiana was [Saint-Domingue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Domingue) (today [Haiti\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haiti); many [Saint Dominicans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Dominicans) fled during this time, and half of the diaspora eventually settled in New Orleans[.\[17\]](#page-38-4)

[Biloxi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biloxi,_Mississippi) in [Mississippi,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mississippi) and [Mobile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile,_Alabama) in [Alabama,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alabama) still contain French American heritage since they were founded by the Canadian [Pierre Le Moyne d'Iberville.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Le_Moyne_d%27Iberville)

The [Houma Tribe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Houma_Tribe) in Louisiana still speak the same French they had been taught 300 years ago.

In the 17th and early 18th centuries, there was an influx of a few thousand [Huguenots,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huguenots) who were [Calvinist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvinism) refugees fleeing religious persecution following the issuance of the 1685 [Edict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edict_of_Fontainebleau) [of Fontainebleau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edict_of_Fontainebleau) by [Louis XIV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_XIV_of_France) of the [Kingdom of France.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_France) [\[18\]](#page-38-5) Some of these refugees settled in the Dutch colony of [New](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Netherland) [Netherland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Netherland) and its capital city, [New Netherland,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Netherland) including being among the first Europeans to settle on [Staten Island.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staten_Island) [\[12\]](#page-37-7) In 1674, with the signing of the [Treaty of Westminster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Westminster_(1674)) to end the [Third Anglo-Dutch War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Anglo-Dutch_War) (1672–1674), the [Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netherlands) ceded the colony to [Great Britain,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Great_Britain) who renamed the colony [New York,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_New_York) and its capital to [New York City,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City) after [Prince James,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_II_of_England) [Duke of York,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_II_of_England) the brother of [King Charles II of England.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_II_of_England)

For nearly a century, French settlers fostered a distinctive French Protestant identity that enabled them to remain aloof from American society, but by the time of the [American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolution) [Revolution,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolution) they had generally intermarried and merged into the larger [Presbyterian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyterian) community[.\[19\]](#page-38-6) In 1700, they constituted 13% of the white population of the [Province of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Carolina) [Carolina,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Carolina) and 5% of the white population of the [Province of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_New_York)

[New York.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_New_York) [\[18\]](#page-38-5) The largest number settling in [South Carolina,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Carolina) where the French comprised 4% of the white population in 1790[.\[20\]](#page-39-0)[\[21\]](#page-39-1) With the help of the well-organized international Huguenot community, many also moved to Virginia.[\[22\]](#page-39-2) In the north, [Paul Revere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Revere) of [Boston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston) was a prominent figure.

A new influx of French-heritage people occurred at the very end of the colonial era. Following the failed [invasion of Quebec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invasion_of_Quebec_(1775)) [in 1775-1776](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invasion_of_Quebec_(1775)), hundreds of French-Canadian men who had enlisted in the [Continental Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_Army) remained in the ranks. Under colonels James Livingston and Moses Hazen, they saw military action across the main theaters of the Revolutionary War. At the end of the war, New York State formed the Canadian and Nova Scotia Refugee Tract stretching westward from Lake Champlain. Though many of the veterans sold their claim in this vast region, some remained and the settlement held. From early colonizing efforts in the 1780s to the era of Quebec's "great hemorrhage," the French-Canadian presence in Clinton County in northeastern New York was inescapable.[\[23\]](#page-39-3)

#### **New England and New York State**

# [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=French_Americans&action=edit§ion=5)

In the nineteenth century, many people of French heritage arrived from [Quebec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quebec) and [New Brunswick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Brunswick) to work in manufacturing cities, especially textile centers, in New England and New York State. They came together in enclaves known as ["Little Canadas"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Canada_(term)). In the same period, Francophones from Quebec became a majority of workers in other regions and sectors, for instance the saw mill and logging camps in the [Adirondack Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adirondack_Mountains) and their foothills. They amounted to an ever-growing share of the region's population; by the midtwentieth century, Franco-Americans comprised 30 percent of Maine's population[.\[27\]](#page-39-7)

![](_page_10_Picture_3.jpeg)

The [Statue of Liberty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statue_of_Liberty) is a gift from the French people in memory of the [American Declaration of Independence.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Declaration_of_Independence)

Factories could provide employment to entire nuclear families, including children. Some French-Canadian women saw New England as a place of opportunity and possibility where they could create economic alternatives for themselves distinct from the expectations of their farm families in Canada. By the early twentieth century, some saw temporary migration to the United States as a rite of passage and a time of self-discovery and self-reliance. Most moved permanently to the United States, using the inexpensive railroad system to visit Quebec from time to time. When these women did marry, they had fewer children with longer intervals between children than their Canadian counterparts. Some women never married and oral accounts suggest that self-reliance and economic independence were important reasons for choosing work over marriage and motherhood. These women conformed to traditional gender ideals in order to retain their 'Canadienne' cultural identity, but they also redefined these roles in ways that provided them increased independence as wives and mothers[.\[28\]](#page-39-8)[\[29\]](#page-40-0) Women also shaped the Franco-American experience as members of religious orders. The first hospital in Lewiston, Maine, became a reality in 1889 when the Sisters of Charity of Montreal, the 'Grey Nuns,' opened the Asylum of Our Lady of Lourdes. This hospital was central to the Grey Nuns' mission of providing social services for Lewiston's predominately French-Canadian mill workers. The Grey Nuns struggled to establish their institution despite meager financial resources, language barriers, and opposition from the established medical community[.\[30\]](#page-40-1)[\[31\]](#page-40-2)

Members of [the French community in Holyoke, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Franco-Americans_in_Holyoke,_Massachusetts) taking English classes at a YMCA night school, 1902

The French-Canadian community in the Northeast tried to preserve its inherited cultural norms. This happened within the institutions of the Catholic Church, though it involved struggling with little success against Irish clerics. According to Raymond Potvin, the predominantly Irish hierarchy was slow to recognize the need for French-language parishes; several bishops even called for assimilation and English language-only parochial schools. By the twentieth century, a number of parochial schools for Francophone students opened, though they gradually closed later in the century and a large share of the French-speaking population left the Church. At the same time, the number of priests available to staff these parishes diminished[.\[32\]](#page-40-3) Like Church institutions, such Franco-American newspapers as Le Messager and [La Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Justice) served as pillars of the ideology of survivance—the effort to preserve the traditional culture through faith and language.[\[33\]](#page-40-4) A product of the commercial and industrial economy of these areas, by 1913, the French and French-Canadian populations of New York City, Fall River (Massachusetts), and Manchester (New Hampshire) were the largest in the country. Out of the 20 largest Franco-American populations in the United States, only four cities were outside of New York and New England, with New Orleans ranking 18th largest in the nation[.\[34\]](#page-40-5) Because of this, a number of French institutions were established in New England, including the Société Historique Franco-américaine in Boston and the [Union Saint-Jean-Baptiste d'Amérique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_Saint-Jean-Baptiste_d%E2%80%99Am%C3%A9rique) of [Woonsocket,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woonsocket) the largest French-Catholic cultural and mutual benefit society in the United States in the early twentieth century[.\[35\]](#page-41-0) Immigration from Quebec dwindled in the 1920s. Amid the decline of the textile industry from the 1920s to the 1950s, the French element experienced a period of upward mobility and assimilation. This pattern of assimilation increased during the 1970s and 1980s as many Catholic organizations switched to English and parish children entered public schools; some parochial schools closed in the 1970s.[\[27\]](#page-39-7)

Franco-Americans were one of the most important Catholic groups in the [Union army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_army) during the [American Civil War.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War) The exact number is unclear, but thousands of Franco-Americans appear to have served in this conflict. Union forces did not keep reliable statistics concerning foreign enlistments. However, historians have estimated anywhere from 20,000 to 40,000 Franco-Americans serving in this war. In addition to those born in the United States, many who served in the Union forces came from Canada or had resided there for several years. [Canada's national anthem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O_Canada) was written by such a soldier named [Calixa Lavallée,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calixa_Lavall%C3%A9e) who wrote this anthem while he served for the Union, attaining the rank of Lieutenant[.\[56\]](#page-43-4) Confederate General [P. G. T. Beauregard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P._G._T._Beauregard) was a notable Louisiana Creole.

Walker (1962) examines the voting behavior in U. S. presidential elections from 1880 to 1960, using election returns from 30 Franco-American communities in New England, along with sample survey data for the 1948–60 elections. According to Walker, from 1896 to 1924, Franco-Americans typically supported the [Republican Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republican_Party_(United_States)) because of its conservatism, emphasis on order, and advocacy of the tariff to protect the textile workers from foreign competition. In 1928, with Catholic [Al Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Smith) as the Democratic candidate, the Franco-Americans moved over to the Democratic column and stayed there for six presidential elections. They formed part of the [New Deal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Deal_Coalition) [Coalition.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Deal_Coalition) Unlike the Irish and German Catholics, very few Franco-Americans deserted the Democratic ranks because of the foreign policy and war issues of the 1940 and 1944 campaigns. In 1952 many Franco-Americans broke from the Democrats but returned heavily in 1960[.\[57\]](#page-43-5)

Additional work has expanded Walker's findings. Ronald Petrin has explored the rise of the Republican ascendency among Massachusetts Franco-Americans in the 1890s; the lengthy economic depression that coincided with President [Grover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grover_Cleveland) [Cleveland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grover_Cleveland)'s administration and Franco-Irish religious controversies were likely factors in growing support for the GOP. Petrin recognizes different political behaviors in large cities and in smaller centers[.\[58\]](#page-43-6) Madeleine Giguère has confirmed the later shift to the Democratic column through her research on Lewiston's presidential vote during the twentieth century[.\[59\]](#page-43-7) In the most in-depth study of Franco-American political choices, Patrick Lacroix finds different patterns of partisan engagement across New England and New York State.

In southern New England, Republicans actively courted the "Franco" vote and offered nominations. The party nominated [Aram J. Pothier,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aram_J._Pothier) a native of Quebec, who won his bid for the governorship of Rhode Island and served seven terms in that office. In northern New England, Franco-Americans faced exclusion from the halls of power and more easily turned towards the Democrats. During the 1920s, the regional disparity disappeared. Due to the nativist and anti-labor policies of Republican state governments, an increasingly unionized Franco-American working class lent its support to the Democrats across the region. Elite "Francos" continued to prefer the GOP[.\[60\]](#page-43-8)

As the ancestors of most Franco-Americans had for the most part left France before the [French Revolution,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution) they usually prefer the [fleur-de-lis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fleur-de-lis) to the modern [French tricolor.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_France) [\[61\]](#page-43-9)

Most Franco Americans have a [Roman Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic) heritage (which includes most French Canadians and Cajuns). Protestants would arrive in two smaller waves, with the earliest arrivals being the [Huguenots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huguenots) who fled from France in the colonial era, many of whom would settle in Boston, Charleston, New York and Philadelphia[.\[69\]](#page-44-4) Huguenots and their descendants would immigrate to the Massachusetts Bay Colony and the provinces of Pennsylvania and Carolina due in large part to colonial anti-Catholic sentiment, during the period of the [Edict of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edict_of_Fontainebleau) [Fontainebleau.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edict_of_Fontainebleau) [\[70\]](#page-45-0) The 19th century would see the arrival of others from Switzerland[.\[71\]](#page-45-1)

From the 1870s to the 1920s in particular, there was tension between the English-speaking [Irish Catholics,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Catholics) who dominated the Church in New England, and the French-Canadian immigrants, who wanted their language taught in the parochial schools. # **French language in the United States**

# [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=French_Americans&action=edit§ion=16)

According to the National Education Bureau, French is the second most commonly taught foreign language in American schools, behind Spanish. The percentage of people who learn French language in the United States is 12.3%[.\[64\]](#page-44-2) French was the most commonly taught foreign language until the 1980s; a subsequent influx of [Hispanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispanic) immigrants aided the growth of Spanish into the 21st century. According to the U. S. 2000 Census, French is the third most spoken language in the United States after English and Spanish, with 2,097,206 speakers, up from 1,930,404 in 1990. The language is also commonly spoken by Haitian immigrants in Florida and New York City.[\[81\]](#page-46-3)

As a result of French immigration to what is now the United States in the 17th and 18th centuries, the French language was once widely spoken in a few dozen scattered villages in the [Midwest.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midwestern_United_States) Migrants from Quebec after 1860 brought the language to New England. French-language newspapers existed in many American cities; especially New Orleans and in certain cities in New England. Americans of French descent often lived in predominantly French neighborhoods; where they attended schools and churches that used their language. Before 1920 French Canadian neighborhoods were sometimes known as "Little Canada"[.\[82\]](#page-46-4)

After 1960, the "Little Canadas" faded away[.\[83\]](#page-46-5) There were few French-language institutions other than Catholic churches. There were some French newspapers, but they had a total of only 50,000 subscribers in 1935[.\[84\]](#page-46-6) The World War II generation avoided bilingual education for their children, and insisted they speak English[.\[85\]](#page-46-7) By 1976, nine in ten Franco Americans usually spoke English and scholars generally agreed that "the younger generation of Franco-American youth had rejected their heritage.["\[86\]](#page-46-8)

## Francophone flags of North America

The **Franco-American flag** is an [ethnic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_group) [flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag) adopted at a Franco-American conference at [Saint Anselm College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Anselm_College) in [Manchester, New Hampshire,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester,_New_Hampshire) in May 1983 to represent their [New England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_England) community. It was designed by Robert L. Couturier, [attorney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawyer) and one-time [mayor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor) of [Lewiston, Maine,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewiston,_Maine) to have a blue field with a white fleur-de-lis over a white fivepointed star[.\[87\]](#page-46-9)[\[88\]](#page-46-10) This flag extends a tradition of designing flags for the [French communities of each Canadian province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Canadian_flags#Francophone_peoples) to the United States.

Blue and white are colors found on the flags of both the [United](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States) [States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States) and francophone nations such as [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France) or [Quebec.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quebec) The star symbolizes the United States and the fleur-de-lis symbolizes [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_people) culture. It can also be seen as representative of [French Canadians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Canadians) who form a sizable population in the American Northeast.

- [Biloxi, Mississippi,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biloxi,_Mississippi) was founded in 1699 by [Pierre Le Moyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Le_Moyne_d%27Iberville) [d'Iberville.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Le_Moyne_d%27Iberville)
- [Boise, Idaho,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boise,_Idaho) founded in the 1820s by French fur traders, means "wooded".
- [Bourbonnais, Illinois,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourbonnais,_Illinois) was named after French Canadian fur

- trader Francois Bourbonnais. The first permanent resident was French Canadian fur trader Noel Le Vasseur in the 1830s.
- [Chicago,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago) [Illinois,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illinois) is derived from a French rendering of the Native American word shikaakwa, translated as "wild onion" or "wild garlic", from the [Miami-Illinois language.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami-Illinois_language) [\[89\]](#page-47-0)[\[90\]](#page-47-1)[\[91\]](#page-47-2)[\[92\]](#page-47-3) The first known reference to the site of the current city of Chicago as "Checagou" was by [Robert de La Salle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ren%C3%A9-Robert_Cavelier,_Sieur_de_La_Salle) around 1679 in a memoir written about the time[.\[93\]](#page-47-4) [Henri Joutel,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Joutel) in his journal of 1688, noted that the wild garlic, called "chicagoua," grew abundantly in the area[.\[90\]](#page-47-1)
- • [Coeur d'Alene, Idaho,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coeur_d%27Alene,_Idaho) French Canadian fur traders allegedly named the local Indian tribe the [Coeur d'Alene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coeur_d%27Alene_Tribe) out of respect for their tough trading practices. Cœur d'alêne literally means "heart of an awl".
- [Davenport, Iowa,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davenport,_Iowa) was founded by [Antoine Le Claire,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine_Le Claire) an interpreter for the [United States Army,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army) in 1836.
- [Detroit,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detroit) [Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan) was founded by [Antoine de la Mothe Cadillac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine_de_la_Mothe_Cadillac) in 1701, a [French Royal Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Royal_Army) captain, and was originally called Fort Pontchartrain du Détroit, after the minister of marine under [Louis XIV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_XIV) and the French word Détroit for ["strait"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strait).
- [Dubuque, Iowa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dubuque,_Iowa) was established as a lead mining site by Canadian [Julien Dubuque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julien_Dubuque) in 1788.
- [Duluth, Minnesota,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duluth,_Minnesota) so-named for an Anglicization of [Daniel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Greysolon,_Sieur_du_Lhut) [Greysolon, Sieur du Lhut,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Greysolon,_Sieur_du_Lhut) modern city established by English, first founded as early French fur-trading post.
- [Dupont, Colorado,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dupont,_Colorado) [Du Pont, Georgia,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Du_Pont,_Georgia) [Dupont, Indiana,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dupont,_Indiana) [Dupont,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dupont,_Ohio) [Ohio,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dupont,_Ohio) [Dupont, Pennsylvania,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dupont,_Pennsylvania) [Dupont, Tennessee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dupont,_Tennessee) and [Du Pont,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Du Pont,_Washington)

[Washington,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Du Pont,_Washington) were all founded by the [Du Pont family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Du_Pont_family) or other French settlers.

- [French Camp, California,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Camp,_California) was the terminus of the Oregon-California Trail used by French-Canadian fur traders (including [Michel Laframboise\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Laframboise) in the 1830s and 1840s, making it one of the oldest settlements in [San Joaquin County.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Joaquin_County)
- [Galveston, Texas,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galveston,_Texas) first European settlement was established in 1816 by French pirate, [Louis-Michel Aury,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis-Michel_Aury) succeeded by [Jean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Lafitte) [Lafitte,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Lafitte) until the island's raiders were evicted by the US Navy in 1821.
- [Grand Forks, North Dakota,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Forks,_North_Dakota) originally "Les Grandes Fourches", when it was settled in the 1740s by fur traders.
- [Green Bay, Wisconsin,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Bay,_Wisconsin) or La Baye, was founded by [Jean Nicolet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Nicolet) in 1634. Many residents of Green Bay are direct descendants of the [French Canadian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Canadian) [inhabitants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inhabitants) and their families.
- [Juneau, Alaska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juneau,_Alaska) was founded in 1891 and named in honor of [Joseph Juneau,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Juneau) a gold prospector from the region of [Montreal,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montreal) who settled the first mining camp in the area.
- [Kaskaskia, Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaskaskia,_Illinois) was founded in 1703 by French Jesuit missionaries and was Illinois's first capital.
- [Milwaukee,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milwaukee) [Wisconsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisconsin) was founded settled by French traders most notably [Jacques Vieau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Vieau) and established as a city by [Solomon Juneau.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomon_Juneau)
- [Mobile, Alabama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile,_Alabama) was founded in 1702 by [Pierre Le Moyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Le_Moyne_d%27Iberville) [d'Iberville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Le_Moyne_d%27Iberville) and his brother [Jean-Baptiste Le Moyne.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_Le_Moyne) It was the first capital of Louisiana.
- [Natchitoches, Louisiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natchitoches,_Louisiana) was founded in 1714 by [Louis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Juchereau_de_St._Denis)

[Juchereau de St. Denis.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Juchereau_de_St._Denis)

- [New Orleans,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Orleans) [Louisiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana) was founded in 1718 by [Jean-Baptiste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_Le_Moyne) [Le Moyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_Le_Moyne) and named after [Philippe II, Duke of Orléans.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippe_II,_Duke_of_Orl%C3%A9ans)
- [New Paltz, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Paltz,_New_York) was founded in 1678 by French [Huguenots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huguenots) settlers, including [Louis Du Bois.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Du Bois_(Huguenot))
- [New Rochelle, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Rochelle,_New_York) was founded by French [Huguenots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huguenot) and named after [La Rochelle,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Rochelle) France.
- [Peoria, Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peoria,_Illinois) was first settled with the establishment of [Fort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Crevecoeur) [Crevecoeur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Crevecoeur) in 1680, ceded to British after 1763; area of downtown was once site of ["La Ville de Maillet"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Peoria,_Illinois)
- [Pierre, South Dakota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre,_South_Dakota) was named after [Pierre Chouteau Jr.,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Chouteau_Jr.) a fur trader of French Canadian origin, who built [Fort Pierre,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Pierre,_South_Dakota) where the capital of Pierre stands today.
- [Pittsburgh,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh) [Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvania) was originally surveyed in 1669 by [Robert de La Salle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_de_La_Salle) and was a post of French and Dutch fur traders, prior to the construction of [Fort Duquesne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Duquesne) and modern founding by the English.
- [Poteau, Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poteau,_Oklahoma) was founded by French traders
- [Portage Des Sioux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portage_Des_Sioux,_Missouri) was founded in 1799 by Zenon Trudeau and François Saucier.
- [Prairie du Chien, Wisconsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prairie_du_Chien,_Wisconsin) was established in 1685 by [Nicholas Perrot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Perrot) as a fur trading post.
- [Prairie du Rocher, Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prairie_du_Rocher,_Illinois) was founded in 1722 by Sister Thérèse Langlois, four years after [Fort de Chartres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_de_Chartres) was built by [Pierre Dugué de Boisbriand.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Dugu%C3%A9_de_Boisbriand)

French Canadian, in 1769.

- [Saint Louis, Missouri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Louis) was founded by a French trader, [Pierre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Lacl%C3%A8de) [Laclède,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Lacl%C3%A8de) and his stepson, a trader from Louisiana, [René](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ren%C3%A9_Auguste_Chouteau) [Auguste Chouteau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ren%C3%A9_Auguste_Chouteau) in 1764.
- [Sainte Genevieve, Missouri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ste._Genevieve,_Missouri) was founded in 1735 by habitants.
- [Saint Ignace, Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Ignace,_Michigan) was founded by father [Jacques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Marquette) [Marquette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Marquette) in 1671.
- [St. Joseph, Missouri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Joseph,_Missouri) was founded by [Joseph Robidoux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Robidoux_IV) c. 1826.

In 1841, it was named Saint-Paul by Father [Lucien Galtier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucien_Galtier) in honor of [Paul the Apostle.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_the_Apostle)
- [Sault Sainte Marie, Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sault_Ste._Marie,_Michigan) was founded in 1668 by fathers [Jacques Marquette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Marquette) and [Claude Dablon.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Dablon)
- [Vincennes, Indiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincennes,_Indiana) was established in 1732 by [François-Marie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois-Marie_Bissot,_Sieur_de_Vincennes) [Bissot, Sieur de Vincennes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois-Marie_Bissot,_Sieur_de_Vincennes) and rallied to the cause of the American revolution with Father [Pierre Gibault.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Gibault)
- [Arkansas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arkansas) named by French explorers from the corrupted Indian word meaning "south wind". [Arkansas Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arkansas_Post) was its first French establishment in 1686 by [Henri de Tonti.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Tonti)
- [Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illinois) French for the land of the [Illini,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illinois_Confederation) a Native American tribe. Also named from the [Pays des Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pays_des_Illinois) which had a substantial population at the time of [New France.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_France) French explorers [Jacques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Marquette) [Marquette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Marquette) and [Louis Jolliet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Jolliet) explored the [Illinois River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illinois_River) in 1673. In 1680, other French explorers constructed a fort at the site of present-day [Peoria,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peoria,_Illinois) and in 1682, a fort atop [Starved Rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starved_Rock) in today's [Starved Rock State Park.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starved_Rock_State_Park) French Canadians came south to settle particularly along the Mississippi River, and Illinois was part of the French empire of [La Louisiane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Louisiane) until 1763, when it passed to the British with their defeat of France in the [Seven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Years%27_War) [Years' War.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Years%27_War)

- • [Indiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiana) – In 1679 the French explorer [René-Robert Cavelier,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ren%C3%A9-Robert_Cavelier,_Sieur_de_La_Salle) [Sieur de La Salle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ren%C3%A9-Robert_Cavelier,_Sieur_de_La_Salle) was the first European to cross into Indiana after reaching present-day [South Bend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Bend,_Indiana) at the [Saint Joseph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Joseph_River_(Lake_Michigan)) [River.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Joseph_River_(Lake_Michigan)) [\[94\]](#page-47-5) French-Canadian [fur traders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fur_trade) soon arrived, bringing blankets, jewelry, tools, whiskey and weapons to trade for skins with the Native Americans. By 1702, [Sieur Juchereau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Juchereau_de_St._Denis) established the first trading post near [Vincennes.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincennes,_Indiana) In 1715, [Sieur de Vincennes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Baptiste_Bissot,_Sieur_de_Vincennes) built [Fort Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Miami_(Indiana)) at [Kekionga,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kekionga) now [Fort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Wayne) [Wayne.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Wayne) In 1717, another Canadian, [Picote de Beletre,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picote_de_Beletre) built [Fort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Ouiatenon) [Ouiatenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Ouiatenon) on the [Wabash River.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wabash_River) In 1732, Sieur de Vincennes built a second fur trading post at Vincennes. French Canadian settlers, who had left the earlier post because of hostilities, returned in larger numbers.
- [Louisiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana) from the French Louisiane, in honor of King [Louis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_XIV_of_France) [XIV of France.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_XIV_of_France) Named by [Cavelier de La Salle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavelier_de_La_Salle) who founded Louisiana and died in [Texas.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas) Many [Acadians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acadians) migrated to Louisiana and are today known as [Cajuns.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cajuns)
- [Maine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maine) Two [Jesuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesuit) missions were established by the French: one on [Penobscot Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penobscot_Bay) in 1609, and the other on [Mount Desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Desert_Island) [Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Desert_Island) in 1613. The same year, [Castine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castine,_Maine) was established by [Claude de La Tour.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_de_Saint-%C3%89tienne_de_la_Tour) In 1625, [Charles de Saint-Étienne de la Tour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_de_Saint-%C3%89tienne_de_la_Tour) erected [Fort Pentagouet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Pentagouet) to protect Castine.
- [Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan) French transcription of [Ojibwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ojibwe_language) word Mishii'igan (syncopated as Mishiigan) which means "great lake". The French forts of [Fort Saint-Joseph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_St._Joseph_(Niles,_Michigan)) and [Fort Michilimackinac,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Michilimackinac) as well as the French establishments of [Detroit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detroit) and [Saint Ignace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Ignace,_Michigan) were located in the area of Michigan which was part of New France.

- [Minnesota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minnesota) The first Europeans in the area were French [fur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fur_trade) [traders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fur_trade) who arrived in the 17th century. Explorers such as [Daniel Greysolon, Sieur du Lhut,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Greysolon,_Sieur_du_Lhut) Father [Louis Hennepin,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Hennepin) and [Joseph Nicollet,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Nicollet) among others, mapped out the state.
- [Missouri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missouri) The first European settlers were mostly ethnic [French Canadians,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Canadian) who created their first settlement in Missouri at present-day [Ste. Genevieve,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ste._Genevieve,_Missouri) about an hour south of St. Louis. They had migrated about 1750 from the [Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illinois) Country. St. Louis was founded soon after by French from New Orleans in 1764.
- [Vermont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vermont) comes from a contraction of French words, Vert, green, and mont, mount, mountain. It was named by the French explorer [Samuel de Champlain.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_de_Champlain) French seigneuries were subdivided along Lake Champlain at the time of New France, which was later given to the British colonies by the [Treaty of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Paris_(1763)) in 1763.
- • [Wisconsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisconsin) – named after the [Meskousing River.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisconsin_River) This spelling was later corrupted from the local Native American language to "Ouisconsin" by French explorers, and over time this version became the French name both for the Wisconsin River and for the surrounding lands. [La Baye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Baye) was Wisconsin's main community at the time of New France. English speakers anglicized the spelling to its modern form when they began to arrive in greater numbers during the early 19th century.[\[95\]](#page-47-6) Richard (2002) examines the major trends in the historiography regarding the Franco-Americans who came to New England in 1860–1930. He identifies three categories of scholars: survivalists, who emphasized the common destiny of Franco-Americans and celebrated their survival; regionalists and social historians, who aimed to uncover the diversity of the Franco-American past in distinctive communities across New England; and pragmatists, who argued that the forces of acculturation were too strong for the Franco-American community to overcome. The 'pragmatists versus survivalists' debate over the fate of the Franco-American community may be the ultimate weakness of Franco-American historiography. Such teleological stances have impeded the progress of research by funneling scholarly energies in limited directions while many other avenues, for example, Franco-American politics, arts, and ties to Quebec, remain insufficiently explored[.\[96\]](#page-47-7)

While a considerable number of pioneers of Franco-American history left the field or came to the end of their careers in the late 1990s, other scholars have moved the lines of debate in new directions in the last fifteen years. The "Franco" communities of New England have received less sustained scholarly attention in this period, but important work has no less appeared as historians have sought to assert the relevance of the French-Canadian diaspora to the larger narratives of American immigration, labor and religious history.

Scholars have worked to expand the transnational perspective developed by Robert G. Le Blanc during the 1980s and 1990s. [\[97\]](#page-48-0) Yukari Takai has studied the impact of recurrent cross-

border migration on family formation and gender roles among Franco-Americans[.\[98\]](#page-48-1) Florence Mae Waldron has expanded on older work by Tamara Hareven and Randolph Langenbach in her study of Franco-American women's work within prevalent American gender norms[.\[99\]](#page-48-2) Waldron's innovative work on the national aspirations and agency of women religious in New England also merits mention[.\[100\]](#page-48-3) Historians have pushed the lines of inquiry on Franco-Americans of New England in other directions as well. Recent studies have introduced a comparative perspective, considered the surprisingly understudied 1920s and 1930s, and reconsidered old debates on assimilation and religious conflict in light of new sources. [\[101\]](#page-48-4)[\[102\]](#page-48-5)[\[103\]](#page-48-6)

At the same time, there has been rapidly expanding research on the French presence in the middle and western part of the continent (the American Midwest, the Pacific coast, and the Great Lakes region) in the century following the collapse of New France[.\[104\]](#page-49-0)[\[105\]](#page-49-1)[\[106\]](#page-49-2)[\[107\]](#page-49-3)
