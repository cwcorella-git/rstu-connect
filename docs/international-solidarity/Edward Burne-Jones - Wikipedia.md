**Sir Edward Coley Burne-Jones, 1st Baronet**, [ARA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Royal_Academicians) ([;\[1\]](#page-36-0) 28 August 1833 – 17 June 1898) was an English painter and designer associated with the [Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Raphaelite_Brotherhood)'s style and subject matter[.\[2\]](#page-36-1)

Burne-Jones worked with [William Morris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Morris) as a founding partner in [Morris, Marshall, Faulkner & Co](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morris_%26_Co.) in the design of decorative arts[.\[3\]](#page-36-2) His early paintings show the influence of [Dante Gabriel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dante_Gabriel_Rossetti) [Rossetti,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dante_Gabriel_Rossetti) but by 1870 he had developed his own style. In 1877, he exhibited eight oil paintings at the [Grosvenor Gallery,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grosvenor_Gallery) a new rival to the [Royal Academy of Arts.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Academy_of_Arts) These included [The](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Beguiling_of_Merlin) [Beguiling of Merlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Beguiling_of_Merlin). The timing was right and he was taken up as a herald and star of the new [Aesthetic Movement.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetic_Movement)

In the studio of Morris and Co. Burne-Jones worked as a designer of a wide range of crafts including ceramic tiles, jewellery, [tapestries,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tapestry) and [mosaics.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosaic) Among his most significant and lasting designs are those for [stained glass windows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stained_glass_windows) the production of which was a revived craft during the 19th century. His designs are still to be found in churches across the UK, with examples in the US and Australia.

## ![](_page_2_Picture_4.jpeg)

Burne-Jones with [William Morris,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Morris) 1874, by [Frederick Hollyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Hollyer)

Born Edward Coley Burne Jones (the hyphenation of his last names was introduced later) was born in [Birmingham,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham) the son of a Welshman, Edward Richard Jones, a frame-maker at

[Bennetts Hill,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bennetts_Hill) where a [blue plaque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_plaque) commemorates the painter's childhood. A pub on the site of the house is called the Briar Rose in honour of Burne-Jones' work[.\[3\]](#page-36-2) His mother Elizabeth Jones (née Coley) died within six days of his birth, and Edward was raised by his father, and the family housekeeper, Ann Sampson, an obsessively affectionate but humourless, and unintellectual local girl[.\[4\]](#page-36-3)[\[5\]](#page-36-4) He attended Birmingham's [King Edward VI grammar school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Edward%27s_School,_Birmingham) in 1844 and the [Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_School_of_Art) [School of Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_School_of_Art) from 1848 to 1852, before studying [theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theology) at [Exeter College, Oxford.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exeter_College,_Oxford) [\[6\]](#page-36-5) At Oxford, he became a friend of [William Morris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Morris) as a consequence of a mutual interest in poetry. The two Exeter undergraduates, together with a group of Jones' friends from Birmingham known as the [Birmingham Set,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_Set) [\[7\]](#page-36-6) formed a society, which they called "The Brotherhood". The members of the brotherhood read the works of [John Ruskin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Ruskin) and [Tennyson,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred,_Lord_Tennyson) visited churches, and idealised aspects of the aesthetics and social structure of the [Middle Ages.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages) [\[3\]](#page-36-2) At this time, Burne-Jones discovered [Thomas Malory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Malory)'s [Le Morte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Morte_d%27Arthur) [d'Arthur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Morte_d%27Arthur) which would become a substantial influential in his life. At that time, neither Burne-Jones nor Morris knew [Dante](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dante_Gabriel_Rossetti) [Gabriel Rossetti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dante_Gabriel_Rossetti) personally, but both were much influenced by his works, and later met him by recruiting him as a contributor to their Oxford and Cambridge Magazine, founded by Morris in 1856 to promote the Brotherhood's ideas[.\[8\]](#page-36-7)[\[9\]](#page-36-8)

Burne-Jones had intended to become a church minister, but under Rossetti's influence both he and Morris decided to become artists, and Burne-Jones left college before taking a degree to pursue a career in art. In February 1857, Rossetti

## wrote to [William Bell Scott:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Bell_Scott)

Two young men, projectors of the Oxford and Cambridge Magazine, have recently come up to town from Oxford, and are now very intimate friends of mine. Their names are Morris and Jones. They have turned artists instead of taking up any other career to which the university generally leads, and both are men of real genius. Jones's designs are marvels of finish and imaginative detail, unequalled by anything unless perhaps [Albert Dürer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_D%C3%BCrer)'s finest works[.\[8\]](#page-36-7)

## Margaret, daughter of Burne-Jones

In 1856 Burne-Jones became engaged to [Georgiana "Georgie"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgiana_Burne-Jones) [Mac Donald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgiana_Burne-Jones) (1840–1920), one of the [Mac Donald sisters.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mac Donald_sisters) She was training to be a painter, and was the sister of Burne-Jones's old school friend. The couple married on 9 June 1860, after which she made her own work in [woodcuts,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodcut) and became a close friend of [George Eliot.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Eliot) (Another Mac Donald sister married the artist Sir [Edward Poynter,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Poynter) a further sister married the ironmaster [Alfred Baldwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Baldwin_(politician)) and was the mother of the Prime Minister [Stanley Baldwin,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Baldwin) and yet another sister was the mother of [Rudyard Kipling.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudyard_Kipling) Kipling and Baldwin were thus Burne-Jones's nephews by marriage).

Georgiana gave birth to a son, [Philip,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Burne-Jones) in 1861. In the winter of 1864, she became gravely ill with [scarlet fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scarlet_fever) and gave birth to a second son, Christopher, who died soon thereafter. The family then moved to 41 Kensington Square, and their daughter Margaret was born there in 1866[.\[10\]](#page-36-9)

In 1867 Burne-Jones and his family settled at the Grange, an 18th-century house set in a garden in [North End, Fulham,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_End,_Fulham) London. For the 1870s Burne-Jones did not exhibit, following a number of bitterly hostile attacks in the press, and a passionate affair (described as the "emotional climax of his life"[\)\[11\]](#page-36-10) with his Greek model [Maria Zambaco,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Zambaco) which ended with her trying to commit [suicide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suicide) by throwing herself into

#### [Regent's Canal.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regent%27s_Canal) [\[11\]](#page-36-10)[\[12\]](#page-37-0)

During these difficult years, Georgiana developed a friendship with Morris, whose wife [Jane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane_Morris) had fallen in love with Rossetti. Morris and Georgie may have been in love, but if he asked her to leave her husband, she refused. In the end, the Burne-Joneses remained together, as did the Morrises, but Morris and Georgiana were close for the rest of their lives[.\[13\]](#page-37-1)

In 1880, the Burne-Joneses bought [Prospect House](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Prospect_House,_Rottingdean&action=edit&redlink=1) in [Rottingdean,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rottingdean) near [Brighton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brighton) in Sussex, as their holiday home and soon after, the next door [Aubrey Cottage](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Aubrey_Cottage&action=edit&redlink=1) to create [North](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=North_End_House&action=edit&redlink=1) [End House,](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=North_End_House&action=edit&redlink=1) reflecting the fact that their Fulham home was in North End Road. (Years later, in 1923, [Sir Roderick Jones,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roderick_Jones_(1877%E2%80%931962)) head of [Reuters,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reuters) and his wife, playwright and novelist [Enid Bagnold,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enid_Bagnold) were to add the adjacent [Gothic House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_House) to the property, which became the inspiration and setting for her play [The Chalk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Chalk_Garden) [Garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Chalk_Garden)).

His troubled son Philip, who became a successful portrait painter, died in 1926. His adored daughter Margaret (died 1953) married [John William Mackail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_William_Mackail) (1850–1945), the friend and biographer of Morris, and Professor of Poetry at Oxford from 1911 to 1916. Their children were the novelists [Angela](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angela_Thirkell) [Thirkell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angela_Thirkell) and [Denis Mackail,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denis_Mackail) and the youngest, Clare Mackail.

In an edition of the boys' magazine, Chums (No. 227, Vol. V, 13 January 1897), an article on Burne-Jones stated that "....his pet grandson used to be punished by being sent to stand in a corner with his face to the wall. One day on being sent there, he was delighted to find the wall prettily decorated with fairies, flowers, birds, and bunnies. His indulgent grandfather had utilised his talent to alleviate the tedium of his favourite's period of penance."

## **Early years: Rossetti and Morris**

## [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Edward_Burne-Jones&action=edit§ion=4)

## ![](_page_7_Picture_5.jpeg)

[Sidonia von Borcke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidonia_von_Borcke), 1860

Burne-Jones once admitted that after leaving Oxford he "found himself at five-and-twenty what he ought to have been at fifteen". He had had no regular training as a draughtsman and lacked the confidence of science. But his extraordinary faculty of invention as a designer was already ripening; his mind, rich in knowledge of classical story and medieval romance, teemed with pictorial subjects, and he set himself to complete his set of skills by resolute labour, witnessed by his drawings. The works of this first period are all more or less tinged by the influence of

Rossetti; but they are already differentiated from the elder master's style by their more facile though less intensely felt elaboration of imaginative detail. Many are pen-and-ink drawings on [vellum,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vellum) exquisitely finished, of which his Waxen Image (1856) is one of the earliest and best examples. Although the subject, medium and manner derive from Rossetti's inspiration, it is not the hand of a pupil merely, but of a potential master. This was recognised by Rossetti himself, who before long avowed that he had nothing more to teach him[.\[14\]](#page-37-2)

Burne-Jones's first sketch in oils dates from this same year, 1856, and during 1857 he made for [Bradfield College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bradfield_College) the first of what was to be an immense series of cartoons for stained glass. In 1858 he decorated a cabinet with the Prioress's Tale from [Geoffrey Chaucer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Chaucer)'s [Canterbury Tales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canterbury_Tales), his first direct illustration of the work of a poet whom he especially loved and who inspired him with endless subjects. Thus early, therefore, we see the artist busy in all the various fields in which he was to labour[.\[14\]](#page-37-2)

In the autumn of 1857 Burne-Jones joined Morris, [Valentine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valentine_Prinsep) [Prinsep,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valentine_Prinsep) J. [R. Spencer Stanhope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Roddam_Spencer_Stanhope)[\[15\]](#page-37-3) and others in Rossetti's illfated scheme to [decorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_Union_murals) the walls of the [Oxford Union.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_Union) None of the painters had mastered the technique of [fresco,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresco) and their pictures had begun to peel from the walls before they were completed. In 1859 Burne-Jones made his first journey to Italy. He saw [Florence,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence) [Pisa,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisa) [Siena,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siena) [Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venice) and other places, and appears to have found the gentle and romantic [Sienese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sienese_School) more attractive than any other school. Rossetti's influence persisted and is visible, more strongly perhaps than ever before, in the two [watercolours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watercolor_painting) of 1860, Sidonia von Bork and Clara von Bork.[\[14\]](#page-37-2) Both paintings illustrate the 1849 [gothic novel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_novel) Sidonia the Sorceress by [Lady Wilde,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane_Wilde) a translation of Sidonia Von Bork: Die Klosterhexe (1847) by Johann Wilhelm Meinhold[.\[16\]](#page-37-4)

![](_page_9_Picture_3.jpeg)

[The Beguiling of Merlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Beguiling_of_Merlin), 1874

In 1864, Burne-Jones was elected an associate of the [Society of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Watercolour_Society) [Painters in Water-Colours—](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Watercolour_Society)which is known as the Old Water-Colour Society—and exhibited, among other works, [The](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Merciful_Knight) [Merciful Knight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Merciful_Knight), the first picture which fully revealed his ripened personality as an artist. The next six years saw a series of fine watercolours at the same gallery[.\[14\]](#page-37-2)

In 1866, Mrs. Cassavetti commissioned Burne-Jones to paint her daughter, [Maria Zambaco,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Zambaco) in Cupid finding Psyche, an introduction which led to their tragic affair. In 1870, Burne-Jones resigned his membership following a controversy over his painting Phyllis and Demophoön. The features of Maria Zambaco were clearly recognisable in the barely draped Phyllis, and the undraped nakedness of Demophoön coupled with the suggestion of female sexual assertiveness offended [Victorian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_era) sensibilities. Burne-Jones was asked to make a slight alteration, but instead "withdrew not only the picture from the walls, but himself from the Society"[.\[17\]](#page-37-5)[\[18\]](#page-37-6) During the next seven years, 1870–1877, only two works of the painter's were exhibited. These were two water-colours, shown at the Dudley Gallery at the [Egyptian Hall, Piccadilly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_Hall) in 1873, one of them being the beautiful [Love Among the Ruins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Love_Among_the_Ruins_(Burne-Jones)), destroyed twenty years later by a cleaner who supposed it to be an oil painting, but afterwards reproduced in oils by the painter. This silent period was one of unremitting production.[[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)]

Hitherto, Burne-Jones had worked almost entirely in watercolours. He now began pictures in oils, working at them in turn, and having them on hand. The first Briar Rose series, Laus Veneris, the Golden Stairs, the Pygmalion series, and The Mirror of Venus are among the works planned and completed, or carried far towards completion, during these years.[\[14\]](#page-37-2)

The beginnings of Burne-Jones' partnership with the fine-art photographer [Frederick Hollyer,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Hollyer) whose reproductions of paintings and—especially—drawings would expose an audience to Burne-Jones's works in the coming decades, began during this period[.\[19\]](#page-37-7)

At last, in May 1877, the day of recognition came with the opening of the first exhibition of the [Grosvenor Gallery,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grosvenor_Gallery) when the Days of Creation, [The Beguiling of Merlin,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Beguiling_of_Merlin) and the Mirror of

Venus were all shown. Burne-Jones followed up the signal success of these pictures with Laus Veneris, the Chant d'Amour, Pan and Psyche, and other works, exhibited in 1878. Most of these pictures are painted in brilliant colours.[[citation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed) [needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)]

A change is noticeable in 1879 in the Annunciation and in the four pictures making up the second series of Pygmalion and the Image; the former of these, one of the simplest and most perfect of the artist's works, is subdued and sober; in the latter, a scheme of soft and delicate tints was attempted, not with entire success. A similar temperance of colours marks [The](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Golden_Stairs) [Golden Stairs,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Golden_Stairs) first exhibited in 1880.[[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)]

The almost sombre [Wheel of Fortune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wheel_of_Fortune_(Burne-Jones)) was shown in 1883, followed in 1884 by [King Cophetua and the Beggar Maid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Cophetua_and_the_Beggar_Maid_(painting)), in which Burne-Jones once more indulged his love of gorgeous colour, refined by the period of self-restraint. He next turned to two important sets of pictures, The Briar Rose and The Story of Perseus, although these were not completed[.\[14\]](#page-37-2)

In 1861, William Morris founded the [decorative arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decorative_arts) firm of Morris, Marshall, Faulkner & Co. with Rossetti, Burne-Jones, [Ford Madox Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford_Madox_Brown) and Philip Webb as partners, together with [Charles Faulkner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Joseph_Faulkner) and [Peter Paul Marshall,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Paul_Marshall) the former of whom was a member of the Oxford Brotherhood, and the latter a friend of Brown and Rossetti[.\[9\]](#page-36-8) The prospectus set forth that the firm would undertake carving, [stained glass,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stained_glass) metal-work, paper-hangings, [chintzes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chintz) (printed fabrics), and [carpets.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpet) [\[14\]](#page-37-2) The decoration of churches was from the first an important part of the business. The work shown by the firm at the [1862](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1862_International_Exhibition) [International Exhibition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1862_International_Exhibition) attracted notice, and later it was flourishing. Two significant secular commissions helped establish the firm's reputation in the late 1860s: a royal project at [St. James's Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._James%27s_Palace) and the "green dining room" at the South Kensington Museum (now the [Victoria and Albert\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_and_Albert_Museum) of 1867 which featured stained glass windows and panel figures by Burne-Jones[.\[21\]](#page-37-9)

In 1871 Morris & Co. were responsible for the windows at [All](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_Saints_Church,_Wilden) [Saints,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_Saints_Church,_Wilden) designed by Burne-Jones for [Alfred Baldwin,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Baldwin_(politician)) his wife's brother-in-law. The firm was reorganised as Morris & Co. in 1875, and Burne-Jones continued to contribute designs for stained glass and later tapestries until the end of his career. Nine windows designed by him and made by Morris & Co were installed in Holy Trinity Church in Frome[.\[22\]](#page-37-10) Stained glass windows in the [Christ Stanmore Hall was the last major decorating commission executed by Morris & Co. before Morris's death in 1896. It was the most extensive commission undertaken by the firm, and included a series of tapestries based on the story of the [Holy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Grail) [Grail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Grail) for the dining room, with figures by Burne-Jones.[\[25\]](#page-37-13) In 1891 Jones was elected a member of the [Art Workers Guild.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_Workers_Guild)

### **Design for the theatre**

## [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Edward_Burne-Jones&action=edit§ion=8)

In 1894, theatrical manager and actor [Henry Irving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Irving) commissioned Burne-Jones to design sets and costumes for the [Lyceum Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyceum_Theatre,_London) production of King Arthur by [J. Comyns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Comyns_Carr) [Carr,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Comyns_Carr) who was Burne-Jones's patron and the director of the New Gallery as well as a playwright. The play starred Irving as [King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Arthur) [Arthur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Arthur) and [Ellen Terry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellen_Terry) as [Guinevere,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinevere) and toured America following its London run[.\[27\]](#page-37-15)[\[28\]](#page-38-0)[\[29\]](#page-38-1) Burne-Jones accepted the commission with enthusiasm, but was disappointed with much of the final result. He wrote confidentially to his friend Helen Mary Gaskell (known as May), "The armour is good—they have taken pains with it ... Perceval looked the one romantic thing in it ... I hate the stage, don't tell—but I do."[\[30\]](#page-38-2)

![](_page_14_Picture_11.jpeg)

## ![](_page_15_Picture_2.jpeg)

[The Golden Stairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Golden_Stairs), 1880

Burne-Jones's paintings were one strand in the evolving tapestry of [Aestheticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aestheticism) from the 1860s through the 1880s, which considered that art should be valued as an object of beauty engendering a sensual response, rather than for the story or moral implicit in the subject matter. In many ways, this was antithetical to the ideals of Ruskin and the early Pre-Raphaelites[.\[31\]](#page-38-3) Burne-Jones's aim in art is best given in his own words, written to a friend:

I mean by a picture a beautiful, romantic dream of something that never was, never will be – in a light better than any light that ever shone – in a land no one can define or remember, only desire – and the forms divinely beautiful – and then I wake up, with the waking of Brynhild[.\[14\]](#page-37-2)

Burne-Jones was elected an Associate of the [Royal Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Academy) in 1885, and the following year he exhibited uniquely at the Academy, showing The Depths of the Sea, a painting of a mermaid carrying down with her a youth whom she has unconsciously drowned in the impetuosity of her love. This picture adds to the habitual haunting charm a tragic irony of conception and a felicity of execution which give it a place apart among Burne-Jones's works. He formally resigned his Associateship in 1893.

One of the Perseus series was exhibited in 1887 and two more in 1888, with The Brazen Tower, inspired by the same legend. In 1890 the second series of [The Legend of Briar Rose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Legend_of_Briar_Rose) were exhibited by themselves and won admiration. The huge watercolour, [The Star of Bethlehem,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_of_Bethlehem_(painting)) painted for the corporation of Birmingham, was exhibited in 1891.

A long illness for a time checked the painter's activity, which, when resumed, was much occupied with decorative schemes. An exhibition of his work was held at the [New Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Gallery_(London)) in the winter of 1892–1893. To this period belong his comparatively few portraits.

In 1894, Burne-Jones was made a [baronet.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baronet) Ill health again interrupted the progress of his works, chief among which was the vast [Arthur in Avalon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Last_Sleep_of_Arthur_in_Avalon). William Morris died in 1896, and the health of Burne-Jones declined substantially after. In 1898 he suffered an attack of [influenza,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Influenza) and had apparently recovered when he was again taken suddenly ill and died on 17 June 1898[.\[14\]](#page-37-2)[\[32\]](#page-38-4) His memorial service was held six days later, at [Westminster Abbey.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_Abbey) His ashes were interred in the churchyard at [St Margaret's Church, Rottingdean,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Margaret%27s_Church,_Rottingdean) [\[33\]](#page-38-5) a place he knew through summer family holidays. In the winter following his death, a second exhibition of his works was held at the New

Gallery, and an exhibition of his drawings at the [Burlington Fine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burlington_Fine_Arts_Club) [Arts Club.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burlington_Fine_Arts_Club) [\[14\]](#page-37-2)

## ![](_page_17_Picture_3.jpeg)

Burne-Jones' [The Last Sleep of Arthur in Avalon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Last_Sleep_of_Arthur_in_Avalon) (between 1881 and 1898), at [Museo de Arte de Ponce,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museo_de_Arte_de_Ponce) [Ponce, Puerto Rico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ponce,_Puerto_Rico) In 1881 Burne-Jones received an honorary degree from [Oxford,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford) and was made an Honorary Fellow in 1882[.\[8\]](#page-36-7) In 1885 he became the President of the [Birmingham Society of Artists.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_Society_of_Artists) At about that time, he began hyphenating his name, merely—as he wrote later—to avoid "annihilation" in the mass of Joneses. [\[34\]](#page-38-6) In November 1893, he was approached to see if he would accept a Baronetcy on the recommendation of the outgoing Prime Minister [William Ewart Gladstone,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Ewart_Gladstone) the following February he legally changed his name to Burne-Jones[.\[35\]](#page-38-7) He was formally created a [baronet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baronet) of Rottingdean, in the county of Sussex, and of the Grange, in the parish of Fulham, in the county of London, in the [baronetage of the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baronetage_of_the_United_Kingdom) on 3 May 1894[,\[36\]](#page-38-8) but remained unhappy about accepting the honour, which disgusted his socialist friend Morris and was scorned by his equally socialist wife Georgiana.[\[34\]](#page-38-6)[\[35\]](#page-38-7) Only his son Philip, who mixed with the set of the [Prince of Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_VII_of_the_United_Kingdom) and would inherit the title, truly wanted it[.\[35\]](#page-38-7) Burne-Jones was made an elected member of the [Royal Academy of Science,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Academy_of_Science,_Letters_and_Fine_Arts_of_Belgium)

## [Letters and Fine Arts of Belgium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Academy_of_Science,_Letters_and_Fine_Arts_of_Belgium) in 1897[.\[37\]](#page-38-9)

Following Burne-Jones' death, and at the intervention of the Prince of Wales, his memorial service was held at [Westminster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_Abbey) [Abbey.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_Abbey) It was the first time an artist had been so honoured.

## ![](_page_18_Picture_4.jpeg)

[Blue plaque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_plaque) on [Bennetts Hill,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bennetts_Hill) Birmingham

Burne-Jones exerted a considerable influence on French painting. He was influential among French [symbolist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolist_painting) painters, from 1889[.\[38\]](#page-38-10) His work inspired poetry by [Algernon Charles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algernon_Charles_Swinburne) [Swinburne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algernon_Charles_Swinburne) – Swinburne's 1866 Poems & Ballads is dedicated to Burne-Jones.

Three of Burne-Jones's studio assistants, [John Melhuish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Melhuish_Strudwick) [Strudwick,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Melhuish_Strudwick) T. M. Rooke and [Charles Fairfax Murray,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Fairfax_Murray) went on to successful painting careers. Murray later became an important collector and respected [art dealer.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_dealer) Between 1903 and 1907 he sold a great many works by Burne-Jones and the [Pre-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Raphaelites)[Raphaelites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Raphaelites) to the [Birmingham Museum and Art Gallery,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_Museum_%26_Art_Gallery) at far below their market worth. Birmingham Museum and Art Gallery now has the largest collection of works by Burne-Jones in the world, including the massive watercolour Star of Bethlehem, commissioned for the Gallery in 1897. The paintings are believed by some to have influenced the young [J.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._R._R._Tolkien)

[R. R. Tolkien,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._R._R._Tolkien) then growing up in Birmingham[.\[39\]](#page-38-11)

Burne-Jones was also a very strong influence on the [Birmingham Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_Group_(artists)) of artists, from the 1890s onwards.

## **Neglect and rediscovery**

## [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Edward_Burne-Jones&action=edit§ion=13)

On 16 June 1933, Prime Minister [Stanley Baldwin,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Baldwin) a nephew of Burne-Jones, officially opened the centenary exhibition featuring Burne-Jones's drawings and paintings at the [Tate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tate_Britain) [Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tate_Britain) in London. In his opening speech at the exhibition, Baldwin expressed what the art of Burne-Jones stood for:

In my view, what he did for us common people was to open, as never had been opened before, magic casements of a land of faery in which he lived throughout his life ... It is in that inner world we can cherish in peace, beauty which he has left us and in which there is peace at least for ourselves. The few of us who knew him and loved him well, always keep him in our hearts, but his work will go on long after we have passed away. It may give its message in one generation to a few or in other to many more, but there it will be for ever for those who seek in their generation, for beauty and for those who can recognise and reverence a great man, and a great artist[.\[40\]](#page-38-12)

But, in fact, long before 1933, Burne-Jones had fallen out of fashion in the art world, much of which soon preferred the major trends in [Modern art,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_art) and the exhibit marking the 100th anniversary of his birth was a sad affair, poorly attended.[\[41\]](#page-38-13) It was not until the mid-1970s that his work began to be re-

assessed and once again acclaimed, following the publication of [Martin Harrison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Harrison_(curator)) and Bill Waters' 1973 monograph and reappraisal 'Burne-Jones'. In 1975, author [Penelope Fitzgerald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penelope_Fitzgerald) published a biography of Burne-Jones, her first book.[\[42\]](#page-38-14) A major exhibit in 1989 at the [Barbican Art Gallery,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbican_Art_Gallery) London (in book form as: John Christian, The Last Romantics, 1989), traced Burne-Jones's influence on the subsequent generation of artists, and another at [Tate Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tate_Britain) in 1997 explored the links between British Aestheticism and Symbolism[.\[38\]](#page-38-10)

A second, lavish centenary exhibit – this time marking the 100th anniversary of Burne-Jones's death – was held at the [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Museum_of_Art) in New York in 1998, before travelling to the [Birmingham Museum and Art Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_Museum_and_Art_Gallery) and the [Musée d'Orsay,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mus%C3%A9e_d%27Orsay) Paris[.\[43\]](#page-38-15)

[Fiona Mac Carthy,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiona_Mac Carthy) in a review of Burne-Jones's legacy, notes that he was "a painter who, while quintessentially Victorian, leads us forward to the psychological and sexual introspection of the early twentieth century"[.\[44\]](#page-39-0)
