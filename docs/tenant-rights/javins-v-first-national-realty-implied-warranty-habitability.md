**428 F.2d 1071 (D.C. Cir.), cert. denied 400 U.S. 925 (1970)**

*Argued January 16, 1970 — Decided May 7, 1970*

This landmark case established the implied warranty of habitability in American landlord-tenant law, fundamentally transforming the legal relationship between landlords and tenants. It is arguably the most influential residential housing case of the twentieth century.

## The Case

### Facts

Three tenants at Clifton Terrace apartment complex in Washington, D.C.—Ethel Javins, Rudolph Saunders, and Stanley Gross—withheld their April rent payments. They cited approximately **1,500 housing code violations** in the building as justification.

The violations included:
- Inoperable toilets
- Peeling paint
- Rat infestation
- Lack of heat and hot water

First National Realty Corporation, the landlord, sued for eviction based on nonpayment of rent. The trial court rejected the tenants' offer to prove that these housing code violations constituted a valid defense against eviction.

### Legal Issues

1. Whether housing code violations arising during a lease term affect a tenant's obligation to pay rent
2. Whether an implied warranty of habitability exists in residential leases

### Holding

The Court of Appeals reversed the lower court's decision, establishing that **an implied warranty of habitability—measured by District of Columbia Housing Regulations—applies to urban residential leases**.

The tenant's obligation to pay rent depends on the landlord maintaining the premises in habitable condition. Breach of this warranty gives rise to the usual remedies for breach of contract.

## Judge Wright's Reasoning

Judge J. Skelly Wright authored the opinion, emphasizing several key points:

### Modern Lease as Contract

The court held that modern apartment leases should be analyzed as **contracts rather than feudal property transfers**. The old common law rule imposing repair obligations on tenants was designed for agricultural land, not urban apartments.

> "Contract principles established in other areas of the law provide a more rational framework for the apportionment of landlord-tenant responsibilities."

### What Tenants Actually Seek

Contemporary tenants seek **"a house suitable for occupation"**—not land itself. The court noted:

> "The tenant today is not interested in an agrarian lease in which the primary focus is on possession of the land and any structures are merely incidental. Today's urban tenants seek a package of goods and services—a well-known package containing far more than just four walls and a floor."

### Consumer Protection Parallel

The opinion drew parallels to consumer protection doctrines in product liability law. Just as manufacturers warrant that products are fit for their intended use, landlords should warrant that rental housing is fit for habitation.

Landlords possess **superior knowledge and capacity** for maintenance. Tenants typically lack the ability to inspect or repair building systems and should not be expected to do so.

### Housing Codes as Standards

Housing codes implicitly incorporate habitability standards into lease agreements. When a landlord leases premises covered by housing regulations, compliance with those regulations becomes part of the bargain.

## Historical Significance

### Impact on American Law

This decision fundamentally transformed landlord-tenant law across the United States:

- Influenced the **Uniform Residential Landlord-Tenant Act**
- Incorporated into the **Restatement (Second) of Property**
- Adopted in some form by **nearly every state**

### Origins in Tenant Organizing

The case arose from a **rent strike** organized by tenants facing deplorable conditions. The over 1,500 code violations demonstrated systemic landlord neglect that tenants could not address individually.

The case illustrates how **collective tenant action** can produce legal precedents that protect all renters.

## Practical Applications

### What the Warranty Covers

The implied warranty of habitability typically requires landlords to maintain:

- Structural integrity
- Plumbing and hot water
- Heating (in cold climates)
- Electrical systems
- Freedom from pest infestation
- Compliance with building and housing codes
- Basic security measures

### Tenant Remedies

When landlords breach the warranty, tenants may have several remedies:

1. **Rent withholding** until repairs are made
2. **Rent abatement** (reduction) proportional to the diminution in value
3. **Repair and deduct** the cost from rent
4. **Lease termination** for substantial breaches
5. **Damages** for harm caused by uninhabitable conditions

## The Continuing Legacy

Javins established a principle that resonates through contemporary tenant organizing: **housing is not merely a commodity but a necessity, and those who provide it have obligations to those who depend on it**.

The case demonstrates that legal doctrine can evolve to meet social needs—and that tenant organizing can drive that evolution.
