# **Green Man**

The **Green Man**, also known as a **foliate head**, [\[1\]](#page-4-0) is a [motif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motif_(visual_arts)) in architecture and art, of a face made of, or completely surrounded by, [foliage,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foliage) which normally spreads out from the centre of the face.[\[2\]](#page-4-1) Apart from a purely decorative function, the Green Man is primarily interpreted as a [symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbol) of rebirth, representing the cycle of new growth that occurs every spring[.\[3\]](#page-4-2)

The Green Man [motif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motif_(visual_arts)) has many variations. Branches or [vines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vine) may sprout from the mouth, nostrils, or other parts of the face, and these shoots may bear flowers or fruit. Found in many cultures from many ages around the world, the Green Man is often related to natural [vegetation deities.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetation_deity) Often used as decorative [architectural ornaments,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ornament_(art)) where they are a form of [mascaron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mascaron_(architecture)) or ornamental head, Green Men are frequently found in [architectural sculpture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architectural_sculpture) on both secular and [ecclesiastical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesiology) buildings in the Western tradition. In churches in England, the image was used to illustrate a popular sermon describing the mystical origins of the [cross of Jesus.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/True_Cross)

"Green Man" type foliate heads first appeared in England during the early 12th century deriving from those of France, and were especially popular in the [Gothic architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_architecture) of the 13th to 15th centuries. ### **Types**

Usually referred to in art history as foliate heads or foliate masks, representations of the Green Man take many forms, but most just show a "mask" or frontal depiction of a face, which in architecture is usually in [relief.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relief) The simplest depict a man's face peering out of dense foliage. Some may have leaves for hair, perhaps with a leafy beard. Often leaves or leafy shoots are shown growing from his open mouth and sometimes even from the nose and eyes as well. In the most abstract examples, the carving at first glance appears to be merely stylised foliage, with the facial element only becoming apparent on closer examination. The face is almost always male; green women are rare. [Lady Raglan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitz Roy_Somerset,_4th_Baron_Raglan#Julia_Hamilton) coined the term "Green Man" for this type of architectural feature in her 1939 article *The Green Man in Church Architecture* in *[The Folklore Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Folk-Lore_Journal)*. [\[7\]](#page-4-6) It is thought that her interest stemmed from carvings at [St. Jerome's Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Jerome%27s_Church,_Llangwm) in [Llangwm,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llangwm,_Monmouthshire) [Monmouthshire.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monmouthshire)[\[8\]](#page-4-7)

The Green Man appears in many forms, with the three most common types categorized as:

- the Foliate Head: completely covered in green leaves
- the Disgorging Head: spews vegetation from its mouth
- the Bloodsucker Head: sprouts vegetation from all facial orifices (e.g. tear ducts, nostrils, mouth, and ears)[\[9\]](#page-4-8)[\[10\]](#page-4-9)

# **History**

In terms of [formalism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formalism_(art)) art historians see a connection with the masks in [Iron Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age) [Celtic art,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_art) where faces emerge from stylized vegetal ornament in the ["Plastic style"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_art#La_T%C3%A8ne_style) metalwork of [La Tène art.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_T%C3%A8ne_culture) [\[11\]](#page-4-10) Since there are so few survivals, and almost none in wood, the lack of a continuous series of examples is not a fatal objection to such a continuity.

The *Oxford Dictionary of English Folklore* suggests that they ultimately have their origins in late [Roman art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_art) from leaf masks used to represent gods and mythological figures.[\[1\]](#page-4-0) A character superficially similar to the Green Man, in the form of a partly foliate mask surrounded by [Bacchic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacchic) figures, appears at the centre of the 4th-century silver salver in the [Mildenhall Treasure,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mildenhall_Treasure) found at a [Roman villa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_villa) site in Suff[olk,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suffolk) England; the mask is generally agreed to represent [Neptune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neptune_(mythology)) or [Oceanus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanus) and the foliation is of [seaweed.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seaweed)[\[12\]](#page-4-11)

In his lectures at Gresham College, historian and professor [Ronald Hutton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Hutton) traces the green man to India, stating "the component parts of Lady Raglan's construct of the Green Man were dismantled.[\[13\]](#page-5-0) The medieval foliate heads were studied by Kathleen Basford in 1978 and Mercia Mac Dermott in 2003. They were revealed to have been a motif originally developed in India, which travelled through the medieval Arab empire to Christian Europe. There it became a decoration for monks' manuscripts, from which it spread to churches."

A late 4th-century example of a Green Man disgorging vegetation from his mouth is at St. Abre, in [St. Hilaire-le-grand,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Hilaire-le-Grand) France[.\[14\]](#page-5-1) 11th century Romanesque [Templar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knights_Templar) churches in [Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem) have Romanesque foliate heads. Sculpture of Green Man in ruins of [Hatra,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hatra) modern Iraq, 2nd century tentatively suggested that the symbol may have originated in [Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_Minor) and been brought to Europe by travelling stone carvers[.\[15\]](#page-5-2) The tradition of the Green Man carved into Christian churches is found across Europe, including examples such as the Seven Green Men of [Nicosia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicosia) carved into the facade of the thirteenth century [St Nicholas Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bedesten,_Nicosia) in Cyprus. The motif fitted very easily into the developing use of vegetal

[architectural sculpture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architectural_sculpture) in [Romanesque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanesque_architecture) and [Gothic architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_architecture) in Europe. Later foliate heads in churches may have reflected the legends around [Seth,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seth) the son of [Adam,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam) according to which he plants seeds in his dead father's mouth as he lies in his grave. The tree that grew from them became the tree of the [True Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/True_Cross) of the crucifixion. This tale was in *[The Golden Legend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Legend)* of [Jacobus de Voragine,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobus_de_Voragine) a very popular thirteenth century compilation of Christian religious stories, from which the subjects of church sermons were often taken, especially after 1483, when [William Caxton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Caxton) printed an English translation of the *Golden Legend*. [\[16\]](#page-5-3)

According to the Christian author Stephen Miller, author of "The Green Man in Medieval England: Christian Shoots from Pagan Roots" (2022),[\[17\]](#page-5-4) "It is a Christian/Judaic-derived motif relating to the legends and medieval hagiographies of the [Quest of Seth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legend_of_the_Rood) – the three twigs/seeds/kernels planted below the tongue of postfall Adam by his son Seth (provided by the angel of mercy responsible for guarding Eden) shoot forth, bringing new life to humankind".[\[18\]](#page-5-5) This notion was first proposed by James Coulter (2006).[\[19\]](#page-5-6)

From the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance) onward, elaborate variations on the Green Man theme, often with animal heads rather than human faces, appear in many media other than carvings (including [manuscripts,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuscript) [metalwork,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metalwork) [bookplates,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bookplate) and [stained glass\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stained_glass). They seem to have been used for purely decorative effect rather than reflecting any deeply held belief.

#### **Modern times**

In Britain, the image of the Green Man enjoyed a revival in the 19th century, becoming popular with architects during the Gothic Revival and the Arts and Crafts era, when it appeared as a decorative motif in and on many buildings, both religious and secular. American architects took up the motif around the same time. Many variations can be found in Neo-Gothic Victorian architecture. He was popular amongst Australian stonemasons and can be found on many secular and sacred buildings, including an example on Broadway, Sydney. In 1887 a Swiss engraver, Numa Guyot, created a bookplate depicting a Green Man in exquisite detail. [20]

In April 2023, a Green Man's head was depicted on the invitation for the Coronation of Charles III and Camilla, designed by heraldic artist and manuscript illuminator Andrew Jamieson. According to the official royal website: "Central to the design is the motif of the Green Man, an ancient figure from British folklore, symbolic of spring and rebirth, to celebrate the new reign. The shape of the Green Man, crowned in natural foliage, is formed of leaves of oak, ivy, and hawthorn, and the emblematic flowers of the United Kingdom."[21][22] which alluded to "the nature worshipper in King Charles" but polarized the public.[6] Indeed, as the medieval art historian Cassandra Harrington pointed out, although vegetal figures were abundant throughout the medieval and early modern period, the foliate head motif is not 'an ancient figure from British folklore', as the Royal Household has proclaimed, but a European import.'[\[4\]](#page-4-3)
