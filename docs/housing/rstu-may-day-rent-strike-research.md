The historical record is unambiguous: rent strikes work when tenants move together, and May 1st carries 140 years of worker defiance in its bones. This research compiles the tactical history, hard data, psychological triggers, and moral language you need to write a homepage that mobilizes—not informs.

The numbers are damning: **20 vacant homes exist for every homeless person in America**, Wall Street firms spent $9.6 billion buying foreclosed family homes after 2008, and in Washoe County, 1,760 people sleep without stable shelter while rents have climbed 50% in five years. History shows that when tenants refuse to pay together, they win. The question is whether enough people in Reno-Sparks will decide that May 1st, 2026 is the day they stop submitting.

## Rent strikes built the tenant protections Americans take for granted

The first major American rent strike exploded on December 26, 1907, when 10,000 Jewish immigrant families on New York's Lower East Side refused a 33% rent hike during a depression. The rallying cry drew on fresh memory: organizers told tenants to fight landlords "as they had the Czar." Sixteen-year-old Pauline Newman—who worked at the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory—organized 400 young women to canvass tenements after their shifts. Housewives spread word through neighborhood networks. Children burned effigies of landlords in the streets. Tenants hung red flags from windows—often dyed petticoats. Signs read simply: **"LOW RENT OR NO RENT."**

The 1918-1920 strikes created America's first rent control. When landlords raised rents during wartime housing shortages, tens of thousands struck across New York City. Children paraded through neighborhoods singing "Strike, Strike, Strike!" Women pushed baby carriages back and forth in picket lines outside buildings. Moving men struck for higher wages, protecting tenants from eviction. Courts were overwhelmed—800 eviction cases from a single building. The result: New York's Emergency Rent Laws of 1920, freezing rents until 1929.

The 1930s brought mass eviction resistance. With 65% of families behind on rent during the Depression, Communist-led Unemployed Councils organized direct action. When marshals came, women stayed in buildings while men left—they'd speak from fire escapes through bullhorns: "Today we are being evicted. Tomorrow it will be you. Are you going to let it happen?" When furniture was thrown on the street, crowds held impromptu rallies using the furniture as platforms—then carried everything back inside after police left. The tactic of repeated failed evictions made throwing families out too expensive. Result: rent control in the Bronx within two years. According to historian Richard Boyer, these tactics restored 77,000 families to their homes in New York City alone.

## The language that moved people to risk eviction together

The slogans were simple and repeatable:
- **"Pay No Rent! Fight or Starve!"** appeared on 1930s picket signs
- **"No Heat, No Rent!"** mobilized the 1917 Bronx
- **"We Are Not Removing"** filled Glasgow windows in 1915
- **"Can't Pay? Won't Pay!"** and **"If we can't pay, let's not pay, TOGETHER!"** drove the 2020 May Day strike—the first nationwide rent strike in U.S. history, with over 200,000 households participating

The most mobilizing language connected individual fear to collective power. A 1930s Bronx organizer described the psychological transformation:

> When someone walked into the unemployment office having just lost their job, the response was: "Hooray! Another one laid off. Wonderful!" The confused newcomer would hear: "We're glad you're here. We'll have one more person to distribute leaflets." The organizer explained: "This was the way we changed the terrible thing that was happening... into productive action. We got into control of our lives. We were no longer victims."

The key insight: **isolation breeds despair; collective action breeds joy.** As one participant recalled: "There we were, unemployed people, but we were dancing."

What made people risk arrest and eviction? Historian Mark Naison identified the mechanism: "There's nothing more painful than seeing the sight of a family out in the street with all their possessions. It may have started with a few communists putting the furniture back, but probably ten times as many people were doing this just because they were angry at seeing their neighbors and saying, 'I'm next.'"

## Psychology research reveals what transforms agreement into action

Social psychologists have identified three primary predictors of collective action:

1. **Identity** (how strongly people identify with a group) has the strongest effect
2. **Efficacy** (belief that collective action can achieve goals) comes second
3. **Injustice** (perception of unfair treatment) is third—but critically, emotional response to injustice predicts action far better than merely perceiving injustice

People must **feel** it, not just think about it.

Research shows intentions explain only 18-23% of variance in actual behavior. Agreeing something is wrong does not produce participation. What closes the gap:

**The most powerful commitment device is a social contract with others**—not a personal intention but a public promise to a group. The more specific the commitment ("On May 1st, I will withhold rent"), the stronger the follow-through. Implementation intentions ("When rent is due, I will join the strike") put the stimulus for action into the environment rather than relying on willpower.

**Moral outrage mobilizes when combined with hope and a clear target.** Diffuse anger paralyzes; targeted anger activates. Naming a specific villain—not "the system" but Blackstone, not "landlords" but Stephen Schwarzman's $1.27 billion annual take—gives outrage somewhere to go.

**The bystander effect breaks when one person acts first.** A decisive figure taking action "can inspire others to follow suit, breaking the spell of diffusion of responsibility."

## Wall Street extracted billions from the foreclosure crisis it caused

The numbers tell a story of systematic wealth transfer. The 2008 financial crisis triggered 7.8 million completed foreclosures nationally—over six million American families lost their homes. The government's response: $250 billion to stabilize banks, $45 billion to Citigroup alone, $45 billion to Bank of America. Homeowner assistance received $31.4 billion—and those were grants that will never be repaid, while bank investments generated returns.

Into the wreckage came Blackstone. Between 2012 and 2016, the private equity giant spent approximately $9.6 billion buying roughly 50,000 foreclosed homes—properties where families had just been evicted. The government enabled this: Fannie Mae's REO-to-Rental program sold foreclosed properties directly to institutional investors. In 2017, Fannie Mae backstopped a $1 billion loan to Invitation Homes—the first time it supported a single-family landlord.

Before 2011, no single investor owned more than 1,000 single-family homes. By 2024, the top 10 institutional investors owned 430,000+ single-family rentals. Invitation Homes alone controls 84,000 homes across 16 markets. Blackstone, through its Tricon and Home Partners acquisitions, controls another 62,000 homes. A 2022 MetLife projection suggests institutional investors could control 40% of all single-family rental homes by 2030—approximately 7.6 million properties.

The profits are staggering:
- Blackstone's total return on Invitation Homes exceeded $7 billion—more than doubling its investment
- CEO Stephen Schwarzman's 2022 compensation totaled $1.27 billion (salary, dividends, and carried interest combined)
- His net worth as of May 2025: $43 billion
- Invitation Homes CEO Dallas Tanner took home $10.97 million in 2024

Corporate landlords behave differently than individual owners. Federal Reserve research shows large institutional landlords are 8% more likely to file evictions than small landlords; some PE-backed firms are 18-19% more likely. A 2025 Kansas City study found corporate landlords 3.7 times more likely to file evictions. A UCLA study found corporate landlords in Los Angeles filed evictions on Black tenants at three times the expected rate.

## The vacancy-homelessness ratio exposes the housing system's moral bankruptcy

The 2024 HUD count found 771,480 people experiencing homelessness on a single night—the highest number ever recorded since tracking began in 2007, an 18% increase from the previous year. Nearly 150,000 children were homeless, a 33% jump. Family homelessness increased 39%, the biggest spike of any group.

Meanwhile, Census data shows 15.2 million vacant housing units in America. The ratio is obscene: **approximately 20 empty homes exist for every homeless person.** Even counting only units actively for rent or sale—roughly 4 million—there are still five vacant homes per homeless person.

Nevada's homeless population reached 10,106 people in 2024, up 17% from the prior year. Washoe County specifically counted 1,760 homeless people, with 363 unsheltered—living in tents, cars, on streets. Emergency shelters operated at 93% capacity. Meanwhile, Reno-area rents have climbed approximately 50-60% since 2019, with median apartment rent now $1,463-$1,700 per month. **A minimum-wage worker in Nevada would need to work 82 hours per week to afford a one-bedroom apartment.**

Research from Yale and NYU demonstrates the eviction-to-homelessness pipeline: eviction increases emergency shelter use by over 300% relative to non-evicted tenants. For Black tenants, the increase is 467%. Eviction creates a record that makes renting again harder, trapping people in a downward spiral.

## May Day was born in worker blood and has always been about collective refusal

On May 1, 1886, over 300,000 workers across America walked off their jobs demanding the eight-hour day—the largest worker mobilization in U.S. history to that point. Three days later, at a Haymarket Square rally in Chicago protesting police violence against strikers, a bomb was thrown. Police fired on workers. Eight anarchist labor leaders were arrested in a show trial. Four were hanged on November 11, 1887.

August Spies spoke his last words from the gallows, now inscribed on the Haymarket Martyrs' Monument: **"The day will come when our silence will be more powerful than the voices you are throttling today."**

In 1889, socialist parties and unions worldwide chose May 1st specifically to honor the Haymarket martyrs. Every May Day since has carried their memory. In Mexico, marchers still shout: "¡Que vivan los mártires de Chicago!"

May 1st was also traditionally "Moving Day" in New York—the day landlords announced rent increases and leases expired. This made it a natural flashpoint for tenant organizing. The 1904, 1918-1920, and 1920 Chicago rent strikes all targeted May 1st. On May Day 2020, organizers coordinated the biggest rent strike in American history—over 200,000 households across dozens of cities. Toronto's Thorncliffe Park rent strike launched on May 1, 2023.

**The date carries 140 years of defiance.** Workers have always understood: collective refusal on May Day connects individual struggle to global solidarity.

## The moral case rests on a simple inversion

The standard framing asks whether tenants are justified in withholding rent. The better question: **is rent justified at all?**

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights, Article 25, establishes that "everyone has the right to a standard of living adequate for the health and well-being of himself and of his family, including... housing." If housing is a human right, then charging for access to a right is extraction of what is already owed.

Economist Henry George identified the core problem in the 19th century: land value comes not from landlord labor but from community development and natural demand for a fixed resource. Landlords collect "unearned increment"—value they did nothing to create.

Frederick Douglass provided the framework for understanding power: **"Power concedes nothing without a demand. It never did and it never will. Find out just what any people will quietly submit to and you have found out the exact measure of injustice and wrong which will be imposed upon them."** The limits of landlords are prescribed by the endurance of tenants.

Audre Lorde distinguished productive anger from destructive hatred: "Anger expressed and translated into action in the service of our vision and our future is a liberating and strengthening act of clarification." Guilt avoids action; anger births change.

Martin Luther King Jr., in his 1963 Letter from Birmingham Jail, wrote: "Freedom is never voluntarily given by the oppressor; it must be demanded by the oppressed... 'Wait' has almost always meant 'Never.'"

James Baldwin, writing about Harlem housing conditions, captured the psychology of the ghetto: "All of Harlem is pervaded by a sense of congestion, rather like the insistent, maddening, claustrophobic pounding in the skull that comes from trying to breathe in a very small room with all the windows shut." His prescription was equally clear: **"A ghetto can be improved in one way only: out of existence."**

## Conclusion: what the research reveals about winning

Every successful rent strike shares common elements:
- Women-led neighborhood networks that organized during the day
- Children's involvement creating spectacle and emotional stakes
- Explicit labor language ("strikers," "scabs," "unions") connecting tenant and worker struggle
- Physical presence making eviction costly through repeated resistance
- Strategic overwhelm of courts and landlord resources
- And above all, solidarity across racial, ethnic, and building lines

The psychological research confirms what organizers have always known: people move from agreement to action through identity (we are tenants together), efficacy (our action will work), and emotional response to injustice (this is wrong and makes us angry). The gap closes when commitments are public, specific, and social—made to each other, not just to oneself.

The data provides the moral shock: $1.27 billion to one man while 771,480 Americans sleep homeless. 20 vacant homes per person without shelter. 7.8 million foreclosures feeding Wall Street portfolios. Rents up 50% while wages stagnate. The numbers are not abstract—they are the material conditions that make a rent strike both necessary and just.

**May 1st, 2026 can join May Day 2020, Glasgow 1915, the Bronx 1930s, and Harlem 1963 in the history of tenants who refused to submit.** The question your homepage must answer is not whether people agree the system is broken—they already know. The question is whether they will move from individual despair to collective power. History shows what's possible when they do.
