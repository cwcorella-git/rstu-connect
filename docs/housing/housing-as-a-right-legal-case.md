The housing crisis in America is not a market failure—it is a policy success. For nearly a century, the federal government deliberately constructed a system that treats shelter as a commodity rather than a fundamental human need.

## The Government Declared Housing a Right—Then Abandoned It

In 1949, Congress established as national policy **"the realization as soon as feasible of the goal of a decent home and a suitable living environment for every American family."** This was not aspiration—it was law.

The Housing Act of 1949 (42 U.S.C. § 1441) explicitly declared that "the general welfare and security of the Nation" requires housing sufficient for all Americans. President Truman called this declaration "a national objective."

### Key Legal Framework

- **Housing Act of 1949**: Declared a decent home for every American family as national policy
- **Housing and Urban Development Act of 1968**: Reaffirmed the national housing goal while acknowledging failure for lower-income families
- **Fair Housing Act of 1968 Section 3608(d)**: Created an affirmative government duty to "further" fair housing—not merely avoid discrimination
- **Affirmatively Furthering Fair Housing (AFFH)**: Requires federal agencies to take proactive steps to address segregation

### State Constitutional Provisions

**New York's Article XVII** provides the strongest state foundation: *"The aid, care and support of the needy are public concerns and shall be provided by the state."* This formed the basis for *Callahan v. Carey* (1981), establishing the nation's only enforceable right to shelter.

**New Jersey's Mount Laurel Doctrine**: Every municipality has a constitutional obligation to provide its "fair share" of affordable housing—the strongest framework in the nation for requiring affordable housing.

**Montana's Constitution** declares that *"the dignity of the human being is inviolable"* while guaranteeing *"the rights of pursuing life's basic necessities."*

## Housing Commodification Was Legally Constructed

### The FHA Explicitly Required Racial Segregation

The Federal Housing Administration, established in 1934, systematically constructed housing as a whites-only wealth-building vehicle. The 1938 FHA Underwriting Manual stated:

> "If a neighborhood is to retain stability it is necessary that properties shall continue to be occupied by the same social and racial classes."

The manual identified "infiltration of inharmonious racial or nationality groups" as an "adverse influence." From 1934 to 1968, **more than 98% of FHA loans went to white home purchasers.**

### HOLC Maps Embedded Racism Into Geography

The Home Owners' Loan Corporation created "Residential Security Maps" for 239 American cities, grading neighborhoods by race. Grade D ("Hazardous")—marked in red, giving us "redlining"—was assigned to any neighborhood with Black residents.

**74% of neighborhoods graded "Hazardous" in the 1930s remain low-to-moderate income today.** Only six majority African-American neighborhoods in the entire United States received grades above D.

### The GI Bill Was "Affirmative Action for Whites"

The VA adopted all FHA racial exclusion programs. In Mississippi in 1947, of 3,229 VA home loans, only **two** went to Black veterans. In New York and Northern New Jersey, of 67,000 families who purchased homes with GI Bill loans, **fewer than 100 were non-white**.

### Urban Renewal Means Negro Removal

Between 1949 and 1971, urban renewal displaced approximately **1.36 million people—60% of them nonwhite**. The federal government funded over 2,100 projects, investing more than $13 billion.

The destruction was intentional. The Chancellor of the University of Chicago described urban renewal as "an effective screening tool" and a way of "cutting down the number of Negroes" near the university.

### HOPE VI Destroyed More Housing Than It Created

The HOPE VI program (1992-2010) demolished **98,592 units while building only 55,318 replacements**. Less than 12% of displaced residents moved into replacement housing. 95% of those displaced were families of color.

## International Law Recognizes Housing as a Human Right

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) declares that **"everyone has the right to a standard of living adequate for the health and well-being of himself and of his family, including food, clothing, housing."**

Eleanor Roosevelt chaired the drafting committee. The United States voted in favor.

**South Africa demonstrates constitutional housing rights are enforceable.** Section 26 declares: "Everyone has a right to have access to adequate housing." In *Government v. Grootboom* (2000), the Constitutional Court found housing policy unconstitutional for failing to provide emergency housing for those in desperate need.

**UN-HABITAT estimates 75% of states** have constitutions or national laws promoting housing rights. The U.S. is the outlier.

## The Criminalization of Homelessness

The Supreme Court's 2024 decision in *City of Grants Pass v. Johnson* allows cities to criminalize sleeping outdoors even when no shelter exists. Justice Sotomayor's dissent: the majority decision *"leaves the most vulnerable in our society with an impossible choice: Either stay awake or be arrested."*

## Tenant Organizing Has Transformed the Law Before

- **Block v. Hirsh (1921)**: Supreme Court upheld rent control, establishing that "housing is a necessary of life"
- **Javins v. First National Realty Corp. (1970)**: Established the implied warranty of habitability—arose from a rent strike with over 1,000 code violations
- **Edwards v. Habib (1968)**: Established the retaliatory eviction defense

The modern tenant movement continues winning. KC Tenants passed a citywide Tenants Bill of Rights. California, Oregon, and Washington have all passed statewide rent control. New York enacted Good Cause Eviction in 2024.

## The Case for Collective Responsibility

The evidence establishes that housing injustice in America was deliberately created by government policy. This collective responsibility is not charity. It is not compassion. It is **obligation arising from causation**.

We made the system. We benefit from the system (those of us who own property, claim mortgage deductions, or live in neighborhoods protected by exclusionary zoning). We are therefore obligated to unmake the system.

Human dignity—sanctified by law from the Eighth Amendment to state constitutional provisions—demands that no person be left unhoused. If dignity is inviolable, if the state cannot demean existence or destroy status in organized society, then a system that produces chronic homelessness while criminalizing its symptoms violates foundational constitutional principles.

**Housing is a right. The law says so. History proves so. Dignity demands so. Now we must make it real.**
