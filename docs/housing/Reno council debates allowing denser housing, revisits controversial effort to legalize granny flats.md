RENO, Nev. (News 4 & Fox 11) -- Five years after rejecting a
plan to allow so-called granny flats in Reno, the city council
Wednesday reconsidered the policy, one of several
recommended by an outside consultant to address the region's
affordable housing problem.
In a 70-page report, housing expert Shane Phillips suggested
the city explore a handful of policy proposals aimed at boosting
supply. Staff estimated the city needs an additional 21,000
affordable units.

Phillips specifically recommended adopting "by-right"
approvals for small and medium projects, upzoning lower-
density multifamily zoning districts and legalizing granny flats,
also referred to as accessory dwelling units or in-law quarters.
After a lengthy special meeting, the city council directed staff
to move forward with drafting a granny flat ordinance and
asked them to come back later this year with more specifics on
other policies.
"We know what we've been doing historically has not
necessarily moved the needle in a measurable way for as
many people as we want, so we're trying to figure out what
tools we have in the toolbox as a city," councilman Devon
Reese said.

By-right approvals
Phillips recommended the city adopt objective design and
development standards and allow smaller and medium
housing projects to be approved "by-right," meaning they
would go straight to the permitting phase assuming all
requirements are met.
Some council members worried this approach would limit the
public feedback process. Councilmember Naomi Duerr
specifically pointed to a recent example where she said
developers responded to public input and made a slightly
smaller project as a result, winning over concerned neighbors
in the process.

Upzoning multi-family areas

One recommendation was to increase zoning density in some
multi-family zoning districts, allowing more units to be built per
acre.
Phillips said it could involve allowing up to 30 units per acre on
land previously zoned for up to 14 or 21 units per acre. Denser
land, which currently permits up to 30 units per acre, could be
rezoned to allow up to 45 or 60 units.
Duerr was skeptical of efforts to significantly increase
maximum density, referring to a neighborhood near Moana
Lane and Plumas St. that she said has very little parking and
higher crime rates.
"It seems like the development pressures, the living pressure
of living so close in an environment like Reno - we're not in
downtown L.A. - is at odds I guess with our culture," she said.

Granny flats

Phillips recommended legalizing accessory dwelling units on
residential land citywide and allowing them to be approved
quickly and inexpensively. Currently, they are not allowed in
most of the Biggest Little City.
ADUs arestandalone housing units on existing single-family
lots, which can be connected to the single-family home or
separated, and are sometimes in the attic or basement of
existing properties.
Phillips and other experts have acknowledged ADUs are not a
quick and widespread solution, but can help the housing crisis
at the margins. If approved, Phillips suggested only a few
hundred might be built in the first few years.
"We're a small community and I don't think a lot of people are
going to race out tomorrow and start building ADUs. Are they
an option? Sure. Are there areas of our town where they would
be appropriate? Likely. But that is something that our
community has got to decide," Reese said.
The city council unanimously rejected a proposal to allow
granny flats following a fierce debate in 2018. That policy
would've given over 12,000 singe-family homeowners the
option to build an ADU on lots larger than 9,000 feet.
Mayor Hillary Schieve, who previously feared the policy would
be 'disastrous' in certain neighborhoods, told News 4-Fox 11
last year that the city 'absolutely' needed to revisit the issue.
Several council members were also not on the council back in
2018.

The policy, when it returns in draft form, is expected to face
staunch opposition, especially from residents in older
neighborhoods such as the Newlands Historic District.
Many fear the units will ruin the character of existing
neighborhoods by increasing density and adding to traffic and
parking issues.
Other solutions
Phillips' report also recommended things like updating the
city's capital projects plan, coordinating better with other local
agencies and proactively regulating short-term rentals,
adopting restrictions on full-unit and year-round rentals.
