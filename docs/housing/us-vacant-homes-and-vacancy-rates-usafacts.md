Source: USAFacts, <https://usafacts.org/> (retrieved March 18, 2026). Census Bureau
vacancy data through 2024, with homeownership and rental vacancy rates through Q3 2025.

Useful as a sourced counter to the "we just need to build more" framing: the national
gross vacancy rate in 2024 was the lowest in at least 25 years, and more than half of
year-round vacant units were being *held off the market* — two facts that cut in
opposite directions and are worth citing carefully. Note also that "vacant" here
includes seasonal and vacation homes, which is why tourist states top the list. This is
a different measure from the "20 vacant homes per homeless person" figure used in the
May Day rent strike research; do not conflate them.

## Headline numbers

- Approximately **15.1 million vacant homes** nationwide in 2024 (Census Bureau).
- Those units represent **10.3% of the country's housing inventory**.
- Vacant homes peaked at nearly **19 million** in the wake of the 2008 housing crisis,
  and the number dropped **more than 20% between 2009 and 2024**.

## Homeownership rates vs. vacancy rates

The homeownership rate is the percentage of homes that are owner-occupied. In Q3 2025
the US homeownership rate was **65.2%**. Over recent decades it peaked at **69.4%** in
Q2 2004 and reached a low of **63.1%** in Q2 2016.

The vacancy rate is the share of homes not lived in as a permanent residence. Vacation
homes, even if occupied, count as vacant because the occupants have a permanent
residence elsewhere. Uninhabitable housing (no roof, no windows) is excluded. Owned and
rented units are tracked separately; rentals are more likely to be vacant.

Vacancies generally declined for both from 2008 through 2021. Rental vacancies hit a
recent low in Q4 2021 and have since increased **1.5 percentage points**. Owner
vacancies hit a recent low in Q2 2023 and have also increased, by **0.5 points**.

As of Q3 2025, **7.1% of rentals** and **1.2% of homeowner inventory** were on the
market. Overall, the gross national vacancy rate in 2024 was **10.3%** — the lowest rate
recorded in at least 25 years.

## Which states have the most vacant homes?

In 2024, **Maine, Vermont, and Alaska** had the nation's highest gross vacancy rates.
Maine has recorded the highest or tied-highest rate in the nation for 13 of the past
14 years.

Since 2009 — the first year of data after the 2008 crisis — gross vacancy rates were
lower in every state by 2024. In sixteen states the rate declined by at least
5 percentage points.

- Biggest declines: **Florida (-8.8 points)**, Arizona (-7.8), Alaska (-7.4).
- Six states had gross vacancy rates over 20% in 2009; by 2024 **Maine was the only one**
  still that high (its own rate having dropped 2.1 points).
- Smallest changes: Louisiana (-0.1), New York (-0.9), Iowa (-1.0).

## Which metros have the most vacant homes?

Across the country, metro vacancy rates ranged from a high of **34.1%** in Cape
Coral–Fort Myers, Florida to a low of **4.0%** in New Haven–Milford, Connecticut. The
national average for the 75 largest metropolitan statistical areas was **9.1%**.

Two Florida metros — Cape Coral–Fort Myers and North Port–Bradenton–Sarasota — had gross
vacancy rates **3.7x** and **2.6x** the national metropolitan average.

This is not necessarily surprising: the gross vacancy rate counts all vacant properties,
including vacation properties, so states with popular tourist destinations register
higher rates. The Census Bureau notes that the largest category of vacant housing is
classified as "seasonal, recreational, or occasional use." In over one-fifth of US
counties, those units made up at least 50% of the vacant housing stock as of the 2020
Census.

Between 2009 and 2024, **Orlando–Kissimmee–Sanford, Florida** recorded the largest gross
vacancy rate decrease (**-11.4 points**), while **Buffalo–Cheektowaga–Niagara Falls, New
York** recorded the largest increase (**+4.9**).

## What do these rates tell us?

Taken on their own, falling vacancy rates depict a tightening real estate market at both
the metro and state level. Tightening supply elevates prices and affordability pressure.
The data can also inform potential sellers about when it might be most advantageous to
list.

In Q3 2025, **more than half of US residences classified as "year-round vacant" were
actively held off the market.**
