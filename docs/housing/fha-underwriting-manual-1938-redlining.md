The Federal Housing Administration's 1938 Underwriting Manual stands as one of the most consequential documents in American housing history. It codified racial discrimination into federal policy, creating the framework for residential segregation that persists to this day.

## Historical Context

During the Great Depression, the Federal Housing Administration (established 1934) was created to stabilize the housing market by insuring mortgages, making homeownership accessible to middle-class families. The agency relied on appraisers following its Underwriting Manual to evaluate properties before approving loan insurance.

Simultaneously, the Home Owners' Loan Corporation (HOLC) produced "Residential Security Maps" for 239 American cities, grading neighborhoods by perceived investment risk. These maps marked predominantly non-white areas as "Hazardous" (Grade D)—a practice that became known as **"redlining."**

## The Manual's Discriminatory Language

The 1938 FHA Underwriting Manual explicitly required racial segregation as a condition of mortgage insurance. Key passages include:

### On Neighborhood Investigation

> "Areas surrounding a location are investigated to determine whether incompatible racial and social groups are present."

### On Racial "Infiltration"

> "If a neighborhood is to retain stability, it is necessary that properties shall continue to be occupied by the same social and racial classes. A change in social or racial occupancy generally contributes to instability and a decline in values."

### On Risk Assessment

The manual assessed risk based on whether areas were in danger of being "invaded" by "inharmonious racial or nationality groups."

### Recommended Restrictive Covenants

To limit perceived risk, the manual recommended **restrictive covenants** that:

> "prohibit the occupancy of properties except by the race for which they are intended."

## Implementation and Impact

### Systematic Denial of Credit

The FHA's policies resulted in systematic denial of mortgage insurance in minority neighborhoods:

- The FHA refused to insure mortgages for properties in **racially mixed neighborhoods**
- Even some **all-white neighborhoods bordering Black areas** were denied coverage due to "possibility of integration"
- When pressed by NAACP attorney Thurgood Marshall, local FHA officials admitted that **"no mortgages were guaranteed for colored people in any neighborhood which was less than 50% Negro"**

### Scale of Discrimination

From 1934 to 1968:

- **More than 98% of FHA-insured loans** went to white home purchasers
- Black veterans were systematically excluded from GI Bill housing benefits that operated through FHA channels
- In Mississippi in 1947, of 3,229 VA home loans, only **two** went to Black veterans
- In New York and Northern New Jersey, of 67,000 GI Bill home purchases, **fewer than 100** were by non-white families

### Lasting Geographic Impact

The effects of FHA-mandated segregation persist:

- **74% of neighborhoods** graded "Hazardous" in the 1930s remain low-to-moderate income today
- Only **six majority African-American neighborhoods** in the entire United States received grades above D
- The racial wealth gap—largely driven by differential homeownership rates—traces directly to these policies

## Scholarly Assessment

Historian David Hillier argues that the FHA:

> "reflected an anti-urban and racial bias deeper than the HOLC's," because its Underwriting Manual required privately-owned lenders to avoid risks associated with neighborhood demographics in order to qualify for publicly-funded mortgage insurance.

The manual didn't merely reflect private prejudice—it **institutionalized and enforced discrimination** through federal policy, requiring banks to discriminate as a condition of accessing government mortgage insurance.
