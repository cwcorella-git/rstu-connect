# The Living Language of Resistance: How Movements Speak in 2026

The language of effective organizing has transformed radically since 2022. Successful tenant unions, labor strikes, and political mobilizations now share a distinct rhetorical DNA: explicit class conflict framing, material specificity over abstract ideology, social media transparency, and personal narrative grounding systemic analysis.

The tenant movement in particular has developed a powerful vocabulary—"My rent is my power," "Every eviction is an act of violence," "Stay dangerous"—that sounds nothing like historical labor rhetoric and everything like the anger of people who've stopped asking for permission.

This report captures how people actually talk about housing, collective action, and resistance today—the living vernacular that makes a rent strike call sound like 2026, not 1930.

## The tenant movement has invented its own language

The contemporary tenant movement speaks in a voice distinctly its own. Tara Raghuveer, founder of KC Tenants and the Tenant Union Federation, has articulated its core philosophy:

> "The vehicle to organize poor and working-class people in the 21st century is the tenant union. The tenant union needs to be for that group of people what the labor union was in the 20th century at its peak."

The movement's signature phrase—**"Every eviction is an act of violence"**—emerged from KC Tenants during COVID and became movement mantra. Raghuveer frames it explicitly: "Every eviction is an act of state-sanctioned violence against one class of people and benefiting another class of people and their profits." This isn't metaphor; it's the foundational claim that reframes eviction from legal procedure to assault.

During the 2024 Kansas City rent strike—the first targeting the Federal Housing Finance Agency and lasting 247 days—tenants developed language that sounds nothing like historical union rhetoric:

- Derek (striking tenant): **"My rent is my power, and I will use my power with my neighbors until we win what we are owed."**
- Anna Heetman (Independence Towers): **"We refused to pay for our own misery. We won't pay one dime in back-rent, and we won't pay with our lives ever again."**
- Victory statement: **"Stay dangerous. Stay united. The slumlords are on notice."**

The LA Tenants Union, through Tracy Rosenthal and Leonardo Vilchis's 2024 book *Abolish Rent*, has articulated the most radical framing: "The entire real estate industry relies on privatizing a common resource (land), hoarding a human need (housing), blocking public intervention or competition, and maintaining a captive market of tenants to exploit and dominate." Their chant—**"Housing is a human right, not just for the rich and white"**—combines the universal claim with the racial dimension.

### Contemporary tenant organizing slogans and phrases

- "If we have a chance, the chance lives in the tenant union" (Tenant Union Federation tagline)
- "The people closest to the problem are closest to the solution"
- "Rights are what you make and what you take"
- "We make our community by defending it"
- "Collectively bargained leases"
- "Safe, accessible, and truly affordable home"
- "Tenants don't need a bailout. They need jubilee."
- "Slumlords" (preferred over "landlords" in militant contexts)
- "Corporate landlords" and "predatory speculators"

## The labor movement found its voice again with explicit class warfare

Shawn Fain revolutionized labor rhetoric during the 2023 UAW strike by combining biblical framing, working-class authenticity, and unabashed class warfare language. His signature moment: throwing Stellantis's contract offer into a labeled trash can on Facebook Live, declaring **"That's where it belongs—in the trash—because that's what it is."**

The trash can wasn't just theater; it signaled a complete break from decades of conciliatory union language. Fain explicitly embraced "EAT THE RICH"—wearing the t-shirt in international media appearances and stating: "Billionaires, in my opinion, don't have a right to exist." He refused to shake hands with CEOs, breaking what he called "a decades-long tradition" of deferential labor leadership.

### What made Fain's rhetoric work

His formula combined several elements no previous union leader had synthesized:

**Biblical-populist fusion:** Fain quotes scripture constantly while carrying his grandmother's Bible from a 1933 orphanage. "In the kingdom of God, no one hoards all the wealth while everyone else suffers and starves." This gives class conflict moral authority while grounding it in Middle American cultural vernacular.

**Generational stakes:** "This is our generation's defining moment"—framing the strike as historical pivot, not routine bargaining.

**Explicit class framing:** "There's been class warfare going on in this country for the last forty years. The billionaire class has been taking everything and leaving everybody else to fight for the scraps."

**Transparency revolution:** Weekly Facebook Lives showing actual corporate proposals with charts—"The entire public started tuning in."

**Self-aware combativeness:** "They think they know us, but us autoworkers know better. We may be foul-mouthed, but we're strategic. We may get fired up, but we're disciplined."

The "Stand Up Strike" framing deliberately echoed the 1937 Sit-Down Strikes while claiming contemporary relevance: "Then as now, we face massive inequality across our society. Then as now, our industry is rapidly changing and workers are left behind."

## Hollywood strikes added humor and cultural fluency

The WGA/SAG-AFTRA strikes (2023) demonstrated that creative workers bring distinctive voice to labor action. Fran Drescher's strike announcement channeled moral outrage: "Eventually the people break down the gates of Versailles, and then it's over and we're at that moment right now."

Picket signs became viral content:
- "Pay your writers or we'll spoil Succession"
- "I told ChatGPT to write a picket sign and it sucked"
- "Give up just ONE yacht"

The implicit argument—the jokes don't write themselves—was proven daily through creative protest language.

## How people actually talk about housing online

The vernacular of housing struggle on TikTok, Reddit, and Twitter has developed its own grammar. The most viral phrase—**"the landlord special"**—describes shoddy repair work (paint slapped over mold, outlets painted over) done before showings. The hashtag has millions of views, and tenants now ironically give apartments "the landlord special" when moving out.

The phrases that resonate most are starkly simple:
- **"I can't afford to live"**—direct, total, unembarrassed
- **"The math is not mathing"**—from a viral TikTok about rent overcharges that doubled requests to NYC's housing agency
- **"Where has the plot gone?"**—expressing bewilderment at how society is supposed to work
- **"Who can afford that?"**—rhetorical question that assumes shared impossibility

The emotional register varies: dark humor ("Pretty soon they gonna split us up in districts"), exhaustion ("We're just living day to day at this point"), TikTok anger at landlord influencers who post rent increases, and solidarity through shared struggle ("I know so many people are struggling").

### What makes housing content go viral

**The forced choice framing creates urgency:** "Every month... pay rent or swim team fees for one of her three kids. Rent or school supplies. Rent or groceries." This makes the crisis concrete, not abstract.

**Specific dollar amounts ground the outrage:** "A one-bedroom apartment, $1,800. Two-bedroom apartment, $2,200." Unlike policy discussions about "affordability," these numbers are viscerally understood.

**The reveal format drives engagement**—tenants discovering landlord fraud, documenting overcharges, exposing lies. "The math is not mathing" launches investigation videos that teach people their rights.

## Progressive political rhetoric that actually mobilizes

The Fighting Oligarchy Tour (2025), with Bernie Sanders and AOC drawing over 261,100 attendees—larger than any Sanders 2016 or 2020 event—demonstrates what mobilization rhetoric looks like now. The key rhetorical moves:

**Naming specific villains:** "Today we have three multi-billionaires, Mr. Musk, Mr. Bezos, Mr. Zuckerberg... These three combined own more wealth than the bottom half of American society." Abstract "inequality" becomes concrete people.

**Personal narrative grounding systemic claims:** AOC: "I don't believe in healthcare, labor and human dignity, because I'm an extremist or a Marxist. I believe it because I was a waitress, because I've scrubbed toilets with my mom to afford school."

**"Common sense" reframing:** "What these folks want to call radical and crazy, I think are just common sense. We live in the wealthiest nation in the history of the world. So I believe that when a person gets sick, they shouldn't go bankrupt. Common sense."

**Division-strategy exposure:** "The same billionaires are taking a wrecking ball to our country and they derive their power from dividing working people apart. They specialize in getting us to turn on one another along lines of left and right, race and gender."

## Grassroots organizers speak differently than politicians

The critical difference: **specificity of asks and immediacy of stakes.** Politicians speak of national statistics; organizers name specific landlords. Politicians say "fight back"; organizers say "Show up at 6pm Tuesday."

Chris Smalls (Amazon Labor Union): "We didn't have no plan. We had no playbook. We just knew that we were doing something." His success—where established unions with millions of members failed—came from worker-to-worker language, barbecues outside the warehouse, solving immediate problems like "How do people get to work?"

Effective organizers don't lead with ideology: "I didn't want to turn someone off by being super explicitly political from the jump... class consciousness does alter your framework... We have only had a positive response from tenants."

## What language stopped working (and why)

The late 2010s progressive vocabulary has largely exhausted itself. "Woke" moved from Black grassroots activism (Ferguson, Black Lives Matter) to white performative signaling to right-wing pejorative. By 2024, it was primarily used mockingly.

### Phrases that now read as cringe or performative

- "Check your privilege"—accusatory rather than illuminating
- "Do the work"—vague, easily claimed without accountability
- "Centering marginalized voices"—academic jargon that alienates
- "Holding space"—new-age inflected, lacks concrete meaning
- "Educate yourself"—dismissive rather than inviting
- "Ally/Allyship"—co-opted to the point of meaninglessness
- DEI language—in full corporate retreat; major companies disbanded DEI departments in 2024

**The core problem:** These phrases signal group membership rather than making concrete demands. They require insider knowledge to parse. They accuse rather than invite. The black squares on Instagram for "Blackout Tuesday" (2020)—which crowded out actual resources—became the emblematic example of performance replacing substance.

### The authenticity test

Language that works now passes a simple test: **Does it connect to material reality?** "The rent is too damn high" does. "Interrogating structural power dynamics" doesn't.

**Language that sounds radical but doesn't work:**
- Academic/theoretical framing
- Purity-test language that excludes potential allies
- Abstract ideological claims without material grounding
- Buzzwords requiring insider knowledge

**Language that actually radicalizes:**
- Material, economic framing with specific numbers
- Shared experience recognition ("I know so many people are struggling")
- Specific demands (repeal rent control bans, build 75,000 homes)
- Stories showing concrete consequences
- Universal framing that doesn't require agreeing on a political framework

## The contemporary resistance vocabulary

The living language of 2026 resistance has distinct characteristics:

### Tone and register

- **Explicitly class-conscious:** "There's a billionaire class, and there's the rest of us"
- **Morally clear without moralizing:** "Every eviction is an act of violence" states a claim, doesn't lecture
- **Strategic and self-aware:** "We may be foul-mouthed, but we're strategic"
- **Humor with substance:** Creative expression that proves the point (picket signs, TikToks)
- **Specific, not abstract:** Dollar amounts, named villains, concrete demands

## Principles for a 2026 rent strike call

Based on this research, effective contemporary resistance language follows these principles:

1. **Name specific enemies, not abstract forces.** "Musk, Bezos, Zuckerberg" works; "the wealthy" doesn't. Name the landlord, the property company, the financing agency.

2. **Ground systemic claims in personal narrative.** AOC's waitressing stories and Fain's autoworker family history provide credibility that policy arguments alone cannot.

3. **Make demands material and specific.** "3% rent cap on all federally-backed buildings"—not "affordable housing."

4. **Use the forced choice frame.** "Pay rent or feed my kids" makes the crisis visceral in ways statistics can't.

5. **Embrace explicit class framing.** The successful movements of 2023-2025 stopped pretending class warfare wasn't happening.

6. **Combine moral clarity with strategic discipline.** Biblical framing plus tactical savvy. Righteous anger channeled productively.

7. **Speak directly to material experience.** "I can't afford to live" doesn't require ideological agreement. It's just true.

8. **Make the path to action specific.** Date, time, location. Not "get involved"—"Show up Tuesday at 6pm."

9. **Build community as the ask itself.** "Turn to the person next to you. Leave here connected to someone you didn't know before."

10. **Reject performative language.** If it sounds like a corporate DEI statement or an academic paper, it's dead on arrival.

---

The language that works now is the language that stopped asking for permission—that states material claims, names specific enemies, makes specific demands, and assumes the moral high ground while demonstrating strategic discipline. It's the language of people who've realized that **rights are what you make and what you take.**
