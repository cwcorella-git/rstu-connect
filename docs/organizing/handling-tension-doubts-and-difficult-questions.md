A canvasser-facing worksheet — the "Tension Table" — for naming hesitation before,
during, or after outreach, whether the hesitation is yours or the person you're talking
to. Pairs with *The Real Work Club Co-Creators Onboarding Guide* (also in this library),
which references this as its tension tool. Directly usable for RSTU door-knocking prep
and canvass debriefs.

## Why this tool exists

Even the best causes raise tough questions. Even the most capable people second-guess
themselves.

This tool helps you name the tension without losing momentum, whether it's your own
hesitation or someone else's resistance. It doesn't just give you answers, it gives you
options to stay steady, compassionate, and curious while still moving forward.

Use it when:

- You feel unsure about what you're doing
- Someone pushes back or questions your effort
- You want to be more prepared, more calm, and more honest

## How it works

The Tension Table has three components:

> 🤯 **The Tension** → 💬 **What Might Be Happening** → 🧭 **How You Might Respond**

Fill it out before you begin your outreach (to prepare), during your effort (as things
arise), or after a hard moment (for reflection and re-alignment).

## Example tensions and responses

| The Tension | What Might Be Happening | How You Might Respond |
|---|---|---|
| "I'm scared they'll judge me or think I'm trying to sell them something." | You're associating outreach with manipulation, not connection. You care deeply, and you don't want to pressure anyone. | "Hey, I get it if this isn't your thing. I just wanted to share because it means something to me." |
| "What if they ask something I don't know how to answer?" | You're afraid that uncertainty equals incompetence. You think you need to be an expert to care. | "That's a great question. I don't know yet, but I'm learning. Want me to find out and follow up?" |
| "What if this doesn't really help? What if I look foolish for backing it?" | You might be wrestling with perfectionism or fear of being 'duped' or disappointed. | "Nothing's perfect. But this feels honest, smart, and worth showing up for. That's enough for me to start." |
| "What if I'm not the right person to speak up?" | You're questioning your role, value, or credentials. Imposter syndrome is active. | "I may not be the loudest or most polished, but I care. That's reason enough to speak." |
| They said: "Isn't that just another charity?" | They may be cynical, uninformed, or defensive about previous disappointing experiences. | "I get that. Most programs are all talk. This one's different — real structure, real results. Want to see how?" |
| They asked: "Do they even want our help?" | People sometimes doubt their usefulness or feel awkward about giving. They may fear being intrusive. | "Yes, but not in a savior way. It's about showing up with respect, not taking over." |
| "I feel like a hypocrite. I haven't figured my own life out." | Imposter syndrome. You think only 'perfect' people are allowed to lead or care. | "You're human. And helping something grow while growing yourself is one of the most honest things you can do." |
| "What if I say the wrong thing and make it worse?" | Fear of causing harm through imperfect communication. Paralysis through over-analysis. | "Saying something imperfect with good intentions is usually better than saying nothing at all." |

## Group facilitation guide

Use this approach with your team, support group, or advocacy community.

1. **Individual reflection (5 minutes):** Invite each person to name one tension or doubt
   they're currently holding about their advocacy work.
2. **Pair sharing (10 minutes):** Have participants share their tension with a partner
   and explore what might be behind it together.
3. **Group brainstorm (15 minutes):** Facilitate a larger discussion about common themes
   and potential responses. Focus on what could be behind common tensions: shame, fear,
   misinformation, social conditioning, past disappointments.
4. **Practice round (10 minutes):** Offer roleplay opportunities where people can
   practice responding to their tensions in real-life scenarios.
5. **Affirmation close (5 minutes):** End with this reminder — "The fact that we're
   naming this together is already a form of leadership."

### Facilitation tips

- Create psychological safety by sharing your own tensions first
- Normalize uncertainty as part of meaningful work, not a sign of weakness
- Focus on progress, not perfection — small steps count
- Remind participants that having tensions means they care deeply

## Why this approach works

The Tension Table honors discomfort without dramatizing it. It gives people:

- Permission to feel stuck without shame or judgment
- A clear path to keep moving despite uncertainty
- The experience of being supported without being rescued
- Practical language for difficult conversations
- Confidence through preparation rather than avoidance

## Building your tension resilience

Regular use of this tool develops:

- **Emotional intelligence:** Better understanding of your own triggers and patterns
- **Response flexibility:** Multiple options instead of fight-or-flight reactions
- **Authentic communication:** Speaking from truth rather than fear
- **Sustained engagement:** Ability to stay involved during difficult periods
- **Leadership capacity:** Confidence to guide others through similar challenges

### Quick reference for daily use

> When tension arises: **STOP → NAME IT → EXPLORE IT → RESPOND THOUGHTFULLY**
>
> Tension often signals that something important is happening. Be curious.
