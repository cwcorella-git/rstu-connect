# NYC Tenant Organizations and the Post-WWI Housing Crisis

*How Rent Strikes Won America's First Rent Control (1917-1929)*

This history documents the massive tenant organizing wave that swept New York City following World War I—a movement that achieved America's first rent control laws through direct action, rent strikes, and political organizing.

## The Crisis Context

Following World War I, New York City experienced a severe housing shortage:

| Year | Apartments Completed | Change |
|------|---------------------|--------|
| 1915 | ~24,000 | Baseline |
| 1919 | 1,481 | **-94%** |

- Defense industry priorities halted residential construction
- Wartime prosperity drew thousands of families to the city
- Vacancy rate plummeted from **5.6% (1916)** to just **0.36% (April 1920)**

Landlords capitalized on scarcity through aggressive rent increases. A particularly exploitative system involved lessees who would lease buildings at fixed rates, then maximize profits by raising rents and cutting services.

## Early Organizing: 1917-1918

### The First Rent Strike

The first significant rent strike occurred in **January 1917** in the Crotona Park section of the Bronx:

- **500-1,000 tenants** organized across 25 buildings
- Led by the Socialist Women's Consumers League
- One observer noted: *"entire blocks are being organized"*
- Though initially defeated in court, participants established the **Bronx Tenants League**

### "No Heat, No Rent"

A severe winter in 1918 reignited resistance:

- Temperatures near zero
- Widespread coal shortages
- Landlords failed to provide heat and hot water
- Tenants conducted a **"No Heat, No Rent"** campaign
- Socialist lawyers won temporary rent reductions for hundreds of families

## Building-Level Victory Tactics

Tenant organizers developed effective pressure tactics:

### The Economics of Eviction

In organized buildings, the **expense of eviction—approximately $10-12 per family**—often exceeded projected rental gains. This made eviction economically irrational.

### Picket and Replacement Prevention

When landlords hired movers, striking residents would:

- **Picket buildings**
- **Threaten prospective replacement tenants**
- Make finding replacements extremely difficult

### Negotiated Settlements

This economic pressure forced many owners to negotiate settlements offering:

- **Half the requested rent increase**
- **One-year leases** (providing security)
- **Repairs**

### The Sophie Epstein Example

In summer 1919, Sophie Epstein helped lead 40 families resisting a near-doubling of rent on East 138th Street in the South Bronx:

- Through picketing and direct action, they won a lease with only a **$1/month increase**
- Non-organizing neighbors paid significantly more
- Demonstrated the concrete benefits of collective action

## The Greater New York Tenants League

By May 1918, organized tenant leagues formed the **Greater New York Tenants League** to push legislative reforms. By year's end, they claimed 1,321 dues-paying members.

By 1919, workers had organized **25,000 apartment dwellers** into the league, which worked with the Socialist Party to lead a rent strike of 500 buildings.

### The Williamsburg Strike

In August 1919, **450 families in 17 jointly owned buildings** struck under the auspices of the Williamsburg Tenants League.

### New Jersey Solidarity

By March 1919, around **8,500 people** had gone on rent strike in New Jersey in just February and March.

## The Red Scare Backlash

The movement faced severe repression as the **Red Scare of 1919** intensified:

### Official Red-Baiting

- Landlords, judges, politicians, and police blamed strikes on **"Bolsheviki"** and **"outside agitators"**
- "Immigrants" blamed for radicalism
- Deportation threatened as solution to "disorder"
- Federal government deported thousands of radicals

### Legal Attacks

- In September 1919, the Mayor's Committee on Rent Profiteering brought **criminal proceedings** against the Socialist East Side Tenants League
- In November 1919, three members of Harlem's Tenants League were **sentenced to workhouses** for conspiracy
- Some judges singled out league members for harsher treatment

## Government Co-optation

### The Mayor's Committee

By 1919, **96,623 families** faced eviction proceedings. Mayor Hylan's Committee on Rent Profiteering, led by Nathan Hirsch, attempted to redirect tenant discontent into "legitimate channels":

- Recruited 150 volunteer lawyers
- Established arbitration panels
- Eventually settled 95% of 30,000 cases

However, the committee's central role was **"defusing Socialist influence and popularity, not necessarily addressing the housing crisis."**

### Conservative Tenant Associations

Conservative tenant associations—led by Irish-American figures like George Donnelly (Fair Play Rent Association) and allied with Democratic and Republican machines—emerged to displace Socialist leagues as "legitimate" representatives:

- **Opposed rent striking**
- Sought legislative solutions through existing channels
- Positioned themselves as respectable alternatives

## Legislative Victory: The Emergency Rent Laws

### April 1920 Laws

Under pressure from multiple constituencies, the legislature acted:

- Judges could grant eviction stays up to one year
- Could evaluate whether rent increases were "unjust, unreasonable and oppressive"
- However, loopholes made the laws inadequate

### September 1920: America's First Rent Control

The **September 1920 Emergency Rent Laws** represented the nation's first rent control program:

- Eliminated the 25% increase threshold
- Placed full burden of proof on landlords
- Restricted landlords' rights to deny lease renewals
- Designated municipal courts as legal arbiters of disputed rents

## The Movement's Decline

### Conservative Takeover

Following the September laws, Socialist tenant leagues declined dramatically:

- Party's internal schism over communism
- External repression
- The laws themselves undercut their appeal

By early 1921, no evidence of Socialist-led tenant activity remained.

### Conservative Limitations

Conservative organizations like the **Washington Heights Tenants Association (WHTA)** flourished instead, maintaining 7,000+ members. But they accepted the premise that tenant protections should expand and contract with housing shortages, failing to advocate for permanent reforms.

### The Laws' Erosion

As housing construction increased and vacancy rates rose (exceeding 5% by 1927), tenant organizations lost leverage:

| Year | Protection Level |
|------|-----------------|
| 1926 | Apartments under $20/room |
| 1927 | Apartments under $15/room |
| 1929 | Laws lapsed entirely |

## Lessons for Today

### What Worked

1. **Direct action produced results.** Rent strikes, properly organized, proved remarkably effective.
2. **Economic pressure on landlords.** Making eviction more expensive than accommodation shifted power.
3. **Neighborhood solidarity.** Organized blocks prevented replacement tenants.
4. **Legal support.** Socialist lawyers helped tenants navigate courts.
5. **Legislative pressure.** Street action created conditions for legal reform.

### What Failed

1. **Accepting "emergency" framing.** By agreeing protections were temporary, conservatives surrendered initiative.
2. **Abandoning transformative demands.** Public housing and permanent reform were excluded from consideration.
3. **Vulnerability to co-optation.** Government recognition marginalized militant representatives.
4. **Dependence on market conditions.** When vacancies rose, organizing lost urgency.

### The Core Insight

The trajectory illustrates how movements can be co-opted even in apparent victory: as government officials recognized tenant legitimacy while simultaneously marginalizing their most militant representatives, the space for transformative demands narrowed considerably.
