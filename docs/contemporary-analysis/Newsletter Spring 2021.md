## **Interesting Facts**

Connecticut: Named for the Pequot word "Quinnehtukqut" which means "beside the long tidal river".

Delaware: Named for the indigenous people, the Delaware or Lenni Lappe.

Indiana: Congress created the name, and it means "Land of the Indians"

Kentucky: Two tribes claim to have named this region. The Wyandot/Algonquin word for "plain" and the Choctaw for "the hunting place".

Missouri: Named for the Missouri Indians who lived along the Missouri River. Missouri means "canoe haven".

South Dakota: Named for the Dakota Lakota tribe. Word is Lakota Sioux meaning "Friends" or "Allies".

Oregon: Named by the Spanish "Orejon" or "big-ear" which is the name the Spanish applied to several Indian tribes in the region.

Wisconsin: Named by the Ojibwa. It means the "grassy place" in the Algonquin language.

## **Myeerah: Search for the Chief**

By: Elizabeth-Ann-Berton Reilly, M. Ed.

In 1923, the following quote appeared in the N. Y. Statesman:

We had the pleasure of examining this morning, the skull of the celebrated Indian chief *Walk-in-the-Water* Which was lately brought from Michigan, and is on its way to Philadelphia, to be examined by the phrenologists of that city. It is large and prominent but exhibits no very striking peculiarities. We shall probably have a particular description of it from some of the adepts in craniology.

This article exemplified the attitude at the time among scientists concerning the so-called racial differences found in examining skulls. Craniology (as it was known), was pseudoscience asserting that by examining skulls of Indigenous peoples, they can postulate how intelligent the Indigenous peoples were. Fortunately, with the advent of the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (NAGPRA) , skulls are no longer collected, and the pseudoscience of craniology is gone. Since 1990, NAGPRA is a system whereby any Indigenous remains found are returned to the rightful tribe or nation. However, it is far from a perfect system. In fact, a tribe needs to be federally recognized first before such remains can be given to them.

Returning to the above quote, one such person under their microscope was Chief Myeerah. This "celebrated Indian chief" was Wyandot Chief Myeerah (English translation: Walk-In-The-Water). His skull was unceremoniously taken from his grave and sold to a scientist. Who was he and why was his skull sought after like some kind of exotic relic? What happened to the rest of his body? This article will address these questions and seek to flesh out who he was as both a man and a Wyandot chief.

There are a few things that are known about Chief Myeerah. He lived in Michigan. He lived in Monguagon, Michigan, (now Wyandotte, Michigan) and he was a Wyandot war chief for the Detroit region. It also appears that sometime in the early nineteenth-century, Chief Myeerah along with about 25 families moved to Monguagon from across the river in Canada. This area was filled with old growth forests, situated right off the Detroit River. They built cabins and farms and raised hogs along with crops.

However, the War of 1812 further disrupted their lives. Chef Myeerah did everything possible to protect his people and not become entangled in the War of 1812. He tried to remain neutral and friendly with both the

Americans and the British. Unfortunately, that did not work. He was forced to side with the British and fight. In fact, he gave an impassioned speech during the War of 1812 to the British, letting them know that his people did not want to go to war against the Americans1 . Understandably, he also did not want his people to lose their lands. Along with other Wyandot chiefs, Chief Myeerah sent a letter to the President of the United States, and also to Congress in February 1812 describing their concerns about their continuing loss of land.

In addition to his role in the War of 1812, there is information concerning his physical description. He was described as being a tall man for the times- ". . . a fine, commanding person, near six feet in eight and wellproportioned, and as straight as an arrow. He was mild and pleasant in his deportment." He was easy to get along with, calm, friendly, and tried to keep his people safe and out of the war.2 Historian Charles Buser described Chief Myeerah as always been well dressed, wearing clothing that was both Indigenous and European in style. This seemed to fit both the chief and the times.

Chief Myeerah was also friendly with several white generals, probably another way to protect his people. For instance, he was friends with American General William Orlando Butler, stating that he saw Butler many times in battle but would never kill him. In addition, Chief Myeerah also at least got along with American General William Hull. This was apparent because General Hull relayed at least one letter to Chief Myeerah from a fellow chief.

The chief was also known as being friendly towards other white people as well. One story which exemplifies Myeerah's relationship with white people was that of a young European woman named Marie. She had a powerful vision that er lover was dying in the battlefield across the river in Amherstburg, Canada. She then ran to Myeerah, begging him to take her to the battle to retrieve his body. It is mentioned in this narrative that Marie and her Scottish father (Angus Mackintosh), were close friends with Chief Myeerah, and she sought his counsel.

Along with white people, Chief Myeerah was also friendly with the famous Shawnee, Chief Blue Jacket. In fact, Chief Blue Jacket lived less than a mile from Myeerah in Monguagon. Not only were they neighbors, but they also signed multiple treaties together and fought together. Chief Myeerah's friendships are emblematic of the diplomatic relationships and friendships between the Wyandot and the Algonquian people, as well as the European settlers. The Wyandot are a people who have a long history of diplomacy. In fact, concerning Wyandot diplomacy, during the War of 1812, Shawnee Chief Blue

Jacket stated, "You know also, that our uncles [Wyandots] have always taken care of the great fire; they being the oldest nation".

However, there is much that we do not know about the chief, one of which is the location of his grave site and what happened to his remains. While researching Myeerah, I discovered that his body was missing in addition to his skull. When I found that a newspaper article written in 1835 discussing how his skull was taken to Philadelphia to be examined by so-called experts, I tried to find out what happened. Even the location of his body was in question. His head was originally taken by a German traveler, Dr. Strum. After he died, his estate sold it to Dr. Samuel George Morton. Dr. Morton, in turn, studied this skull to try and determine more about the indigenous people. According to the NAGPRA Coordinator at the University of Pennsylvania Museum of Archaeology and Anthropology, his skull was eventually (2018) repatriated to the Wyandotte Nation in Oklahoma, not the Wyandot of Anderdon Nation.

## **SEAS Project Takes Off**

## by Linda Filipek

The Seas Masters Project with the Wyandot of Anderdon Nation, International Wildlife Refuge, and the Metro Parks is now underway. Master's Projects are interdisciplinary experiences that enable SEAS master's students to develop solutions to pressing problems facing their real-world clients. This year Alyson Oostoyk and Dede Lawal, under the tutelage of Professor Joan Iverson Nassauer, have accepted our proposal to study the land issues caused by rising lake levels.(Prof. Nassauer was not able to attend the meeting).

During the meeting, the attendees went on a tour of Six Points led by Grand Chief Ted Roll where they viewed the property and learned of its history. Everyone was saddened by the amount of land lost due to the rising waters in the area and how it is affecting, not only the land but the wildlife in the area.

Dede Lawal and Alyson Oostoyk took soil samples for analysis while Camilla Lizurdia and Edward Waisanem documented the day creating a record of the work that is being done. We hope that this will help us to save our land and be able to implement the Master Plan that was created some time ago and making the best use of the earth given to us by the Creator.

Pictured above are Jesse Fletcher and Susan White of the International Wildlife Refuge, Michael Odette, our Second Chief, Prof. Rebecca Hardin, Camilla Lizurdia, Dede Lawal, Linda Filipek, Alyson Oostoyk, Edward Waisanem and Chief Ted Roll. Also attending but missing from the picture is our Treasurer, Susan Szachta and Secretary Danielle Lowler.

## **Making Maple Sugar**

## From the compilation by Mary Minnie

Recently a group, sponsored by the River Raisin National Battlefield Park spent some time learning how to tap trees and use the sap to make maple syrup and maple sugar. Mary tells us that a "Sugar Bush" is a collection of sugar maple trees ( Ninaatig(oog) growing In the same area used to produce maple sugar and maple syrup. This effort is a native tradition celebrating community, dedication, and cooperation that benefits all and lasts the year-round.

Did you know that there was no honey production on our continent before the Europeans brought honey bees here in the 1600's? Thus maple sugar was used for seasonings, medicinally and ceremoniously. Our ancestors good use of everything that was given to them and wasted nothing.

Tapping of the trees takes place over several weeks and does not end until the sap becomes cloudy, usually in early spring when the trees begin to bud, and nights become warmer.

The collected sap is boiled in large copper pots over an open fire. As moisture leaves the sap in the form of steam a bone is hung over the pot. This collects moisture and causes water to drip back into the pot, breaking up the bubbles and allowing the sap to boil without boiling over and spilling everywhere. The sap is boiling at 211 degrees Fahrenheit and could cause sever injury or even death to anyone who has it spilled on them accidentally.

## **Some Native Values**

Here are some things that make us different from other cultures:

**Giving:** The person respected in many Native cultures is the one who shares his wealth with others.

**Material Things:** Members of the tribe are often suspicious of people who collect many material possessions. Some tribes, the Sioux for instance, even hold celebrations and give most of their possessions to others as "love-gifts".

**Balance with Nature:** The earth is here to enjoy. If man accepts this world as it is and lives with it as he should, there will be no sickness or lack of food.

**Patience:** To have much patience and to wait is considered food. Hurry only causes mistakes and problems.
