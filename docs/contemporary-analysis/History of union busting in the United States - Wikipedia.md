## ![](_page_0_Picture_6.jpeg)

[Pinkerton guards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinkerton_National_Detective_Agency) escort [strikebreakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strikebreaker) in [Buchtel, Ohio,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buchtel,_Ohio) 1884.

The **history of** [union busting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_busting) **in the United States** dates back to the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution) in the 19th century. The Industrial Revolution produced a rapid expansion in factories and manufacturing capabilities. As workers moved from farms to factories, mines and other hard labor, they faced harsh working conditions such as long hours, low pay and health risks. Children and women worked in factories and generally received lower pay than men. The government did little to limit these conditions. Labor movements in the industrialized world developed and lobbied for better rights and safer conditions. Shaped by wars, depressions, government policies, judicial rulings, and global competition, the early years of the battleground between unions and management were adversarial and often identified with aggressive hostility. Contemporary [opposition to trade unions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opposition_to_trade_unions) known as [union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_busting) [busting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_busting) started in the 1940s, and continues to present challenges to the labor movement. Union busting is a term used by [labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labor_union) organizations and [trade unions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_unions) to describe the activities that may be undertaken by employers, their proxies, workers and in certain instances states and governments usually triggered by events such as [picketing,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picketing_(protest)) [card check,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Card_check) [worker organizing,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organizing_(management)) and [strike actions.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strike_actions) [\[1\]](#page-44-0) Labor legislation has changed the nature of union busting, as well as the organizing tactics that labor organizations commonly use.

# **Strikebreaking and union busting, 1870s– 1935**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=History_of_union_busting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=1)

October 4, 1870 job advertisement in [The Baltimore Sun:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Baltimore_Sun) "Union men need not apply".

Hiring agencies specialising in anti-union practices have been an option available to employers from the bloody strikes of the last quarter of the nineteenth century, until today[.\[2\]](#page-44-1)

Working with owner [John D. Rockefeller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_D._Rockefeller), [Charles Pratt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Pratt)'s [Astral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astral_Oil_Works) [Oil Works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astral_Oil_Works) in 1874 began to buy refineries in Brooklyn to decrease competition. Around this time, the [coopers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooper_(profession))' union opposed Pratt's efforts to cut back on certain manual operations, as they were the craftsmen who made the barrels that held the oil. Pratt [busted the union,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_busting) and his strategies for breaking up the organization were adopted by other refineries. [\[3\]](#page-44-2)

Creative methods of union busting have been around for a long time. In 1907, [Morris Friedman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morris_Friedman) reported that a Pinkerton agent who had infiltrated the [Western Federation of Miners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Federation_of_Miners) managed to gain control of a strike relief fund, and [attempted to exhaust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Mc Parland#Mc Parland_in_Colorado) [that union's treasury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Mc Parland#Mc Parland_in_Colorado) by awarding lavish benefits to strikers[.\[4\]](#page-44-3) However, many attacks against unions have used force of one sort or another, including police action, military force, or recruiting [goon squads.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goon_squad)

#### **Physical attacks against unions**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=History_of_union_busting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=2)

Unions such as the [Industrial Workers of the World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Workers_of_the_World) (IWW) were devastated by the [Palmer Raids,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palmer_Raids) carried out as part of the [First](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Red_Scare) [Red Scare.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Red_Scare) The [Everett Massacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everett_Massacre) (also known as Bloody Sunday) was an armed confrontation between local authorities and IWW members which took place in [Everett, Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everett,_Washington) on Sunday, November 5, 1916. Later, [communist-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist)led unions were isolated or destroyed and their activists purged with the assistance of other union organizations during the [Second Red](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Red_Scare) [Scare.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Red_Scare)

## ![](_page_2_Picture_7.jpeg)

## Artist's depiction of the Haymarket Square riot

In May 1886 the Knights of Labor were demonstrating in the [Haymarket Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haymarket_affair) in Chicago, demanding an [eight-hour day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight-hour_day) in all trades. When police arrived, an unknown person threw a bomb into the crowd, killing one person and injuring several others. "In a trial marked by prejudice and hysteria" a court sentenced seven [anarchists,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anarchists) six of them German-speaking, to death - with no evidence linking them to the bomb.[\[5\]](#page-45-0)

Strikes also took place that same month (May 1886) in other cities, including in Milwaukee, where seven people died when Wisconsin Governor [Jeremiah M. Rusk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremiah_Mc Lain_Rusk) ordered [state-militia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_militia) troops to fire upon thousands of striking workers who had marched to the Milwaukee Iron Works Rolling Mill in Bay View, on Milwaukee's south side.

In 1914 one of the most bitter labor conflicts in American history took place at a mining colony in Colorado called [Ludlow.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludlow_massacre) After workers went on strike in September 1913 with grievances ranging from requests for an eight-hour day to allegations of subjugation, Colorado governor [Elias Ammons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elias_Ammons) called in the [National Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorado_National_Guard) in October 1913. That winter, Guardsmen made 172 arrests[.\[a\]](#page-44-4)[\[6\]](#page-45-1)

The strikers began to fight back, killing four mine guards and firing into a separate camp where strikebreakers lived. When the body of a strikebreaker was found nearby, the National Guard's General [Chase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Chase_(doctor_and_soldier)) ordered the tent colony destroyed in retaliation[.\[6\]](#page-45-1)

"On Monday morning, April 20, two dynamite bombs were exploded, in the hills above Ludlow ... a signal for operations to begin. At 9 am a machine gun began firing into the tents [where strikers were living], and then others joined."[\[6\]](#page-45-1) One eyewitness reported: "The soldiers and mine guards tried to kill everybody; anything they saw move"[.\[6\]](#page-45-1) That night the National Guard rode down from the hills surrounding Ludlow and set fire to the tents. Twenty-six people, including two women and eleven children, were killed[.\[7\]](#page-45-2)

#### **Union busting with police and military force**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=History_of_union_busting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=3)

For approximately 150 years, union organizing efforts and strikes have been periodically opposed by police, security forces, [National Guard units,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_National_Guard) special police forces such as the [Coal and Iron Police,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_and_Iron_Police) and/or use of the [United States Army.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army) Significant incidents have included the [Haymarket Riot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haymarket_Riot) and the [Ludlow massacre.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludlow_massacre) The [Homestead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homestead_strike) struggle of 1892, the [Pullman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pullman_strike) walkout of 1894, and the [Colorado Labor Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorado_Labor_Wars) of 1903 are examples of unions destroyed or significantly damaged by the deployment of military force. In all three examples, a strike became the triggering event.

- **Pinkertons and militia at Homestead, 1892** – One of the first union busting agencies was the [Pinkerton National](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinkerton_National_Detective_Agency) [Detective Agency,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinkerton_National_Detective_Agency) which came to public attention as the result of a shooting war that broke out between strikers and three hundred Pinkerton agents during the [Homestead Strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homestead_Strike) of 1892. When the Pinkerton agents were withdrawn, state militia forces were deployed. The militia repulsed attacks on the [Carnegie Steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnegie_Steel_Company) plant, and prevented violence against strikebreakers crossing picket lines, causing a decisive defeat of the strike, and ended the power of the [Amalgamated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amalgamated_Association_of_Iron_and_Steel_Workers) [Association of Iron and Steel Workers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amalgamated_Association_of_Iron_and_Steel_Workers) at the Homestead plant.

- **Federal troops end the railroad blockades by the American Railway Union, 1894** – During the [Pullman Strike,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pullman_Strike) the [American Railway Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Railway_Union) (ARU), out of union solidarity, called out its members according to the principle of [industrial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_unionism) [unionism.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_unionism) Their actions in blocking the movement of railroad trains were illegal but successful, until twenty thousand federal troops were called out to ensure that trains carrying US mail could travel freely. Once the trains ran, the strike ended.
- **National Guard in the Colorado Labor Wars, 1903** The [Colorado National Guard,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorado_National_Guard) an employers' organization called the [Citizens' Alliance,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizens%27_Alliance) and the [Mine Owners' Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mine_Owners%27_Association) teamed together to eject the [Western Federation of Miners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Federation_of_Miners) from mining camps throughout Colorado during the [Colorado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorado_Labor_Wars) [Labor Wars.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorado_Labor_Wars)

#### **Strikebreaking by governmental injunction**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=History_of_union_busting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=4)

In May 1895, in the case of [In re Debs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_re_Debs), the [Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_the_United_States) unanimously upheld the right of the federal government to use [injunctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injunction) to end labor strikes.

The case stemmed from an injunction against [Eugene Debs,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_V._Debs) president of the American Railway Union, and other strike leaders during the Pullman Strike of 1894. President [Grover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grover_Cleveland) [Cleveland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grover_Cleveland) sided with the [Pullman Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pullman_Company) during the strike, and Cleveland's attorney general [Richard Olney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Olney) sought a court order to end the strike from federal judge [Peter S. Grosscup,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_S._Grosscup) whom he knew to hold anti-union sentiments. In July 1894, Grosscup issued an injunction, described as an "omnibus injunction" due to its wide scope, that forbade Debs and other union officials "from in any way or manner interfering with, hindering, obstructing or stopping" the railroads entering Chicago, as well as from communicating with their subordinates in the union. In July 1894, Debs and four other union leaders were arrested and charged with violating the injunction. In December 1894, US circuit court judge [William A.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Allen_Woods) [Woods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Allen_Woods) found Debs and the other union leaders to be in contempt of court for violating the injunction and sentenced them to prison terms ranging from three to six months.[\[8\]](#page-45-3)[\[9\]](#page-45-4)

The practice of strikebreaking by governmental injunction continued until the passage of the [Norris-La Guardia Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norris%E2%80%93La_Guardia_Act) in 1932, which prohibited federal courts from issuing injunctions against nonviolent labor disputes, including strikes, picketing, and boycotts by labor unions[.\[10\]](#page-45-5)

### **Anatomy of a corporate union buster**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=History_of_union_busting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=5)

[Corporations Auxiliary Company,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporations_Auxiliary_Company) a union buster during the first half of the 20th century, would tell employers,

Our man will come to your factory and get acquainted... If he finds little disposition to organize, he will not encourage organization, but will engineer things so as to keep organization out. If, however, there seems a disposition to organize he will become the leading spirit and pick out just the right men to join. Once the union is in the field its members can keep it from growing if they know how, and our man knows how. Meetings can be set far apart. A contract can at once be entered into with the employer, covering a long period, and made very easy in its terms. However, these tactics may not be good, and the union spirit may be so strong that a big organization cannot be prevented. In this case our man turns extremely radical. He asks for unreasonable things and keeps the union embroiled in trouble. If a strike comes, he will be the loudest man in the bunch, and will counsel violence and get somebody in trouble. The result will be that the union will be broken up[.\[11\]](#page-45-6)

In the period 1933 to 1936, Corporations Auxiliary Company had 499 corporate clients[.\[12\]](#page-45-7)

# **College students as strikebreakers in the Interborough Rapid Transit strike of 1905**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=History_of_union_busting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=6)

Following a [walk out](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walk_out) of subway workers, management of the trains [Interborough Rapid Transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interborough_Rapid_Transit_Company) in New York City appealed to university students to volunteer as motormen, conductors, ticket sellers and ticket choppers. Stephen Norword discusses the phenomenon of students as strikebreakers in early 20th Century North America: "Throughout the period between 1901 and 1923, college students represented a major, and often critically important source of strikebreakers in a wide range of industries and services. ... Collegians deliberately volunteered their services as strikebreakers and were the group least likely to be swayed by the pleas of strikers and their sympathizers that they were doing something wrong.["\[13\]](#page-45-8)

#### **Jack Whitehead, the first "King of Strike Breakers"**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=History_of_union_busting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=7)

There were a significant number of strikes during the 1890s and very early 1900s. Strikebreaking by recruiting massive numbers of replacement workers became a significant activity. Jack Whitehead saw opportunity in labor struggles; while other workers were attempting to organize unions, he walked away from his union to organize an army of strikebreakers. Whitehead was the first to be called "King of the Strike Breakers"; by deploying his private workforce during strikes of steelworkers in [Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh,_Pennsylvania) and [Birmingham,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham,_Alabama) [Alabama,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham,_Alabama) he became wealthy. By demonstrating how lucrative strikebreaking could be, Whitehead inspired a host of imitators. [\[14\]](#page-45-9)

#### **James Farley inherits the strikebreaker title**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=History_of_union_busting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=8)

After Whitehead, men like James A. Farley and [Pearl Bergoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_Bergoff) turned union busting into a substantial industry. Farley began his strikebreaking career in 1895, and opened a detective agency in [New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City) in 1902. In addition to detective work, Farley accepted industrial assignments, specializing in breaking strikes of streetcar drivers[.\[15\]](#page-46-0) Farley hired his men based in part upon courage and toughness, and in some strikes they openly carried firearms. They were paid more than the strikers had been. Farley was credited with a string of successful strikebreaking actions, employing hundreds, and sometimes thousands of strikebreakers. Farley was sometimes paid as much as three hundred thousand dollars for breaking a strike, and by 1914 he had taken in more than ten million dollars. Farley claimed that he had defeated thirty-five strikes in a row. But he suffered from tuberculosis, and as he faced death, he declared that he turned down the job of breaking a streetcar strike in Philadelphia because this time, "the strikers were in the right.["\[16\]](#page-46-1)

#### **Bergoff Brothers Strike Service and Labor Adjusters**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=History_of_union_busting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=9)

[Pearl Bergoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_Bergoff) also began his strikebreaking career in New York City, working as a [spotter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labor_spies) on the [Metropolitan Street Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Railways) in Manhattan. His job was to watch conductors, making certain that they recorded all of the fares that they accepted. In 1905 Bergoff started the Vigilant Detective Agency of [New York City.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City) Within two years his brothers joined the lucrative business, and the name was changed to the Bergoff Brothers Strike Service and Labor Adjusters. Bergoff's early strikebreaking actions were characterized by extreme violence. A 1907 strike of garbage cart drivers resulted in numerous confrontations between strikers and the strikebreakers, even when protected by police escorts. Strikers sometimes pelted the strikebreakers with rocks, bottles, and bricks launched from tenement rooftops[.\[17\]](#page-46-2)

## ![](_page_10_Picture_2.jpeg)

Anti-union cartoon in monthly magazine The American Employer depicting the [AFL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Federation_of_Labor) as a fly on the wheel, 1913

In 1909, [the Pressed Steel Car Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressed_Steel_Car_Company) at [Mc Kees Rocks,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mc Kees_Rocks,_Pennsylvania) [Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mc Kees_Rocks,_Pennsylvania) fired forty men, and eight thousand employees walked out under the banner of the [Industrial Workers of the](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Workers_of_the_World) [World.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Workers_of_the_World) Bergoff's agency hired strikebreaking toughs from the Bowery, and shipped vessels filled with unsuspecting immigrant workers directly into the strike zone. Other immigrant strikebreakers were delivered in boxcars, and were not fed during a two-day period. Later they worked, ate, and slept in a barn with two thousand other men. Their meals consisted of cabbage and bread[.\[18\]](#page-46-3)

There were violent confrontations between strikers and strikebreakers, but also between strikebreakers and guards when the terrified workers demanded the right to leave. An Austro-Hungarian immigrant who managed to escape told his government that workers were being held against their will, resulting in an international incident. In addition to kidnapping, strikebreakers complained of deception, broken promises about wages, and tainted food[.\[19\]](#page-46-4)

During federal hearings, Bergoff explained that "musclemen" under his employ would "get... any graft that goes on", suggesting that was to be expected "on every big job".[\[19\]](#page-46-4) Other testimony indicated that Bergoff's "right-hand man", described as "huge in stature, weighing perhaps 240 pounds", surrounded himself with thirty-five guards who intimidated and fleeced the strikebreakers, locking them into a boxcar prison with no sanitation facilities when they defied orders.[\[20\]](#page-46-5)

At the end of August a gun battle erupted, leaving six dead, six dying, and fifty wounded. Public sympathy began to swing away from the company, and toward the strikers. Early in September the company acknowledged defeat and negotiated with the strikers. Twenty-two had died in the strike. But Bergoff's business was not hurt by the defeat; he boasted of having as many as ten thousand strikebreakers on his payroll. [\[20\]](#page-46-5) He was getting paid as much as two million dollars per strikebreaking job[.\[21\]](#page-46-6)

#### **Anti-union vigilantes during the First Red Scare**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=History_of_union_busting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=10)

Unlike the [American Federation of Labor,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Federation_of_Labor) the [Industrial Workers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Workers_of_the_World) [of the World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Workers_of_the_World) opposed the First World War. The [American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Protective_League) [Protective League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Protective_League) (APL) was a pro-war organization formed by wealthy Chicago businessmen. At the height of its power the APL had 250,000 members in 600 cities. In 1918, documents from the APL showed that ten percent of its efforts (the largest of any category) were focused on disrupting the activities of the IWW. The APL burgled and vandalized IWW offices, and harassed IWW members. Such actions were illegal, yet were supported by the [Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodrow_Wilson) administration[.\[22\]](#page-46-7)

#### **Spies, "missionaries", and saboteurs**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=History_of_union_busting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=11)

## ![](_page_12_Picture_5.jpeg)

Anti-union cartoon in monthly magazine The American Employer depicting the [AFL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Federation_of_Labor) as a cannon aimed at a government building, 1914

Strikebreaking by hiring massive numbers of tough opportunists began to lose favor in the 1920s; there were fewer strikes, resulting in fewer opportunities[.\[20\]](#page-46-5)[\[23\]](#page-47-0) By the 1930s, agencies began to rely more upon the use of [informants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labor_spies) [and labor spies.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labor_spies)

Spy agencies hired to bust unions developed a level of sophistication that could devastate targets. "Missionaries" were undercover operatives trained to use whispering campaigns or unfounded rumors to create dissension on the picket lines and in union halls. The strikers themselves were not the only targets. For example, female missionaries might systematically visit the strikers' wives in the home, relating a sob story of how a strike had destroyed their own families. Missionary campaigns have been known to destroy not only strikes, but unions themselves[.\[24\]](#page-47-1)

In the 1930s, the Pinkerton Agency employed twelve hundred labor spies, and nearly one-third of them held high level positions in the targeted unions. The [International Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Association_of_Machinists_and_Aerospace_Workers) [of Machinists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Association_of_Machinists_and_Aerospace_Workers) was damaged when Sam Brady, a veteran Pinkerton operative, held a high enough position in that union that he was able to precipitate a premature strike. All but five officers in a [United Auto Workers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Auto_Workers) local in [Lansing, Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lansing,_Michigan) were driven out by Pinkerton agents. The five who remained were Pinkertons. At the Underwood Elliott-Fisher Company plant, the union local was so badly injured by undercover operatives that membership dropped from more than twenty five hundred to fewer than seventy-five[.\[25\]](#page-47-2)

#### **General strikebreaking methods**

### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=History_of_union_busting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=12)

During the period from roughly 1910 to 1914, [Robert Hoxie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Hoxie) compiled a list of methods used by employers' associations to attack unions. The list was published in 1921, as part of the book Trade Unionism in the United States. These methods include counter organization, inducing union leaders to support management, supporting other pro-business enterprises, refusing to work with pro-union enterprises, obtaining information on unions among others[.\[26\]](#page-47-3)

Hoxie summarized the underlying theories, assumptions, and attitudes of employers' associations of the period. According to Hoxie, these included the supposition that employers' interests are always identical to society's interests, such that unions should be condemned when they interfere; that the employers' interests are always harmonious with the workers' interests, and unions therefore try to mislead workers; that workers should be grateful to employers, and are therefore ungrateful and immoral when they join unions; that the business is solely the employer's to manage; that unions are operated by nonemployees, and they are therefore necessarily outsiders; that unions restrict the right of employees to work when, where, and how they wish; and that the law, the courts, and the police represent absolute and impartial rights and justice, and therefore unions are to be condemned when they violate the law or oppose the police[.\[27\]](#page-47-4)

Given the proliferation of employers' associations created primarily for the purpose of opposing unions, Hoxie poses counter-questions. For example, if every employer has a right to manage his own business without interference from outside workers, then why hasn't a group of workers at a particular company the right to manage their own affairs without interference from outside employers[?\[28\]](#page-47-5)

## **Strikebreaking and union busting, 1936–1947**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=History_of_union_busting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=13)

Shortly after the landslide victory of Franklin D. Roosevelt in the [presidential election of 1936,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1936_United_States_presidential_election) the Supreme Court in April 1937 handed down a decision in which it concurred with the findings of the [National Labor Relations Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Labor_Relations_Board) (NLRB) that the [Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jones_and_Laughlin_Steel_Company) [and Laughlin Steel Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jones_and_Laughlin_Steel_Company) had engaged in unfair labor practices, including firing workers "because of their union activity and for the purpose of discouraging membership in the union," and upheld the NLRB's order that the company offer reinstatement with backpay to ten of the fired union workers at the company's steelworks in [Aliquippa,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aliquippa,_Pennsylvania) Pennsylvania, and post notices that the company would not fire or discriminate against union members or those seeking to join the union.[\[29\]](#page-47-6)

Later in 1937, during the [Little Steel strike,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Steel_strike) approximately 8,000 striking steel workers were illegally fired from their jobs, more than 1,000 striking workers were arrested, and between 16 and 18 strikers were killed over the course of the strike, including 10 workers who were killed in an incident on Memorial Day that came to be known as the [Memorial Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1937_Memorial_Day_massacre) [massacre of 1937.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1937_Memorial_Day_massacre) [\[30\]](#page-47-7)

Employers in the United States have had the legal right to permanently replace economic strikers since the Supreme Court's 1937 decision in [NLRB v. Mackay Radio & Telegraph Co.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NLRB_v._Mackay_Radio_%26_Telegraph_Co.) [\[31\]](#page-48-0)

Meanwhile, employers began to demand more subtle and sophisticated union busting tactics, and so the field called "preventive labor relations" was born[.\[32\]](#page-48-1) The new practitioners were armed with degrees in industrial psychology, management, and labor law. They would use these skills not only to manipulate the provisions of national labor law, but also the emotions of workers seeking to unionize.

# **Nathan Shefferman (Labor Relations Associates), 1940s–1950s**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=History_of_union_busting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=14)

After passage of the [Wagner Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wagner_Act) in 1935, the first nationally known union busting agency was Labor Relations Associates of Chicago, Inc. (LRA) founded in 1939 by Nathan Shefferman, who later in 1961 wrote The Man in the Middle, [a guide to union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_busting#Guide_to_modern_union_busting) [busting,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_busting#Guide_to_modern_union_busting) and has been considered the 'founding father' of the modern union avoidance industry[.\[31\]](#page-48-0) Shefferman had been a member of the original NLRB, and became director of employee relations at Chicago-based [Sears, Roebuck and](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sears,_Roebuck_and_Company) [Company.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sears,_Roebuck_and_Company) Sears had been engaged in blocking unions from the AFL Retail Clerks union throughout the 1930s. Sears provided \$10,000 seed money to launch LRA[.\[34\]](#page-48-3) In 1957 during hearings conducted by the United States, Congress, and Select Committees on Improper Activities in the Labor and Management Field[.\[35\]](#page-48-4) Nate Shefferman was questioned by Robert Kennedy and testimony revealed that [Teamsters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Brotherhood_of_Teamsters) union "top brass" were regularly sent to meet with him. An article written by Victor Riesel for Inside Labor on May 28, 1957[\[36\]](#page-48-5) reveals that Dave Beck, President of the Teamster's Union in that era, worked closely with Nate Shefferman on many deals not the least of which may have been his influence at Sears to discourage employees from joining the AFL Retail Clerks union which was trying to raid the Teamsters membership to join them instead. An indicator of the close relationship between the Teamster's President David Beck and Shefferman (excerpted from the article): "Beck dispatched Shefferman to

Jim Hoffa last year (1956) to offer Hoffa the union's presidency if Hoffa would first help re-elect Beck and then wait 6 months for Beck to resign on grounds of ill health". The article asks "Why did Beck and the multi-million dollar Shefferman work so closely—and on what?["\[36\]](#page-48-5)

By the late 1940s, LRA had nearly 400 clients. Shefferman's operatives set up anti-union employee groups called "Vote No" committees, developed ruses to identify pro-union workers, and helped arrange sweetheart contracts with unions that would not challenge management[.\[37\]](#page-48-6) Consultants from LRA "committed numerous illegal actions, including bribery, coercion of employees and racketeering.["\[31\]](#page-48-0)

Shefferman built "a daunting business on a foundation of false premises", of which "perhaps the most incredible—and most widely believed—is the myth that companies are at a disadvantage to unions organizationally, legally, and financially during a union-organizing drive." What businesses sought to accomplish through such propaganda was for Congress to amend the [Wagner Act.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Labor_Relations_Act) [\[38\]](#page-48-7)

One of Shefferman's associates defined his technique simply by saying: "We operate the exact way a union does," he said. "But on management's side. We give out leaflets, talk to employees, and organize a propaganda campaign."[\[39\]](#page-48-8)

From 1949 through 1956, LRA earned nearly \$2.5 million providing such anti-union services.[\[40\]](#page-49-0)

In 1957, the [United States Senate Select Committee on](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Senate_Select_Committee_on_Improper_Activities_in_Labor_and_Management) [Improper Activities in Labor and Management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Senate_Select_Committee_on_Improper_Activities_in_Labor_and_Management) (also known as the Mc Clellan Committee) investigated unions for corruption, and employers and agencies for union busting activities. Labor Relations Associates was found to have committed violations of the [National Labor Relations Act of 1935,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Labor_Relations_Act_of_1935) including manipulating union elections through bribery and coercion, threatening to revoke workers' benefits if they organized, installing union officers who were sympathetic to management, rewarding employees who worked against the union, and spying on and harassing workers.[\[41\]](#page-49-1) The Mc Clellan Committee believed that "the National Labor Relations Board [was] impotent to deal with Shefferman's type of activity."[\[42\]](#page-49-2) In 1959, the state of [North Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Carolina) passed a law banning [collective bargaining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collective_bargaining) by all government workers[.\[43\]](#page-49-3)

### **Captive audience techniques**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=History_of_union_busting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=18)

Common legal tactics used by employers to prevent unionization include forcing employees to attend anti-union meetings, known as [captive audience meetings,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Captive_audience_meeting) where prounion workers are unable to present alternative views; covering the workplace with anti-union posters, banners, or videos; instructing managers to tell workers that they may lose their jobs if they vote to unionize; and having managers hold one-on-one meetings with workers to argue it would be bad for them to vote for a union[.\[61\]](#page-51-2) Employers' messages frequently focus on themes such as the idea that unions will drive employers out of business, that unions only care about extracting dues payments from workers, and that unionization is unnecessary or futile[.\[61\]](#page-51-2) Overall, US employers spend more than \$400 million per year on union-avoidance consultants to help them prevent employees from forming unions[.\[62\]](#page-51-3) As of July 2024, by state law, workers are allowed to voluntarily leave anti-union meetings without employer retaliation in Illinois, Washington State, Oregon, Minnesota, Maine, Connecticut, and New York[.\[63\]](#page-51-4)[\[64\]](#page-51-5) "Captive audience bans" can be broad - applying in some states to any political or religious topic - and have been challenged in court on the grounds they violate the [Free Speech Clause](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Speech_Clause) of the federal constitution or that they are pre-empted by federal law.[\[64\]](#page-51-5) In November 2024, the NLRB ruled that captive audience meeting were illegal throughout the United States[.\[65\]](#page-52-0)[\[66\]](#page-52-1)

#### **Anti-union corporate training materials**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=History_of_union_busting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=19)

A number of US corporations have also produced anti-union training materials that have been disseminated to managers or other employees.

In 2018, Amazon distributed a 45-minute union-busting training video to managers at [Whole Foods,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whole_Foods_Market) which it had acquired in 2017, instructing managers to look for warning signs that might indicate "vulnerability to organizing," including use of the term "living wage" and workers showing an "unusual interest in policies, benefits, employee lists, or other company information," and to immediately escalate potential signs of organizing to general managers.[\[67\]](#page-52-2)

In January 2022, Target emailed store managers at the company new training documents, instructing managers to look for warning signs of union organizing within their stores, including conversations among workers regarding pay, benefits, job security, or other grievances, and to coordinate with human resources to prevent unionization[.\[68\]](#page-52-3)

# **Accusations and complaints by workers of unfair labor practices**

In July 2015, the [International Association of Machinists and](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Association_of_Machinists_and_Aerospace_Workers) [Aerospace Workers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Association_of_Machinists_and_Aerospace_Workers) union filed a complaint with the NLRB against [Amazon,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_(company)) alleging that the company engaged in [unfair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unfair_labor_practice) [labor practices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unfair_labor_practice) by surveilling, threatening, and "informing employees that it would be futile to vote for union representation" during a during a union drive in 2014 and 2015 at an Amazon warehouse in [Chester,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chester,_Virginia) Virginia[.\[71\]](#page-52-6) In 2016, Amazon settled the complaint with the NLRB, denying any wrongdoing but agreeing to post a list at the warehouse of 22 forms of union-busting behavior that the company promised not to engage in, including threatening workers with the loss of a job or other reprisals if they were union supporters, interrogating workers about the union, or engaging in surveillance of workers while they participated in union activities[.\[71\]](#page-52-6) In May 2024, workers at an Amazon warehouse in [St. Peters,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Peters,_Missouri) Missouri filed an unfair labor practice charge against the company with the NLRB, accusing the company of using "intrusive algorithms" as part of a surveillance program to deter union organizing at the warehouse[.\[72\]](#page-52-7) In June 2024, a group of 104 delivery drivers at Amazon's DIL7 facility in

#### **Formal charges and rulings by the NLRB**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=History_of_union_busting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=21)

Multiple accusations have led to formal charges and rulings by the NLRB against US corporations for engaging in illegal antiunion practices.

In May 2012, an NLRB judge overturned the results of a unionization election at a Target store in Valley Stream, New York, and ordered a new vote, finding that Target managers had illegally barred employees from wearing union buttons and distributing fliers, threatened to discipline employees who discussed unionization, and threatened to close the store if the workers voted to unionize[.\[104\]](#page-57-4)

In April 2019, an NLRB judge found that Lowe's Home Centers violated the National Labor Relations Act by forbidding workers from discussing their pay[.\[105\]](#page-57-5)

In May 2019, following a complaint filed by the [United](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Steelworkers) [Steelworkers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Steelworkers) in November 2017, an NLRB judge found [Kumho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kumho_Tire)

[Tire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kumho_Tire) had engaged in "pervasive" illegal conduct during a unionization campaign at the company's tire manufacturing plant in Macon, Georgia, with at least 12 managers including the company's CEO issuing illegal threats to lay off employees or to close the plant if it unionized[.\[106\]](#page-58-0)[\[107\]](#page-58-1)

In December 2019, the office of NLRB general counsel [Peter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_B._Robb) [Robb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_B._Robb) filed a complaint against [Chipotle,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chipotle_Mexican_Grill) accusing the company of firing a worker in New York City in retaliation for trying to organize a union, as well as alleging that a manager threatened to fire workers if they engaged in protected union activities, implying they could face physical violence as a result[.\[108\]](#page-58-2) In November 2022, the NLRB accused Chipotle of illegally closing a store in [Augusta,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augusta,_Maine) Maine where workers were in the process of forming a union[.\[109\]](#page-58-3)

In March 2021, the NLRB ruled that Tesla violated labor law when it fired a union organizer, Richard Ortiz, and when the company's CEO, Elon Musk, posted a tweet that was viewed as threatening to labor organizers within the company, and it ordered Tesla to have Musk delete the anti-union tweet and to reinstate Ortiz and to compensate him for the loss of earnings. [\[110\]](#page-58-4) In April 2022, the NLRB ruled that Tesla's dress code, which prohibited workers from wearing union logos or insignia, was unlawful[,\[111\]](#page-58-5) although in November 2023 the [United](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Court_of_Appeals_for_the_Fifth_Circuit) [States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Court_of_Appeals_for_the_Fifth_Circuit) overturned the NLRB's ruling[.\[112\]](#page-58-6) In April 2023, the NLRB ruled that Tesla violated labor law by prohibiting workers at an Orlando, Florida service center from discussing pay or raising grievances about working conditions with upper management[.\[113\]](#page-59-0) In May 2024, an official with the NLRB filed a complaint claiming that Tesla's workplace rule barring workers from using workplace technology for the purposes of "unauthorized solicitating [sic] or promoting" or "creating channels and distribution lists" violated US labor law by illegally discouraging workers at an assembly plant in Buffalo, New York from union organizing.[\[114\]](#page-59-1) [\[115\]](#page-59-2)

In November 2021, a regional director for the NLRB found that Amazon had violated labor laws at a warehouse in [Bessemer,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessemer,_Alabama) Alabama, including by interrogating, surveilling, and threatening pro-union workers, as well as by setting "overly broad" restrictions on workers' access to the warehouse, during a union drive at the warehouse by the [Retail, Wholesale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retail,_Wholesale_and_Department_Store_Union) [and Department Store Union.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retail,_Wholesale_and_Department_Store_Union) [\[116\]](#page-59-3) In May 2022 the NLRB accused Amazon CEO [Andy Jassy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andy_Jassy) of violating federal labor law during media interviews in 2022 where he said workers are better off without a union[,\[117\]](#page-59-4) and in May 2024 an NLRB judge ruled that Jassy's comments had indeed violated the law.[\[118\]](#page-59-5) In December 2023, the NLRB found that Amazon had illegally interrogated warehouse workers in Staten Island, New York, as well prohibiting workers from handing out union literature and confiscating union literature from workers[.\[119\]](#page-59-6) Overall, as of September 2023, 222 open or settled unfair labor practice charges had been filed against Amazon in 25 states.[\[120\]](#page-59-7) In December 2021, an administrative judge with the NLRB found that [Cemex Construction Materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cemex) had committed more than two dozen unfair labor practices, including threatening, surveilling, and interrogating workers, as well as hiring security guards to intimidate workers in the lead up to a 2019 Teamsters Silk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joy_Silk) doctrine[.\[121\]](#page-60-0)[\[122\]](#page-60-1)

In January 2022, the NLRB filed a complaint against The New York Times, alleging that the company illegally prevented workers designated as "intern managers" from showing support for a recently formed union of tech workers at the company[.\[123\]](#page-60-2)

As of February 2023, the NLRB had brought 75 complaints against Starbucks accusing it of more than 1,000 illegal actions[.\[69\]](#page-52-4) In August 2022, the NRLB ordered [Starbucks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starbucks) to reinstate seven employees who were allegedly fired for supporting a [Starbucks union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starbucks_unions) campaign at a store in Memphis, Tennessee[.\[124\]](#page-60-3) The NLRB was granted an injunction to this effect by US District Judge [Sheryl Lipman,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheryl_H._Lipman) which was upheld by the [Sixth Circuit Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Court_of_Appeals_for_the_Sixth_Circuit) in 2023, though the injunction was later overturned by the Supreme Court in June 2024, in a decision that requires federal courts to apply a stricter standard to similar cases going forward[.\[125\]](#page-60-4)

In December 2022, the NLRB ruled that [Apple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_Inc.) illegally interrogated and made coercive statements to retail store workers in Atlanta, Georgia during a union drive.[\[126\]](#page-60-5) In

November 2023, the NLRB filed a complaint against Apple over its practices at a retail store in Towson, Maryland, accusing the company of offering new benefits to non-unionized workers while denying the same benefits to unionized workers at the Towson store with the aim of "discouraging" other employees from unionizing[.\[127\]](#page-60-6) In May 2024, the NLRB ruled that Apple illegally questioned workers about union matters and confiscated union flyers at its World Trade Center store in New York City in 2022, affirming the findings of an administrative judge in 2023[.\[128\]](#page-61-0)

# **History of labor legislation**

[\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=History_of_union_busting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=24)

#### **Railway Labor Act, 1926**

[\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=History_of_union_busting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=25)

The [Railway Labor Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railway_Labor_Act)[\[152\]](#page-64-4)[\[153\]](#page-64-5) (RLA) of 1926 was the first major piece of [labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labor_relations) legislation passed by Congress. The RLA was amended in 1936 to expand from railroads and cover the emerging airline industry. At UPS, the mechanics, dispatchers, and pilots are the labor groups that are covered by the RLA. It was enacted because Railroad management wanted to keep the trains moving by putting an end to "wildcat" strikes. Railroad workers wanted to make sure they had an opportunity to organize, be recognized as the exclusive bargaining agent in dealing with a company, negotiate new agreements and enforce existing ones. Under the RLA, agreements do not have expiration dates; instead they have amendable dates which are indicated within the agreement.

The National Labor Relations Act (NLRA)[,\[154\]](#page-64-6) often referred to as the Wagner Act, was passed by Congress July 5, 1935. It established the right to organize unions. The Wagner Act was the most important labor law in American history and earned the nickname "labor's bill of rights". It forbade employers from engaging in five types of labor practices: interfering with or restraining employees exercising their right to organize and bargain collectively; attempting to dominate or influence a labor union; refusing to bargain collectively and in "good faith" with unions representing their employees; and, finally, encouraging or discouraging union membership through any special conditions of employment or through discrimination against union or non-union members in hiring. Before the law, employers had liberty to spy upon, question, punish, blacklist, and fire union members. In the 1930s workers began to organize in large numbers. A great wave of work stoppages in 1933 and 1934 included citywide general strikes and [factory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupation_of_factories) [occupations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupation_of_factories) by workers. Hostile skirmishes erupted between workers bent on organizing unions, and the police and hired security squads backing the interests of factory owners who opposed unions. Some historians maintain that Congress enacted the NLRA primarily to help stave off even more serious —potentially revolutionary—labor unrest. Arriving at a time when organized labor had nearly lost faith in Roosevelt, the Wagner Act required employers to acknowledge labor unions that were favored by a majority of their work forces. The Act established the National Labor Relations Board, with oversight over union elections and unfair labor practices by employers. [\[155\]](#page-64-7)

#### **Taft–Hartley Act, 1947**

### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=History_of_union_busting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=27)

The [Taft–Hartley Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taft%E2%80%93Hartley_Act)[\[156\]](#page-64-8) was a major revision of the National Labor Relations Act of 1935 (the Wagner Act) and represented the first major revision of a New Deal act passed by a post-war Congress. In the mid-term elections of 1946, the Republican Party gained majorities in both houses for the first time since

1931. With the Truman administration initially taking no stand on the bill, it passed both houses with strong bipartisan support. In addition to overwhelming Republican support, a clear majority of House Democrats voted for the bill, while Democrats in the Senate split evenly, 21–21[.\[157\]](#page-65-0)

The Taft–Hartley Act was vehemently denounced by union officials, who dubbed it a "slave labor" bill. Truman vetoed the bill with a strong message to Congress, but despite Truman's all-out effort to stop the veto override, On June 23, 1947, Congress overrode his veto with considerable Democratic support, including 106 out of 177 Democrats in the House, and 20 out of 42 Democrats in the Senate[.\[158\]](#page-65-1) However, twentyeight Democratic members of Congress declared it a "new guarantee of [industrial slavery"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_slave).

Management always had the upper hand, of course; they had never lost it. But thanks to Taft–Hartley, the bosses could once again wage their war with near impunity.

Taft–Hartley gave the National Labor Relations Board the power to act against unions engaged in unfair labor practices; previously, the board could only consider unfair practices by employers. It defined specific employer rights which broadened an employer's options during union organizing drives. It banned the closed shop, in which union membership is a precondition of employment at an organized workplace. It allowed state ["right to work"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-to-work_law) laws which prohibit mandatory union dues.

The act required union officials to swear that they were not communists. This provision was overturned by the Supreme

# Court in 1965[.\[159\]](#page-65-2)

The act gave the president the power to petition the courts to end a strike if it constitutes a national emergency. Presidents have invoked the Taft–Hartley Act thirty-five times to halt work stoppages in labor disputes; almost all of the instances took place in the late 1940s, 1950s and 1960s, under presidents Truman, Eisenhower, Kennedy, and Johnson, after which the provision fell into disuse. The last two times the emergency provision was invoked were in 1978 by Jimmy Carter and 2002 by George W. Bush.

#### **Landrum–Griffin Act, 1959**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=History_of_union_busting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=28)

The Landrum–Griffin Act of 1959 is also known as the [Labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labor_Management_Reporting_and_Disclosure_Act) [Management Reporting and Disclosure Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labor_Management_Reporting_and_Disclosure_Act) (LMRDA[\)\[160\]](#page-65-3) defined financial reporting requirements for both unions and management organizations. Pursuant to LMRDA Section 203(b) employers are required to disclose the costs of any persuader activity as it regards consultants and potential bargaining unit employees[.\[161\]](#page-65-4)

#### [Martin J. Levitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_J._Levitt)'s interpretation is as follows:

The law regulates labor unions' internal affairs and union officials' relationships with employers. But the law also required companies to report certain expenditures related to their anti-union activities. Fortunately for union busters, loopholes in the requirements allow management and their agents to ignore the provisions aimed at reforming their behavior[.\[162\]](#page-65-5) The loopholes require consultants to file if they communicate with employees either for the purpose of persuading them not to join a union, or to gain knowledge about the employees or the union that may be passed on to the employer. However, most consultants accomplish these goals by indirect means, using supervisors and management as their first line of contact with employees. Even before the Act was passed, labor consultants had identified front-line supervisors as the most effective lobbyists for management. [\[163\]](#page-65-6)

Landrum–Griffin also seeks to prevent consultants from [spying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labor_spying_in_the_United_States) [on employees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labor_spying_in_the_United_States) or the union. Information is not to be compiled unless it is for the purpose of a specific legal proceeding. According to Martin Levitt, "It is easy for consultants to use this provision as a cover for "all kinds of information gathering". [\[163\]](#page-65-6)

According to Levitt, "because of Landrum–Griffin's vague language, attorneys are able to directly interfere in the unionorganizing process without any reporting requirements. Therefore, "young lawyers run bold anti-union wars and dance all over Landrum–Griffin." The provisions of Landrum–Griffin allowing special rights for lawyers resulted in labor consultants working under the shield of labor attorneys, allowing them to easily evade the intent of the law.["\[164\]](#page-65-7)

#### Levitt stated:

With the help of our trusted attorneys, our anti-union activities were carried out [under Landrum-Griffin] in backstage secrecy;

meanwhile we gleefully showcased every detail of union finances that could be twisted into implications of impropriety or incompetence.

- —Martin Jay Levitt, 1993, Confessions of a Union Buster[\[163\]](#page-65-6)
