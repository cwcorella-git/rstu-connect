# **"We Know This Place": Neoliberal Racial Regimes and the Katrina Circumstance**

## *Jordan T. Camp*

And we know this place, for we have seen more times than we'd like to imagine bloated cadavers floating through waters of a city gone savage, foraging the land for what can be salvaged. But what can be saved when all is lost? It happened in August, twenty-nine days in. We are now five days out of the only place we knew to call house and home. Few things are certain: one, we have no food; two, there are more bodies lying at the roadside than hot plates being distributed or first aid being administered or recognition as a citizen. Fourteenth Amendment, X, refugee, check. And we know this place. It's ever-changing yet forever the same: Money and power and greed, the game. —Sunni Patterson, "We Know This Place"

Progressive social movements do not simply produce statistics and narratives of oppression; rather, the best ones do what great poetry always does: transport us to another place, compel us to relive horrors and, more importantly, enable us to imagine a new society. We must remember that the conditions and the very existence of social movements enable participants to imagine something different, to realize that things need not always be this way. It is *that* imagination, that effort to see the future in the present, that I shall call "poetry" or "poetic knowledge."

## —Robin D. G. Kelley, *Freedom Dreams*

I n the wake of Hurricane Katrina, Black New Orleans was represented as a symbol of disorder as the major network news broadcasts focused attention on one of the poorest cities in the poorest region in the country, a city where Blacks counted for 67 percent of the city's population before the storm.1 Mass mediated cultural outlets manufactured images, narratives, and imaginaries of the Black poor as either hapless victims or as the source of the social problems they endured.2

The narrative of criminality that emerged in the wake of Katrina provides the opportunity to interpret and explain the logic, institutions, and social forces that produce what Ruth Wilson Gilmore conceptualizes as "fatal couplings of power and difference."3 I introduce the concept "neoliberal racial regimes of security" to explain how the Katrina circumstance fits into a predictable pattern of criminalizing the racialized poor because of roles played by privatization and the warfare racial state in neoliberal globalization.4 The security responses to the event need to be seen alongside the capitalist interests, the racialized structures of meaning, and relations of force that organize the Gulf Coast region.5 My claim is that the mass-mediated discourses of counterinsurgency and security are expressions of a moral panic that relies on ideological constructions of race and crime condensed in images of "looters" disrupting the liberal social contract.6 Racial domination pervades discourses of security.7 Because these ideas, images, and discourses have material causes and consequences, I argue that scholar-activists should engage directly with the voices of besieged communities to reinvigorate languages of struggle.8

In New Orleans, artists, activists, and theorists at the grassroots draw on collective memories of past social conflicts to challenge the hypervisibility of racialized and gendered poverty, violence, and misery. At the same time, they contest the invisibility of what I am calling a neoliberal racial regime of security.9 Prominent among these is poet, teacher, singer, and Black grassroots activist Sunni Patterson. In sharp contrast to the discourses that helped implement, explain, and legitimate the repressive responses to the event, Patterson brings us into a different scene by articulating what Robin D. G. Kelley calls "poetic knowledge" through her music and poetry.10 Her words counter the ideological obfuscations about race and power in hegemonic political and mass-mediated discourses, "it's ever-changing yet forever the same."11In her "poetics of struggle," the aggrieved folk demonized as actual or potential looters are represented as oppressed people struggling for freedom.12

Sunni Patterson had lived most of her life in New Orleans's Ninth Ward and the community of Algiers before being one of the two hundred fifty thousand people (most of them Black) displaced after the storm. Patterson and her family were forced to migrate to Houston, where she lived for a year before returning to the city. She explains that there is a creative link between her art and where she grew up: "I know and understand the type of beat . . . they want to hear . . . and it is a beat I like too because I come from these same streets."13 In addition to her role as a politically engaged artist, she has worked as a public school teacher in the city. In her course Community Activism Through Creative Writing, Patterson introduced hip-hop and popular fiction as pedagogical tools to facilitate the study of expressive culture and social movements. Perhaps most critically, she provided materials to students that show how youth have always played central roles in freedom struggles.14 In 2008, Patterson was forced to move back to Houston after being evicted from her apartment in the wake of hurricane Gustav, but she longs to return and "sit by the river in New Orleans."15

Through her music, poetry, performance, and teaching, Patterson works to elaborate the liberationist trajectory she encountered through Afro-diasporic expressive culture, social movements, and radical social theory. She cites as critical influences Black musicians, creative writers, and critical theorists including Amiri Baraka, Gwendolyn Brooks, Octavia Butler, Frantz Fanon, Aretha Franklin, Fela Kuti, C. L. R. James, and Nina Simone.16 She taps this radical tradition to cultivate the struggle for a new society based on the knowledge found in the expressive cultures of aggrieved communities.17 She is immersed in expressive and political cultures that articulate Black and oppressed people's struggles within and against "racial capitalism."18 This is powerfully evidenced in her works *Porch Prophesies* and *Ofo Ase / Words of Power*, but also through the circulation of her poetics in multiple venues, including a broadcast of *Democracy Now!* on the second anniversary of Katrina and appearances on HBO's *Def Poetry Jam* and at the Panafest in Ghana. In 2006, Patterson was an emcee for a historic event organized by more than a hundred organizations to commemorate the year of struggle for the right to return and reconstruct the city. The march of thousands of Katrina survivors from across the country began where the levees broke in the Lower Ninth Ward and ended in Congo Square, a symbolic epicenter for self-active resistance, freedom dreams, and Afro-diasporic expressive culture.19

Patterson's "highly poetic politics" call up an archive of popular memory inside aggrieved communities who *know this place*. 20 Her poetry and music reveal how the historical struggle for new social relations waged by Black and poor people in the post-Katrina Gulf Coast is also being taken up on the terrain of knowledge production and expressive culture.21 By providing countermemories of events, reclaiming the past, and articulating new ways of seeing power, her work demonstrates the significance of poetics in social movements.22 Patterson sees her work as representing the antiracist principles of contemporary abolitionists.23

Patterson conceived the poem "We Know This Place" as an ethical agreement with oppressed ancestors to continue the struggle for freedom. The poem draws a direct link between slavery and the debacle in the wake of Katrina by invoking the memory of the Middle Passage: "For we have glanced more times than we'd like to share / into eyes that stare with nothing there / behind them but an unfulfilled wish / and an unconscious yearning for life."24 The image of the slave ship was particularly resonant in the events following Katrina, Patterson remembers, "because this time where all of the water, and all of the boats, and how we were treated . . . was just like then . . . to have a body next to you, that's no different than being in the bellies and in the bowels of these ships."25 Thus, her poetics vividly link struggles in post-Katrina New Orleans to a genealogy of transatlantic slavery and captivity.26 "We Know This Place" flips the racist script expressed in mass-mediated discourses about the event of Katrina.27 There is a stark difference in meaning between those constructed by hegemonic racial narratives in the wake of the storm and those invoked in Patterson's lyrics: "We know this place, all too well, dank with the smell of death and doom."28 She explains that her goal in writing the poem was to show that "this is not a place that we have not been, but there is a place where we have not been, and we can get to that place if we move forward in real beauty, if we move forward in real love, if we move forward in the real notion of freedom."29 Indicating how routes from oppression to liberation can be realized, these words speak to the emotions and subconscious desires of struggle by articulating the *future in the present*. 30

Patterson's cultural productions serve as an especially compelling counterdiscourse to the language and symbolism that have come to inhabit neoliberal racial regimes of security.31 The ostensibly race "neutral" language of security promoted by politicians and reproduced in dominant media narratives constructed the Black poor as "criminals" and "thugs."32 Such categorizations occurred through manufactured images linking race and crime, through the circulation of discourses of "hoodlums," "looters," and "refugees."33 These constructions hid the white supremacist and capitalist conditions that produced poverty for the many. Images of besieged whiteness then served as justification for militarism, policing, and mass incarceration.34 This racial imaginary has been shaped by a security political economy that expands militarized policing and mass imprisonment to control the production of labor surpluses that are disproportionately Black.35

The event of Katrina reveals the ways in which anti-Black racism, racist violence, and neoliberal security narratives permeate the cultural and political landscape of the United States.36 In keeping with Ruth Gilmore's generative definition, racism here refers to the "state-sanctioned or extralegal production and exploitation of group-differentiated vulnerability to premature death."37 The case of the post-Katrina Gulf Coast shows that American and ethnic studies scholars working to interpret and explain the *fatal couplings* of race and power are up against academic disciplines and institutions that professionally obscure how the exercise of "racial power" actually works.38 Engaging the poetic knowledge of people like Sunni Patterson enables us to see that racial capitalism produces an "unacceptable standard of human conduct."39 The marriage of poetic knowledge and scholarly research is never about merely describing the past or present conditions for the purposes of filling "abstract homogenous time" but is focused instead on tapping into the existence of an alternative social order that is already present in the self-activity of ordinary people.40

Realities shaped by privatization, forced migration, racialization, the feminization of poverty under neoliberal globalization, criminalization, mass incarceration, and exploitation appear as processes for which there are no alternatives.41 But these practices, processes, and institutions also create potential opportunities for the development of alternative modalities of governance rooted in the activity of aggrieved groups themselves as they struggle for an augmented social wage, housing, public education, health care, water, food, land, and transportation for the waged and unwaged organized as a commons.42 The poetic evidence articulated in Patterson's work helps us locate the existence of historical alternatives to neoliberal racial regimes of security in the collective memory and subjectivity of aggrieved communities in New Orleans as well as oppressed people across the planet.43 Her music and poetry represent radical imaginations, beliefs, and practices.44 This blend of social memory and historical consciousness compels us to ask how poetic knowledge about the construction of neoliberal racial regimes of security can be applied politically.45

What is the consequence of the intensification of a racial regime of security in the context of dramatic class and gender inequality? How is this intensification seen from the perspective of aggrieved communities? How do the freedom dreams and collective struggles of the aggrieved themselves suggest alternative modes of governance, cultural dignity, and rights to a social wage? A genealogy of the transition from "welfare to warfare state" will help us better understand neoliberal security narratives within a longer history of racial regimes.46 This article explores the politics of poetic knowledge to foreground alternative ways of seeing struggles for a social wage and new commons that are otherwise obscured through racialized neoliberal security narratives of the event of Katrina.47

### **The Katrina Circumstance**

On August 28, 2005, the day before the levees broke, New Orleans mayor Ray Nagin declared a "state of emergency." He issued an evacuation order for the city with the exception of select personnel from the state, hospitals, the Orleans Parish Prison, and the prisoners held there.48 Massive flooding overwhelmed the poorest sectors of the city—including devastation in Patterson's neighborhood in the Ninth Ward that destroyed her family's home.49 Instead of a determination of how to best meet the basic survival needs of communities most affected by the storm, a power struggle emerged between federal politicians and those at the state level, demonstrating continuity with traditional practices of "benign neglect" of the Black poor and working class.50 According to the *Washington Post*, the Bush administration sought control over the National Guard soldiers and police officers then under the governor's supervision. According to one official, Bush had "clear legal authority to federalize National Guard units to quell civil disturbances under the Insurrection Act and [would] continue to try to unify the chains of command that are split among the president, the Louisiana governor, and the New Orleans mayor."51 Political debates over control and power received precedence over rescue efforts aimed at protecting human life. Left vulnerable to catastrophe by federal budget cuts and decades of city and state neglect, poor and Black people were abandoned in neighborhoods in the floodplain most affected when the levee broke.52

Louisiana governor Kathleen Blanco issued orders to soldiers from the National Guard returning from Iraq entering New Orleans to "shoot to kill" the "looters."53 Brigadier General Gary Jones of the Louisiana National Guard explained to Joseph Chenelly of the *Army Times* that the first objective of the military was to establish "security" in the city because "anarchy" reigned. Indeed, Chenelly characterized the deployment of troops as an effort to quell "insurgency."54 In an interview with *ABC News*, Louisiana corrections officer Melissa Murray depicted New Orleans as a "war zone."55 New Orleans U. S. attorney Jim Letten reported to the *Washington Post* that a temporary jail was set up for "looting" and, more vaguely, "other crimes."56 The White House's position on this specific response is unambiguous: "The security situation is a concern. It is a priority . . . And there is a zero tolerance approach."57 On September 2, President George W. Bush arrived in New Orleans at Louis Armstrong Airport, where, according to the *New York Times*, "television pictures showed a convoy of National Guard and civilian trucks plowing through flooded streets to bring supplies and the promise of order to this chaotic city."58

The "promise of order" through security was part of an effort to calm bourgeois anxieties and provide assurances that "law and order" would be restored.

In an unwitting demonstration of the double meaning of "coverage," news reports mystified white supremacy, new kinds of enclosures, and the warfare racist state in sensationalized media accounts of the event.59 The Katrina circumstance instances a pattern of militarization of the urban spaces inhabited by the racialized poor that serves to protect bourgeois property interests. These actions occur within the context of the power of regional racial regimes and their arbitrary abuses of human rights in Louisiana and Mississippi.60 The Department of Homeland Security contracted the firm Blackwater to provide private security to government officials in the region.61 The fact that this same firm also provides security to U. S. diplomats in Iraq provides the broader political context for understanding Blackwater operations in New Orleans.62 Additional links between the war in Iraq and the state response in the Gulf Coast were seen in the blatant corporate profiteering. Vice President Cheney's former company, Halliburton's subsidiary Kellogg Brown and Root, was awarded no-bid contracts to reconstruct military bases in Mississippi and Louisiana.63 This situation suggests that, in contrast with official concerns over looting by poor and Black residents of New Orleans, the real danger was the sort of ruling-class looting represented by the alliance of state power and private contractors.

The failure of the U. S. government to respond to dramatic human suffering in the wake of Katrina by providing evacuation, water, food, health care, and rescue for the Black and poor has been well documented.64 Indeed, while the few struggled for power, the many suffered from conditions that produce and exploit vulnerabilities to premature death.65 Analyzing the social forces that made Louisiana and Mississippi the forty-ninth and fiftieth poorest states, one begins to grasp the relationship between poor levee construction and the most impacted spaces in the region being occupied by the Black working class.66 At the same time, the longer history of white supremacy and class exploitation in the region ultimately supported the intensified privatization of education, health care, and housing.67

In April 2008, Bush met with Mexican president Felipe Calderón and Canadian prime minister Stephen Harper for a trilateral summit in New Orleans, the last of four meetings to discuss the passage of the Security and Prosperity Package (SPP), which includes a \$1.4 billion Merida initiative to fund increases in military and police actions modeled after Plan Colombia and the "war on drugs."68 Included in the package is a plan to further militarize the privatization regime of the North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA).69 Bush stated:

New Orleans is one of America's really top cities, and they—the people of New Orleans appreciate the help you gave them right after Hurricane Katrina. And so I want to thank you very much for that. New Orleans has always been a crossroads for our continent, and today there's no better place for our nations to look forward to a bright future, and that's what we're here to do.70

Bush's claim that New Orleans provides an opportune space for elites to envision the future of free trade and security was, while cruel, in some ways brutally honest about the vision of privatization, dispossession, and mass incarceration that the North American ruling class promotes worldwide in response to accumulating waves of struggle.71 In a nefarious extension of the post-NAFTA neoliberal agendas, the security discourse provided a cultural cover for the punitive response to human suffering and pain in New Orleans. In this way, the subordination of New Orleans to the hemispheric security interests of racial capital is linked to a regime of police and prisons that target the poor, who are disproportionately people of color.72

In a major conceit, the security discourse calls upon benevolence to explain the U. S. empire's efforts to "save the people from their own anarchy."73 In this discourse the state prevents a purported chaotic descent into barbarism by promoting militarism. As W. E. B. Du Bois argued many years ago, such narratives draw upon well-established images of races of dark people unable to govern their own affairs.74 Following Du Bois's analytical lead one is able to better understand, as Amy Kaplan puts it, that "domestic and foreign spaces are closer than we think, and the dynamics of imperial expansion cast them into jarring proximity."75 Critical scholarship demonstrates the ways in which racial and gendered capital and its security regime have orchestrated interlocking forms of domination in domestic and global spaces.76

The rise of neoliberal racial regimes of security owes a great deal to the social movements, political cultures, and socioeconomic conditions of the last forty years.77 According to Vijay Prashad, the Black freedom struggle was about access to the social wage that had been extended only to whites from the 1930s until the 1960s through what George Lipsitz calls a "possessive investment in whiteness." The Black freedom movement organized so that the social wage might be extended to all people through redistributive mechanisms in the state.78 Changes in the global economy and reaction from fractions of the ruling class, however, attacked the Keynesian state with the rhetoric of "welfare state."79 The withdrawal of the social wage and imposition of privatization and militarism under neoliberalism can thus be read as responses to insurgent demands for the redistribution of social wealth, autonomy, and dignity.80

Ruth Gilmore demonstrates that the U. S. racial state has been increasingly legitimated as a site of social control in the post-1968 period.81 At the same time, moral panics about race and crime revived the "common sense" idea that the behaviors of aggrieved racial groups were to blame for social problems.82 The class anger of white workers was manipulated through a rearticulation of the "wages of whiteness"—the psychic and sometimes material wage offered to whites that promises them that, no matter how degraded their circumstances become, they will always be white, will have preference over racial others, and will have the security of a floor they cannot fall below.83 The wages of whiteness direct poor whites' attention away from the effects of a larger withdrawal of social wages from the working class as a whole.84 Careful examinations of the neoliberal conditions fostered by aggressive austerity measures in the last four decades demonstrate the role of whiteness in reproducing dramatic class inequality.85 The removal of the social wage that was won during the "Age of Civil Rights" led to an intensification of organized race and class oppression to repair the rupture to power created by antiracist struggles and poor people's movements.86

During the 1960s, the U. S. welfare-warfare state devised a "war on crime" that is key to understanding the racial character of U. S. security discourses.87 Examining what Dylan Rodríguez conceptualizes as the U. S. "prison regime," it becomes clear how "war on crime" and "drugs," "law and order," and "national security" rhetoric in the post-1970s period were articulated—in response to Black and Third World liberation—to legitimate mass imprisonment and militarized policing.88 By the 1980s the United States government withdrew the social wage and organized the state's capacity toward militarism.89 The emergence of the prison regime during the period should be understood, according to Gilmore, as a "politics of abandonment," or the "rigorously coordinated and organized setting aside of people and resources."90 The results have been egregious in the United States for the many: millions of poor people of color are locked away in prisons and jails. Still millions more criminalized people are on probation or parole, or serve as military labor, police, prison guards, border security, and workers in weapons production or other forms of "security" work.91

## **Neoliberal Racial Regimes of Security**

Pundits relying on a historical narrative of progress expressed surprise that racial insult and class injury "still" happened in the wake of Katrina. Such a perspective relies on a view of history that is untenable.92 According to Cedric Robinson, racial regimes "are constructed social systems in which race is proposed as a justification for the relations of power."93 The conceptualization of racial regimes has been part of an effort to render visible their hostility to exposure. Racial regimes are "commonly masqueraded as natural orderings . . . [y]et they are actually contrivances, designed and delegated by interested cultural and social powers."94 While the discourse of security is not an openly racist rhetoric, it engages in a racist practice, and thus suggests the neoliberal era's continuity with prior racial regimes.95 This racialized rhetoric calls for analytics that grasp the role of knowledge production itself in struggles over meaning, power, and social wealth.96

In the wake of Katrina the mass media sensationalized New Orleans as one of multiple spaces constructed in the racial imaginary as being prone to "violent rebellion."97 Discourses of illegality, violence, and disorder conjure a memory of state responses to urban rebellions constructed as "race riots."98 Read against the ideological climate of the post-1992 Los Angeles rebellion period, as Wendy Cheng and Michelle Commander explain, this discourse provided the language that purportedly justified "government negligence" after the storm.99 This historical imagining resurrects myths of American identity and intensifies an accumulation of anxieties about the racialized poor representing threats to "security."100 Revealing these conceits as they express themselves in discourses during the drama of social conflict shows the "blood that has dried in the codes" of security.101

Pre-Katrina New Orleans had the highest rate of incarceration of any city in the country, with a rate of 1,480 per 100,000 residents being locked away in cages.102 Louisiana locks up 814 people out of every 100,000 of its citizens. During the past twenty years the prison population has increased 285 percent. When this evidence is considered in the light of race and gender inequality, the data shows that Blacks are incarcerated at a rate almost six times higher than whites, and women are the fastest growing prison population. Louisiana's expenditures on prisons are almost 10 percent of the budget, while 15 percent is allocated for education.103 Blacks constitute 32 percent of Louisiana, but make up 72 percent of the prisoners.104

Criminalization, racialization, and representation are not unrelated.105 For example, the discourse of "looting" in the wake of Katrina was particularly revealing of how the Black poor have been criminalized within the white supremacist logics of neoliberal racial regimes of security. While widely circulated photographs depicted whites taking goods, the discourse of looting was mainly reserved for Blacks.106 Two further examples demonstrating the criminalization of the Black poor include the abandonment of the prisoners held at Orleans Parish Prison and the construction of "Camp Greyhound." According to Human Rights Watch, prisoners in the Orleans Parish Prison "were abandoned in their cells without food or water for days as floodwaters rose toward the ceiling." The prisoners that remained in the locked prison (almost 90 percent Black) reported seeing bodies floating in the filthy floodwaters. As the report explains: "Many of the men held at the jail had been arrested for offenses like criminal trespass, public drunkenness, or disorderly conduct. Many had not even been brought before a judge and charged, much less been convicted."107 The Louisiana Department of Corrections sent Warden Burl Cain from the infamous Angola State Prison to transform the Greyhound parking lot into a cage made out of razor wire with an entrance sign: "Welcome to New Angola South."108 Guards from the prison were brought in to impose the technique of mass arrest of "looters" to "securitize" the city from the specter of the imagined threat of poor Blacks to "law and order."109

In December 2006, Angela Davis gave a keynote address at a conference in New Orleans organized by Critical Resistance titled "Amnesty for Prisoners of Katrina: A Weekend of Reconciliation and Respect for Human Rights." She posed the critical question of how to identify spaces "haunted by racism." By showing how the "ghosts of slavery" shape the ideological process whereby Blacks are labeled as threats to social order under neoliberal racial regimes, Davis provided a framework to grasp the ways in which the contemporary practice of racism "expresses itself under the sign of equality. . . . The negative affirmation of a legal personality of black people continues to hold sway today." The stakes in this conceptualization have to do with thinking about how the prison serves as "the negative side of democratic freedom . . . [j]ust as slavery . . . used to furnish the evidence of freedom to people who weren't slaves." Thus, Davis evoked long liberationist trajectories of abolitionism in her call to support Critical Resistance's campaign to gain amnesty for "Prisoners of Katrina" in the struggle against the systematic racist human rights abuses Black prisoners endured.110

As late as October 2008 the National Guard continued to patrol the streets of New Orleans. A report by National Public Radio's JJ Sutherland stated: "The guard's mission is to prevent looting and provide . . . law and order."111 According to the report, soldiers work twelve-hour shifts patrolling the streets by foot and in military police vehicles. They do not make arrests, but detain people until the New Orleans police arrive.112 In his column "This Land," *New York Times* journalist Dan Barry writes: "Three years after Hurricane Katrina New Orleans still needs the military to help keep some semblance of order in certain neighborhoods." He sets a scene that renders the militarized policing of Black and poor communities a reasonable technique of ensuring order. Barry provides several examples to support his narrative about the legitimacy of the military presence, including a driver "suspected of being high," yet "in the ensuing search and questioning, they learn that she is on her way to work as an exotic dancer. No drugs in her purse, Specialist Sylvia says. 'Only stripping supplies and condoms.'" This story concludes with a narrative that reflects a moral panic and *anticipatory criminalization*:

Long past midnight the Crown Vic prowls the palpable absence in the Lower Ninth Ward. The beam of the sergeant's flashlight skitters across a collapsed storefront, a restored home bearing a No Trespassing sign, an abandoned house bearing an old spray-painted X. It settles there upon a man, sitting alone in the consuming pitch, waiting for something.113

In the last analysis, this neoliberal security narrative suggests the predominantly Black Ninth Ward is a *place* of drugs, prostitution, excess, and disorder "in need of regulation" by the patriarchal racial state.114

### **The Politics of Poetic Knowledge**

Because the explanations of intensified social conflict and abandonment in the wake of Katrina are often filtered through mass-mediated discourses, the *ways of seeing* systems of oppression promoted by aggrieved Black and poor communities in the region require further elaboration.115 As Clyde Woods suggested in his initial call for papers for this special issue of *American Quarterly*, the crisis of the post-Katrina response to human suffering demands antiracist scholarship that learns from the "transformative power of Gulf Coast cultural traditions and movements." Radical artists, cultural workers, and grassroots activists can provide poetic knowledge of a new society struggling to be born in the degraded present, and expose what Cedric Robinson describes as the conceits of "*a civilization maddened by its own perverse assumptions and contradictions . . . loose in the world*."116 Sunni Patterson's poetry and music subvert the perverse assumptions that have shaped the ideological terrain of the Katrina circumstance and show how the agenda of New Orleans has become a global imperative.

At the U. S. Social Forum held in Atlanta, Georgia, during the summer of 2007, Patterson asked the audience to "be mindful of words," and how "trained our eyes have to be" to understand the legacy of racial slavery in the present. "We Know This Place" speaks from within the "aesthetic genealogy" of the Black radical tradition.117 Specifically, Patterson's invocation "Fourteenth Amendment, X, refugee, check" refers to the emancipation struggle after the Civil War to create abolition democracy through the Fourteenth Amendment. It also references the betrayal of abolition democracy articulated in Du Bois's *Black Reconstruction in America*. 118 Her poem links struggles in post-Katrina New Orleans to a longer genealogy of freedom dreams.119 With this historical drama in mind, I read Patterson's poetics as located in long histories of Black radicalism by describing her role as an "urban archaeologist."120 Read against the neoliberal racial regime of security, Patterson's poetics reveal her commitments to the fugitive knowledge of the aggrieved and subvert the regime's cultural cover, exposing the mystifications of the mass-mediated discourse of counterinsurgency and security.121 Indeed, Patterson's *Porch Prophesies* and *Ofo Ase/Words of Power* provide a different way of seeing, knowing, and feeling from the perspective of aggrieved communities in the Gulf Coast in the wake of Katrina.122

Patterson's "We Know This Place," for example, expresses the poetic knowledge of Black working-class New Orleanians' struggle against premature death. There are tactics in her poetics that show the historical—in other words, not natural—basis of race and class inequality. She shows that Black people in New Orleans have been here before, that the descendents of humans who were treated as if they were property know all too well the costs of elevating property rights over human rights. As her poem explains:

So we know this place, for we have glanced more times than we'd like to share into eyes that stare with nothing there behind them but an unfulfilled wish and an unconscious yearning for life though death rests comfortably beside us . . . The silent cries of the keepers are louder than the booms that come from the guns they use to occupy the space.123

Patterson's words denaturalize the imagined link between place and race, showing that the production of the militarized space was linked to long histories of vulnerability to premature death experienced by the Black poor.124

And we know this place. It's decked in all its array and splendor, golden streets with good intentions capture our attention, gadgets and inventions pesticide the food supply, flu-like symptoms, diabetic condition, a cancer in the system, held on hold, it's a pistol to the temple.125

Read in the context of the brutal restriction of spatial mobility through incarceration and forced migration because of gentrification, Patterson's poetics articulate a process of becoming that connects domestic struggles against racism and poverty to global struggles for justice and dignity. During a talk at a town hall meeting, for example, Patterson read a poem that linked the struggles of Black people in New Orleans with social struggles across the U. S. and global South. "You tell me," Patterson asked, what's the difference between two women in New Orleans shot point-blank in the back of the head and two women bombed in their car in Baghdad? Or government sanctioned killings in Kenya and a nigga held hostage in a house in Virginia? Or poverty in Haiti and poverty in Jamaica? Or rape in Rwanda and rape in Somalia? A sweatshop in China or one in Guatemala? Or small pox in blankets [and] syphilis in Tuskegee? Formaldehyde and FEMA? Ethnic cleansing and Katrina? I recall within a speech Dr. King made us aware . . . that *injustice anywhere is injustice everywhere*. 126

Here she engaged directly with the politics of poetic knowledges in the wake of Katrina and the crises of neoliberalism and empire to show that the *production of vulnerabilities to premature death* is part of a global system of organized oppression.127

Patterson taps into deep cultural reservoirs to articulate new languages and subjectivities, claiming what Gaye Theresa Johnson calls a "spatial entitlement," which she defines as the ability to circulate in "sonic waves" voices of struggle and dignity.128 As an expression of what Clyde Woods elaborates as "blues epistemologies," Patterson's cultural productions contend not only with present ideologies but also with subjugated histories of social conflict.129 Her song "We Made It" provides radical cultural criticism and a "polemical historicity."130

So I'm from a stock that pitch cocktail bombs and hand grenades. We pour cayenne pepper around the perimeter of the building to keep the police dogs at bay. I'm like the Panther Party in the Desire Housing Projects in New Orleans.131

The Katrina tragedy occurred thirty-five years after the Louisiana chapter of the Black Panther Party's office was fired upon by the New Orleans police department across from the infamously overcrowded Desire Projects. The hurricane struck nearly four decades after Malcolm X's assassination in New York. It landed on shore a half-century after Emmet Till's murder in Mississippi. As Gilmore reminds us, themes of premature death can never be far away from the social memory and lived experiences of working-class Blacks.132 In historical terms, Patterson's reclamation of the collective memory of Black people's long and persistent struggle against premature death shows how the subject of violence is manipulated in the popular imaginary:

I'm telling you, death ain't always good. It will leave you fending for water and food. It will riddle up your body in the Audubon Ballroom They'll El-Hajj Malik el-Shabazz you, crown you king, then dethrone you in a Lorraine Hotel. They'll disfigure your body to where folks can't tell if you Emmett Till or not, tell the mama, "Keep that casket open, let all the world see it ain't just burning in Mississippi." Hell, it's hot wherever you be, from the rooftop to the cell block. step on up to the auction block, and bend over, touch your toes, show your teeth, lift her titties, examine his balls, now, this damn near sounds like a hip-hop song, but it's slavery at its peak, it's a circus for all the freaks.133

The song's evocation of bitter memories rooted in the experience with premature death by the Black poor suggests the "structure of an affective social experience."134 That is, Patterson's poetics symbolize a lifetime of racist indignities, sexual humiliations, and class insults that call up the archive of social memory created in the Black freedom struggle.135 In other words, the poet articulates the uncanny ability of aggrieved communities "to look death right in the face and say we made it, we made it, we made it, we made it."136

Recalling the social memory forged in the Black freedom movement, grassroots social theorist Curtis Muhammad draws on his experience in more than four decades of organizing, including his participation in the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC) and New Orleans–based Community Labor United, to suggest an emergent consensus at the grassroots regarding freedom agendas. In an interview with *Democracy Now!* two years after Katrina, Muhammad explained that the current commitment to organizing against prisons and for public education is equivalent to the consensus around the right to vote during the civil rights movement.137 Ultimately, struggles against the prison regime and for public education are related to these complex questions that emerge from the struggle against the *organization of oppression*. 138 As Muhammad instructs, these struggles for justice and dignity express commonalities in that all search for a way to address an intersecting set of questions about race and class oppression to develop the potential for the leadership of the many.

According to Charles Payne, the grassroots philosophy of social change articulated by Ella Jo Baker, Septima Clark, Fannie Lou Hamer, Myles Horton, and Amzie Moore "tested the limits . . . on the ability of the oppressed to participate in the reshaping of their own lives."139 That is, the philosophy and practice focused on the development of ordinary people's capacity for self-governance.140 This grassroots philosophy also focused on tapping into people's culture, which is based on the understanding that people's movements are not based simply on anger about oppression but draw their energy from the enhanced dignity gained by participation in struggles for freedom.141

In a recent interview, Robin Kelley posed a fundamental question for those in search of visions of social alternatives to the neoliberal racial regime of security in the wake of Katrina: "Why don't the people who have been displaced by Katrina have a right to return?"142 People's movements in the wake of Katrina demanded the right to return of the two hundred fifty thousand displaced survivors to places like Texas, Florida, Georgia, and Washington, D. C., but were prevented from doing so because of the privatization of public housing and militarization of space.143 To pose the question of why Black and poor people were denied the right to return calls up a social memory and history of social struggles for civil rights and a social wage. This social memory sustains people's movements for justice and dignity in the present. Kelley argues that perhaps the most visionary idea emerging out of contemporary radical social movements is "the commons." Commenting on the importance of Peter Linebaugh's historical examination of peoples' movements for political and economic rights, *The Magna Carta Manifesto*, Kelley reflects on the costs of forgetting:

The things that we had once accepted as common, as common courtesy, common practice, are taken away. If you also take away the memory of that, then people aren't willing to fight for it. But if you return the memory, people will fight . . . It's saying this is the obligation of society to provide these things for us.144

Scholars and activists can make use of Kelley and Linebaugh's observations on political and economic struggles for a new commons and against the national security state by remembering that the rights to education, health care, public transportation, and housing won during the civil rights movement have been taken away because of neoliberal policies of privatization.145 The concepts of social wage and new commons are suggestive for envisioning historical alternatives in the Gulf Coast region and the nation. This is especially true in a cultural and political context where the right to a social wage has been restricted by the few, while the many suffer from a one-sided class war on the poor, where race is proposed as a justification for mass incarceration, militarized policing, and organized oppression.146

Yet as Kelley calls up Karl Marx's maxim to suggest, shifting the level of analysis will require "throwing off the muck of ages" and building new modes of understanding to construct new social relations.147 Remembering that the transition to neoliberalism and the warfare state meant a shift of resources and power away from the poor challenges narratives about the inevitability of neoliberal racial regimes of security.148 Alternatives to that regime exist in the social memory and collective struggles of aggrieved groups for justice and dignity. Consider, for example, the call by Alisa Bierria, Shana Griffin, Mayaba Liebenthal, and Incite! Women of Color Against Violence and Racism in New Orleans for an "authentic visibility" to "acknowledge the intersection of oppressions within the public and private spheres, [and] create opportunities for everyone to construct new ways to think about justice and liberation."149 Those new ways of thinking include taking on genuine solidarity by supporting the right of return of displaced communities of Katrina survivors, and struggling against the organization of oppression and for a social wage. Gilmore argues that since the social wage is based on political and economic resources produced by labor, social movements can organize themselves to transform them into "life-enhancing use[s]."150 This framework shakes loose common-sense notions of structure and agency through recognition of ordinary people's collective capacity to "*make* power" and "shake the ground."151 The cultivation of a practice where a social wage is secured as a political and economic right entails supporting the freedom dreams and collective struggles of aggrieved Katrina communities themselves.

New Orleans's Ninth Ward poet, author, and Black radical activist Kalamu ya Salaam writes that the task for radical intellectuals who desire a reinvigoration of revolutionary principles in the wake of Katrina is to link critical social theory with the vernacular traditions and freedom dreams of ordinary people to articulate a philosophy of praxis.152 As poet laureate of Louisiana Brenda Marie Osbey wrote in the wake of the storm, the Black and poor masses of New Orleans demonstrate an "unimaginable strength and resilience."153 After all, the history of people's struggle teaches us, as Patterson suggests, those who are oppressed "have been here before." On the question of "what must be done" four years after Katrina, Patterson reflects that a new freedom agenda will require examining the things that "haven't been done," but also asking "what is it that we want?" She explains how knowledge born in the struggle against organized oppressions shows that "it is us that make the difference in these neighborhoods, and these communities, and this society, in this city, in this state, in this country, in this world, it is us. So we have to be able to . . . redefine what this power means . . . for us . . . and begin to see this within ourselves . . . Now that could be something serious."154 Patterson concludes that oppressed peoples need their basic material necessities met—food, clothes, and shelter—but also to reclaim the history of the struggle for freedom. This poetic evidence proves *not only have we made it, but we can keep making it . . . this is not a place that we have not been, but there is a place where we have not been, and we can get to that place if we move forward*. 155

The transformative visions of poets, scholar-activists, and social theorists at the grassroots suggest the task for social movements in a post-Katrina circumstance is to link struggles for a social wage to poetic visions that reclaim the past and articulate the future in the present.156 Sunni Patterson's verses call up the long history of Black, working-class, and commoners' movements to *shake the ground*. 157 Her words nourish the spirit and in turn suggest the "dialectical ability" of working-class communities to "push their historic development agenda forward, ever forward."158 On this score, Patterson's *highly poetic politics* not only function as a critique of the relation between premature death and neoliberal racial regimes of security, but also reveal a collective desire for and vision of freedom:

I swear, we know this place, because we have vowed before never again to return, but here we are, back in the desert, dry mouth and thirsting for waters from Heaven. But come, come, children, rally around, and maybe together we can make a sound that will shake the trees or *rattle the ground,* make strong our knees, we's-a freedom bound.159
