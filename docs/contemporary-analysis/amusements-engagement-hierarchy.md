## **Amusements [Engagement Hierarchy]**

## ![](_page_0_Picture_9.jpeg)

The next rung on the [Engagement Hierarchy](https://web.archive.org/web/20250913094130/http://http//whatgamesare.com/2010/12/the-engagement-hierarchy.html) above *[distraction](https://web.archive.org/web/20250913094130/http://whatgamesare.com/2010/12/distractions-engagement-hierarchy.html)* is *amusement*. Amusements are games into which a typical player might invest some time, which are potentially interesting enough to be habit-forming, but which they still might have trouble identifying out of a line-up.

Most casual games fall into the category of amusement, but so do a lot of console and PC games. Designing to amuse is difficult to do well because it's rarely just a matter of making a more involved distraction or a lighter *connection*. Each stage of the Engagement Hierarchy requires a different approach, which is why game design is usually not a scalable part of production.

## **One Simple and Natural Dynamic**

The ideal amusement teaches the player [one game dynamic](https://web.archive.org/web/20250913094130/http://whatgamesare.com/2010/12/game-dynamics-vs-game-mechanics.html), and often only one. That dynamic is simple, usually physical or skill-based, and *natural*. Naturalism means that the core action is something that the average player can quickly intuit because it is close to something else that they already know.

Some examples of simple and natural dynamics include:

## Swipe gestures in *Fruit Ninja* to cut fruit

- Rotation left and right in *Tetris* to turn blocks
- Move left, right and shoot in *Space Invaders*
- Draw back and throw carefully in *Wii Sports: Bowling*
- Click object to collect it in *City Ville*
- Shoot and dodge in *Time Crisis*
- Race close to danger in *Burnout*
- Run-and-Jump in *Sonic the Hedgehog*
- Tilt the world in *Super Monkey Ball*

Simple and natural game dynamics are easy to learn because the player does not have to conceptually understand what the dynamics are trying to teach. What the player does is immediately apparent, and so all they have to do is practise doing it better, and develop strategies for doing it smarter.

Where casual games often fail is in making sure their dynamic is simple and natural. A dynamic might be simple from a code perspective, but requires the player to make a cognitive leap, for example. That's usually a failure. Alternatively, it might be a natural action, but the results are subtle and hard to interpret. Likewise, this is a cause of failure.

Amusements often feature *segmented* play. This means that the game is formally split into short levels, tracks or waves, each distinct from the last. The ideal separation of segments is one that adds a new enemy, puzzle, object or objective, so the player feels that the game is a continuous challenge. Segments sometimes connect together in worlds, usually employing a variety of themes (ice world, fire world, industrial world etc.), but without any wish to make something deeper of them.

## **Rewards**

Amusements reward players repeatedly and quickly, with endless *Well Done* messages, gold coins, bonuses, music, sound effects and animation. Amusements often do this to the point of garish overload because they are constantly working to keep the player amused. It may seem obvious, but often many technically correct and interesting would-be amusements fail to include sound, for example, or have a muted visual style. Such games often seem arid.

While old school game design often made such rewards the subject of hard-fought skill challenges, games today often take the attitude of pinball machines, and just be loud and cheerful regardless. The modern amusement is more focused on delivering little delights and shocks to the player than strictly monitoring their performance like an exam.

The reward structure does not have to make perfect sense, but it needs to not feel random. Players like some luck, but they also like to feel that what they do in the game (their *agency*) actually achieves something. As long as the balance feels about right, they'll keep playing until boredom strikes (see below).

## **Forming Habits**

The goal of all kinds of amusements is to provide short, repeated fun sessions. Ideally into infinity. The player should be motivated and entertained by the prospect of play, as well as the play itself. However amusements are not usually constructed for extended multi-hour sessions, because to do so reveals how repetitive they are, and makes players aware of how bored they are becoming.

Short segmented bursts of play, on the other hand, are more likely to be habit-forming. The ideal game segment for an amusement is only a few minutes, probably no more than 10. That might comprise a level, a race around a track, a few minutes of a football match or a daily check in on your social game. Players who are in the mood for amusement are thinking in terms of fun which adds a little something to their day.

The ability to save game progress is important for encouraging habits. One of the simplest and yet most fundamental reasons why social games have been stealing a lot of traffic from casual Flash distractions is that the players' *game states* are saved. So your best scores, levels and achievements are all retained by the game's servers, and this means that the habit doesn't feel entirely futile. Games without this ability very quickly become boring.

## **Boredom**

All amusements eventually become boring. Players either get tired of seeing the same rewards repeatedly, or they hit their maximum *mastery* of and are no longer improving at the core game dynamic. There is definitely a point in most amusements where striving for points or levels seems too hard, or futile, and that's when the majority of players give up. Players that do stay in the game for longer are not engaging with the game as an amusement any more. It has become a *connection*, an object of *investment* or even a *culture* to them.

Boredom also has a lot to do with whether the game dynamic is *extensible*. A good example is the game *[Flick Kick](https://web.archive.org/web/20250913094130/http://www.pikpokgames.com/football/) [Football](https://web.archive.org/web/20250913094130/http://www.pikpokgames.com/football/)* on i Phone, where the game dynamic is to flick and curve the ball to score goals, aiming for the edges to get *Skill Shot* bonuses. The game rearranges defenders and the goalkeeper in front of the goal in many non-random patterns, which makes the challenge of the kick continually interesting. The addition of extra game modes also helps to stave off boredom, although the timer mode is by far the most interesting.

A bad example is *[Canabalt](https://web.archive.org/web/20250913094130/http://www.adamatomic.com/canabalt/)* where the jumping mechanic, while fun, moves so quickly that the game starts to feel pretty random for all but the most lightning-reflex players. While the game is entertaining, it is too short and too hard to master. (*Canabalt* is popular for another reason, however, which is to do with connection. Find out more in the next article in this series.)

## **The Orange Game**

Some amusements achieve life-long memory in the hearts of players and have become cultural icons. In particular, old school amusements like *Space Invaders, Pac Man, Pong, Tetris, Donkey Kong* and other legends from the dawn of gaming resonate with a whole generation. They are identified with younger and more innocent times. Even today, some re-releases of these games will attract interest. They have become connections to their audience. Some have even become the founding stones of investment and culture. Retro-gaming, for example, is a small culture that has formed around yesteryear successes.

Those are the exceptions.

When I worked for [BSkyB](https://web.archive.org/web/20250913094130/http://sky.com/) as an external producer, we performed focus tests on the audience for our interactive TV games. In recorded sessions, as well as studying analytic data, we would often see the same pattern repeated:

- Players would commonly choose the default new game of the week and have no real favourite game.
- They would interact with branded games from shows that they recognised.
- If they did have a favourite they would know how to find their game on the service, but not know what the game was named.

I always remember one woman in particular who said: '*I know how to find my game. It's the one in the top corner. The orange one. I play it all the time.*' Even though she had been playing it for more than six months, she had no idea what her favourite game was called.

A similar pattern repeats all across amusement gaming. The players are really not that interested in the game as an item of culture, status or self-identification. Instead, amusement players want something to make them smile and pass the time. It's only a rare game that they remember the name of, and usually that's one that defines the platform for them.

*Wii Sports* or *Angry Birds* are retained, but not much else.

## **Platform Dependency**

Amusements often find marketing difficult. Although they can use the same sharing mechanisms that drive distractions, they do not work as well because sharing activity from amusements lacks *sociality*.

Sociality means social relevance. If you see a share of *'Tadhg is most like Spiderman!'* from a superhero quiz that I've shared on Facebook, the implicit invitation to you is to see what superhero you might be. This has social relevance because everyone knows who Spiderman is, and most people would like to identify either as Spiderman or some other well-known hero. Though only a distraction, that distraction has high sociality, and sociality is one of the keys to *resonance*.

*High scores*, on the other hand, lack sociality. They don't really mean anything to your friends, and offer no real reason to compel clicks. Some will if the theme of the game resonates with them, but even that is quite weak. If my superhero quiz did not have Spiderman as one of the answers, it would have the same problem. Answering a quiz and being told you are Purpleman doesn't mean anything.

Amusements are widespread but often indistinguishable to players. This means that they are heavily *platform-*dependent to spread. An example in social games is the use of marketing and cross-promoting channels ([as I wrote about in my](https://web.archive.org/web/20250913094130/http://whatgamesare.com/2010/12/cityville-explained-part-1-social-games.html) [City Ville post earlier today](https://web.archive.org/web/20250913094130/http://whatgamesare.com/2010/12/cityville-explained-part-1-social-games.html)). On the i Phone, the entire developer ecosystem relies on the editorial goodwill of Apple, who decide what the featured apps of the week are going to be. On most Flash game portals, the most prominent link gets as much traffic as every other link on the home page *put together*.

Amusements are just there, in players' eyes. They're fun, they play them, they like them and they want to habitually play them if they can. But they don't fundamentally care about them, and players who do engage with them on a higher level (and there are always a few) do so sporadically. A game needs to be crafted to attract higher engagement to really achieve it and make it sink into players' minds.

The major exception to this is the platform killer app. Some consoles, like the Wii, push particular games very hard as a part of their whole package, so the game gets swept up into the marketing story of the platform. *Tetris* is a major example of how a game can ride a platform (the Game Boy), as is *Angry Birds*. Amusements twinned with compelling platforms can go very far. Those that are on existing platforms or portals usually struggle for oxygen, however.

## **Anonymity**

Leading on from this, nobody cares who the developer that made the amusement is. If amusements are most commonly identified with the platform (which they are) then players often think that the platform actually made the game. It's bizarre to consider, but many players of Zynga games have no idea who Zynga are, and think that games existing on Facebook means Facebook made them. Likewise if you asked to your mum, husband, wife, son or daughter playing *Cut the Rope* who actually made the game, many would say '*i Phone'*.

There's no reason to know because the game is not important to the player. Other games may well be, but amusements are not. They're not generally particularly *thaumatic* games, so they don't bridge connections or engage the humanity of the player. So the player has no reason to really care. It's just an activity that they're doing for fun.

This despite the fact that the game may well display the name of the developer or publisher every time it loads, or try to reinforce a brand relationship by using the name of the studio in the game title. These are not reasons to engage further. They are just labels, and life is full of labels to ignore already. If you don't want to be anonymous then maybe you shouldn't be making games that encourage anonymity.

## **Nearly Free**

The best business model that works for amusements is *nearly free*. That means virtual good, pay-to-play and *App Store* prices. In most cases, the overriding message to the player is that trying the game requires little or no up-front commitment, and if the game does become something of a habit later, well then maybe a little cash could parted with.

In some ways, the new world of freemium and virtual goods is all new, but in others it feels like the old days of arcade games and small pricing. Pay-to-play gaming such as *skill gaming* (entering high score competitions for money) is vaguely along the same lines, and the forthcoming distribution of Facebook Credits as a commercial platform across the web may well bring forth a whole new generation of such economics.

What doesn't work for amusements are subscriptions or advertising. Subscriptions are just too much of an up-front commitment. A subscription forces a player to really think about what they are doing with their time, energy and money, and often the answer comes down on the wrong side for amusements.

Advertising doesn't work too well for the opposite reason. With many Flash games the game may well be wrapped in an *in-skin* page, but the player will often sit on that page for 15 or 20 minutes playing the game. For advertising-based business models that is basically dead time. It won't result in more engagement for the advertisement, nor more opportunities to serve impressions.

For retail, amusements generally do not sit anywhere near a *premium* price point. The 59p/99c *App Store* price, or the *Steam 75% off* sale, or even completely free, is what players like. This is why a lot of console games try and sell themselves as something more engaging than they actually are: Connection is monetarily more valuable than amusement. However those same games are the ones that end up in the second hand discount bin days after their official launch. Some console games are deliberately made and sold as high quality amusements (such as *Mario Kart*), and they usually avoid the discount bin fate because they know what they're aiming for. However more serious games (like *The Need for Speed* series) are prime discount material because they don't deliver on the promised connection, and their amusement value is quite tepid.

A lot of console games could do with not trying to be so Hollywood-serious, and realise that they are in fact amusements. It might save a lot of development money in the long run and lead to focused and just plain better games.
