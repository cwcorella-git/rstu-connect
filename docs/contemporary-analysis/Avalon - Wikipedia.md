**Avalon** ([\)\[note 1\]](#page-18-0) is an island featured in the [Arthurian legend.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthurian_legend) It first appeared in [Geoffrey of Monmouth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_of_Monmouth)'s 1136 [Historia Regum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historia_Regum_Britanniae) [Britanniae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historia_Regum_Britanniae) as a place of magic where [King Arthur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Arthur)'s sword [Excalibur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excalibur) was made and later where Arthur was taken to recover from being gravely wounded at the [Battle of Camlann.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Camlann) Since then, the island has become a symbol of Arthurian mythology, similar to Arthur's castle, [Camelot.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camelot)

Avalon was associated from an early date with mystical practices and magical figures such as King Arthur's sorceress sister [Morgan,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morgan_le_Fay) cast as the island's ruler by Geoffrey and many later authors. Certain [Cornish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornish_mythology) and [Welsh traditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_mythology) have maintained that Arthur is an eternal king who had never truly died but [would return](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Arthur%27s_messianic_return) as the "once and future" king. The particular motif of his rest in Morgan's care in Avalon has become especially popular. It can be found in various versions in many French and other medieval Arthurian and other works written in the wake of Geoffrey, some of them also linking Avalon with the legend of the [Holy Grail.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Grail)

Avalon has often been identified as the former island of [Glastonbury Tor.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glastonbury_Tor) An early and long-standing belief involves the purported discovery of Arthur's remains and their later grand reburial, in accordance with the medieval English tradition in which Arthur did not survive the fatal injuries he suffered in his final battle. Besides Glastonbury, several other alternative locations of Avalon have also been claimed or proposed. Many medieval sources also localized the place in [Sicily,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicily) and European folklore connected it with the phenomenon of [Fata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fata_Morgana_(mirage)) [Morgana.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fata_Morgana_(mirage))

The meaning and origin of the name Avalon have been long debated by Arthurian scholars as well as Celtic and [Romance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_languages) philologists[.\[1\]](#page-19-0) [Geoffrey of Monmouth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_of_Monmouth) in his pseudo-chronicle [Historia Regum Britanniae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historia_Regum_Britanniae) ("The History of the Kings of Britain", c. 1136) calls the place Insula Avallonis, meaning the "Isle of Avallon" in [Latin.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) In his later [Vita Merlini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vita_Merlini) ("The Life of Merlin", c. 1150), he calls it Insula Pomorum, the "Isle of Fruit Trees" (from Latin pōmus "fruit tree"). Today, the name is generally considered to be from the [Welsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_language) afal "apple tree, fruit tree" (from [Proto-Celtic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Celtic) \*abalnā, literally "fruit-bearing (thing)"). A [Cornish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornish_language) or [Breton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breton_language) origin is also possible, deriving from aball or avallen(n). [\[2\]](#page-19-1)[\[3\]](#page-20-0)[\[4\]](#page-20-1)[\[5\]](#page-20-2)[\[6\]](#page-20-3)

The tradition of an "apple" island among the ancient Britons may also be related to [Irish legends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_legends) of the [otherworld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_Otherworld) island home of [Manannán mac Lir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manann%C3%A1n_mac_Lir) and [Lugh,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lugh) [Emain Ablach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emain_Ablach) (also the [Old Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Irish) poetic name for [Isle of Man\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isle_of_Man)[,\[3\]](#page-20-0) where Ablach means "Having Apple Trees["\[7\]—](#page-20-4) from Old Irish aball ("apple") — and is similar to the [Middle Welsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Welsh) name [Afallach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afallach), which was used to replace the name Avalon in medieval Welsh translations of French and Latin Arthurian tales. All are related to the Gaulish root \*aballo "fruit tree" (found in the place name [Aballo/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avallon) [Aballone\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avallon) and are derived from Proto-Celtic \*abal- "apple", which is related at the [Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-European_language) level to English apple, Russian яблоко (jabloko), Latvian ābele, et al.[\[8\]](#page-20-5)[\[9\]](#page-20-6) In the early 12th century, [William of Malmesbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_of_Malmesbury) claimed the name of Avalon came from a man called Avalloc, who once lived on this isle with his daughters[.\[10\]](#page-20-7) [Gerald of Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerald_of_Wales) similarly derived the name of Avalon from its purported former ruler, Avallo[.\[11\]](#page-20-8) The name is also similar to "Avallus", described by [Pliny the Elder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pliny_the_Elder) in his 1st-century [Naturalis Historia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalis_Historia) as a mysterious island where amber could be found.[\[12\]](#page-20-9)

#### **Geoffrey of Monmouth**

## [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Avalon&action=edit§ion=3)

According to Geoffrey in the Historia, and much subsequent literature which he inspired, [King Arthur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Arthur) was taken to Avalon (Avallon) in hope that he could be saved and recover from his mortal wounds following the tragic [Battle of Camlann.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Camlann) Geoffrey first mentions Avalon as the place where Arthur's sword [Excalibur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excalibur) (Caliburn) was forged.

Geoffrey dealt with the subject in more detail in the Vita Merlini, in which he describes for the first time in Arthurian legend the fairy or fae-like enchantress [Morgen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morgan_le_Fay) (i.e. Morgan) as the chief of [nine sisters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nine_sorceresses) (including Moronoe, Mazoe, Gliten, Glitonea, Gliton, Tyronoe and Thiten[\)\[13\]](#page-21-0) who together rule Avalon. Geoffrey's telling, in the in-story narration by the bard [Taliesin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taliesin) to [Merlin,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merlin) indicates a sea voyage was needed to get there. The description of Avalon, which is heavily indebted to the early medieval Spanish scholar [Isidore of Seville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isidore_of_Seville) (having been mostly derived from the section on famous islands in Isidore's work [Etymologiae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etymologiae), XIV.6.8 "[Fortunatae Insulae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortunate_Isles)")[,\[14\]](#page-21-1) [\[15\]](#page-21-2)[\[16\]](#page-21-3)[\[17\]](#page-21-4) shows the magical nature of the island:

The Isle of Fruit Trees which men call the Fortunate Isle (Insula Pomorum quae Fortunata uocatur) gets its name from the fact that it produces all things of itself; the fields there have no need of the ploughs of the farmers and all cultivation is lacking except what nature provides. Of its own accord it produces grain and grapes, and apple trees grow in its woods from the close-clipped grass. The ground of its own accord produces everything instead of merely grass, and people live there a hundred years or more. There nine sisters rule by a pleasing set of laws those who come to them from our country.[\[18\]](#page-21-5)\[note [2\]](#page-18-1)

In [Layamon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Layamon)'s [Brut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Layamon%27s_Brut) version of the Historia, Arthur is taken to Avalon to be healed there through means of magic water by a distinctively [Anglo-Saxon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Saxon) version of Morgen: an [elf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elf) queen of Avalon named Argante[.\[27\]](#page-22-0) In the Didot-Perceval, [Perceval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perceval)'s [Grail Quest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grail_Quest) adventures include him fighting a flock of ravens that turn out to be fairy maidens from Avalon, sisters of the wife of one Urbain of the Black Thorn, in a story likely representing Geoffrey's shapeshifting Morgen and her sisters as inspired by the Welsh [Modron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modron) (Urbain thus being Modron's husband [Urien\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urien) and possibly also influenced by the Irish [Mórrigan.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Morr%C3%ADgan) [\[28\]](#page-22-1)[\[29\]](#page-22-2) Geoffrey's Merlin not only never visits Avalon but is not even aware of its existence, until told about it after Arthur's delivery there by Taliesin. This would change to various degrees in the later Arthurian prose romance tradition that expanded on Merlin's association with Arthur, as well on the subject of Avalon itself.

In many versions of Arthurian legend, including [Thomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Malory) [Malory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Malory)'s compilation [Le Morte d'Arthur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Morte_d%27Arthur), [Morgan the Fairy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morgan_le_Fay) and several other magical queens (numbering either three, four, or "many["\[30\]\)](#page-23-0) arrive after the battle to take the mortally wounded Arthur from the battlefield of Camlann [\(Salisbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salisbury_Plain) [Plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salisbury_Plain) in the romances) to Avalon in a black boat. Besides Morgan, who by this time had already become Arthur's supernatural sibling in the popular romance tradition, they sometimes come with the [Lady of the Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lady_of_the_Lake) among them. The others may include the Queen of Northgales (North Wales) and the Queen of the [Wasteland.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wasteland_(mythology)) [\[31\]](#page-23-1) In the [Vulgate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulgate_Cycle) Queste, Morgan tells Arthur of her intention to relocate to Avalon, "where the ladies who know all the magic in the world are" (ou les dames sont qui seiuent tous les enchantemens del monde [[sic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sic)]), not long before his final battle[.\[32\]](#page-23-2) Its Welsh version also claims, within its text, to be a translation of old Latin books from Avalon, as does the French [Perlesvaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perlesvaus). [\[33\]](#page-23-3)[\[34\]](#page-23-4) In Lope Garcia de Salazar's Spanish summary of the [Post-Vulgate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-Vulgate) Roman du Graal, Avalon is conflated with (and explicitly named as) the mythological [Island of Brasil,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brasil_(mythical_island)) said to be located west of Ireland and afterwards forever hidden in mist by Morgan's enchantment[.\[35\]](#page-23-5)

In some texts, Arthur's fate in Avalon is left untold or uncertain. that Morgan "keeps his healed body for her very own and they now live together.["\[40\]](#page-24-0) In a similar narrative, the chronicle [Draco Normannicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Draco_Normannicus) contains a fictional letter from King Arthur to [Henry II of England,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_II_of_England) claiming Arthur having been healed of his wounds and made immortal by his "deathless (eternal) [nymph"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nymph) sister Morgan in the holy island of Avalon (Avallonis eas insula sacra) through the island's miraculous herbs[.\[41\]](#page-24-1)[\[42\]](#page-24-2) This is reminiscent of the British tradition mentioned by [Gervase of Morgan features as an immortal ruler of a fantastic Avalon, sometimes alongside the still-alive Arthur, in some subsequent and otherwise non-Arthurian chivalric romances such as Tirant lo Blanch, [46] as well as the tales of Huon of Bordeaux, [47] where the faery king Oberon is a son of either Morgan by name or "the Lady of the Secret Isle", [48] and the legend of Ogier the Dane,[49] where Avalon can be described as an enchanted fairy castle (chasteu d'Auallon[50]), [51] as it is also in Floriant et Florete. [52] In his La Faula, Guillem de Torroella claims to have visited the Enchanted Island (Illa Encantada) and met Arthur who has been brought back to life by Morgan and they both of them are now forever young, sustained by the Holy Grail. [53] In La Bataille Loquifer, Morgan and her sister Marsion bring the hero Renoart to Avalon, where Arthur now prepares his return alongside Morgan, Gawain, Ywain, Perceval and Guinevere. [54] [55] Such stories, which also include Lion de Bourges, Mabrien, *Tristan de Nanteuil*, and others, typically take place centuries after the times of King Arthur. According to William W. The Arthurian world evoked is that of Avalon after Arthur's disappearance, whether or not it is explicitly named. Avalon has been also occasionally described as a valley. In Le Morte d'Arthur, for instance, Avalon is called an isle twice and a vale once (the latter in the scene of Arthur's final voyage, oddly despite Malory's adoption of the boat travel motif). Notably, the vale of Avalon (vaus d'Avaron) is mentioned twice in [Robert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_de_Boron) [de Boron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_de_Boron)'s Arthurian prequel [Joseph d'Arimathie](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Joseph_d%27Arimathie_(poem)&action=edit&redlink=1) [\[fr\]](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_d%27Arimathie_(roman)) as a place located in western [Britannia,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Britain) to where a fellowship of early Christians started by [Joseph of Arimathea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_of_Arimathea) brought the Grail after its long journey from the [Holy Land,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Land) finally delivered there by Bron, the first [Fisher King.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_King) [\[65\]](#page-26-5)[\[66\]](#page-26-6)

In his final romance, [Perceval, the Story of the Grail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perceval,_the_Story_of_the_Grail), Chrétien de Troyes featured the sea fortress of Escavalon, ruled by the unspecified King of Escavalon. The name Escavalon might be simply a corruption of the word Avalon that can be literally translated as "Water-Avalon"[,\[67\]](#page-26-7) albeit some scholars proposed various other developments of the name Escavalon from that of Avalon (with [Roger Sherman Loomis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Sherman_Loomis) noting the similarity of the evolution of Geoffrey's Caliburn into the Chrétien's Escalibur in the case of Excalibur[\[68\]\)](#page-26-8), perhaps in connection with the Old French words for either Slav or [Saracen.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saracen) [\[69\]](#page-26-9) Chretien's Escavalon was renamed as Askalon in [Parzival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parzival) by [Wolfram von Eschenbach,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfram_von_Eschenbach) who might have been either confused or inspired by the real-life Middle Eastern coastal city of [Ascalon.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascalon) [\[70\]](#page-27-0)

It is possible that the Chrétien-era Escavalon has turned or split into the Grail realm of [Escalot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astolat) in later prose romances[.\[71\]](#page-27-1)

Nevertheless, the kingdoms of Escalot and Escavalon both appear concurrently in the Vulgate Cycle. There, Escavalon is ruled by King Alain, whose daughter Florée is rescued by Gawain and later gives birth to his son [Guinglain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinglain) (and possibly two others). The character of Alain may have been derived from Afallach (Avallach) of Avalon[.\[72\]](#page-27-2)

#### **Connection to Glastonbury**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Avalon&action=edit§ion=6)

Though no longer an island in the 12th century, the high conical bulk of [Glastonbury Tor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glastonbury_Tor) in today's South-West England. Today, it is a rock outcrop in the town of [Glastonbury,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glastonbury) situated about 15 miles (25 kilometres) from the sea, but had been surrounded by marsh before the draining of [fenland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fen) in the [Somerset Levels.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somerset_Levels) In ancient times, [Ponter's Ball Dyke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ponter%27s_Ball_Dyke) would have guarded the only entrance to the island. [The Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Britain) eventually built another road to the island[.\[73\]](#page-27-3) Glastonbury's earliest name in Welsh was the Isle of Glass, which suggests that the location was at one point seen as an island. At the end of the 12th century, Gerald of Wales wrote in [De instructione](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_instructione_principis) [principis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_instructione_principis):

What is now known as Glastonbury was, in ancient times, called the Isle of Avalon. It is virtually an island, for it is completely surrounded by marshlands. In Welsh it is called Ynys Afallach, which means the Island of Apples and this fruit once grew in great abundance. After the Battle of Camlann, a noblewoman called Morgan, later the ruler and patroness of these parts as well as being a close blood-relation of King

Arthur, carried him off to the island, now known as Glastonbury, so that his wounds could be cared for. Years ago the district had also been called Ynys Gutrin in Welsh, that is the Island of Glass, and from these words the invading Saxons later coined the place-name "Glastingebury"[.\[74\]](#page-27-4)

Lead cross inscribed with Arthur's epitaph, published in [William](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Camden) [Camden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Camden)'s Britannia (1607)

Around 1190, monks at [Glastonbury Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glastonbury_Abbey) claimed to have discovered the bones of Arthur and his wife Guinevere. The discovery of the burial is described by chroniclers, notably Gerald, as being just after King [Henry II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_II_of_England)'s reign when the new abbot of Glastonbury, [Henry de Sully,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_de_Sully_(died_1195)) commissioned a search of the abbey grounds. At a depth of 5 m (16 feet), the monks were said to have discovered an unmarked tomb with a massive [treetrunk coffin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treetrunk_coffin) and, also buried, a lead cross bearing the inscription:

Avalonia.

"Here lies entombed the renowned king Arthur in the island of Avalon."

Accounts of the exact inscription vary, with five different versions existing. One popular today, made famous by Malory, claims "Here lies Arthur, the king that was and the king that shall be" (Hic iacet Arthurus, rex quondam rexque futurus[\[75\]\)](#page-27-5), also known in the now-popular variant "the once and future king" (rex quondam et futurus). The earliest is by Gerald in Liber de Principis instructione c. 1193, who wrote that he viewed the cross in person and traced the lettering. His transcript reads: "Here lies buried the famous Arthurus with Wenneveria his second wife in the isle of Avalon" (Hic jacet sepultus inclitus rex Arthurus cum Wenneveria uxore sua secunda in insula Avallonia[\[76\]\)](#page-27-6). He wrote that in the coffin were two bodies, whom Giraldus refers to as Arthur and "his queen"; the male body's bones were described as gigantic. The account of the burial by the chronicle of [Margam Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margam_Abbey) says three bodies were found, the other being that of [Mordred;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mordred) [Richard Barber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Barber) argues that Mordred's name was airbrushed out of the story once his reputation as a traitor was appreciated[.\[77\]](#page-27-7)

The story is today seen as an example of [pseudoarchaeology.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology) Historians generally dismiss the find's authenticity, attributing it to a publicity stunt performed to raise funds to rebuild the Abbey after it had been destroyed by a 1184 fire[.\[note 3\]](#page-19-2) [Leslie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leslie_Alcock) [Alcock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leslie_Alcock) in his Arthur's Britain postulated a theory according to which the grave site had been originally discovered in an ancient mausoleum sometime after 945 by [Dunstan,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunstan) the Abbot of Glastonbury, who reburied it along with the 10th-century stone cross; it would then become forgotten again until its rediscovery in 1190[.\[79\]](#page-28-0)

In 1278, the remains were reburied with great ceremony, attended by King [Edward I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_I_of_England) and Queen [Eleanor of Castile,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleanor_of_Castile) before the High Altar at Glastonbury Abbey[.\[80\]](#page-28-1) They were moved again in 1368 when the [choir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choir_(architecture)) was extended[.\[81\]](#page-28-2) The site became the focus of pilgrimages until [the dissolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Monasteries) of the abbey in 1539. The fact that the search for the body is connected to Henry II and Edward I, both kings who fought major [Anglo-Welsh wars,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Anglo-Welsh_wars) has had scholars suggest that propaganda may have played a part as well[.\[82\]](#page-28-3) Gerald was a constant supporter of royal authority; in his account of the discovery aims to quash the idea of the possibility of [King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Arthur%27s_messianic_return) [Arthur's messianic return:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Arthur%27s_messianic_return) [\[note 4\]](#page-19-3)

Many tales are told and many legends have been invented about King Arthur and his mysterious ending. In their stupidity the British [i.e. Welsh, Cornish and Breton] people maintain that he is still alive. Now that the truth is known, I have taken the trouble to add a few more details in this present chapter. The fairy-tales have been snuffed out, and the true and indubitable facts are made known, so that what really happened must be made crystal clear to all and separated from the myths which have accumulated on the subject.[\[74\]](#page-27-4)

The burial discovery ensured that in later romances, histories based on them and in the popular imagination, Glastonbury became increasingly identified with Avalon, an identification that continues strongly today. The later development of the legends of the Holy Grail and Joseph of Arimathea interconnected these legends with Glastonbury and with Avalon, an identification which also seems to be made in Perlesvaus. Modern writers such as [Dion Fortune,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dion_Fortune) [John Michell,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Michell_(writer)) [Nicholas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Mann_(occult_writer)) [Mann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Mann_(occult_writer)) and [Geoffrey Ashe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Ashe) have formed theories based on perceived links between Glastonbury and Celtic legends of the Otherworld in attempts to link the location firmly with Avalon, drawing on the various legends based on Glastonbury Tor as well as drawing on ideas like [Earth mysteries,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_mysteries) [ley lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ley_lines) and even the myth of [Atlantis.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantis) Arthurian literature also continues to use Glastonbury as an important location as in [The Mists of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mists_of_Avalon) [Avalon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mists_of_Avalon), [A Glastonbury Romance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Glastonbury_Romance), and [The Bones of Avalon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bones_of_Avalon). Even the fact that [Somerset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somerset) has many apple orchards has been drawn in to support the connection[.\[86\]](#page-28-6) Glastonbury's reputation as the real Avalon has made it a popular site of tourism. Having become one of the major [New Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Age_communities) [communities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Age_communities) in Europe, the area has great religious significance for [neo-Pagans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Paganism) and [modern Druids,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Druidism) as well as some Christians. Identification of Glastonbury with Avalon within [hippie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippie) subculture, as seen in the work of Michell and in the [Gandalf's Garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gandalf%27s_Garden) community, also helped inspire the annual [Glastonbury Festival.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glastonbury_Festival) [\[87\]](#page-28-7)
