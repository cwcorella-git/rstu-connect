## **How has fascism evolved since World War II?**

**fascism**, [political ideology](https://www.britannica.com/topic/ideology-society) and mass movement that dominated many parts of central, southern, and eastern Europe between 1919 and 1945 and that also had adherents in western Europe, the [United States,](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-States) [South Africa,](https://www.britannica.com/place/South-Africa) [Japan,](https://www.britannica.com/place/Japan) Latin America, and the [Middle East.](https://www.britannica.com/place/Middle-East) Europe's first fascist leader, [Benito](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Benito-Mussolini) [Mussolini,](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Benito-Mussolini) took the name of his party from the Latin word [fasces](https://www.britannica.com/topic/fasces), which referred to a bundle of elm or birch rods (usually containing an ax) used as a symbol of penal authority in [ancient](https://www.britannica.com/place/ancient-Rome) [Rome.](https://www.britannica.com/place/ancient-Rome) Although fascist parties and movements differed significantly from one another, they had many characteristics in common, including extreme militaristic [nationalism,](https://www.britannica.com/topic/nationalism) [contempt](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/contempt) for electoral [democracy](https://www.britannica.com/topic/democracy) and political and cultural [liberalism,](https://www.britannica.com/topic/liberalism) a belief in natural social [hierarchy](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/hierarchy) and the rule of elites, and the desire to create a Volksgemeinschaft (German: "people's community"), in which individual interests would be subordinated to the good of the nation. At the end of [World War](https://www.britannica.com/event/World-War-II) [II,](https://www.britannica.com/event/World-War-II) the major European fascist parties were broken up, and in some countries (such as [Italy](https://www.britannica.com/place/Italy) and [West Germany\)](https://www.britannica.com/place/West-Germany) they were officially banned. Beginning in the late 1940s, however, many fascist-oriented parties and movements were founded in Europe as well as in Latin America and South Africa. Although some European "neofascist" groups attracted large followings, especially in Italy and [France,](https://www.britannica.com/place/France) none were as influential as the major fascist parties of the interwar period.

## **National fascisms**

[Adolf HitlerN](https://cdn.britannica.com/34/71234-050-1373A4A8/Adolf-Hitler-parade-Nazi-Munich.jpg)azi Party leader Adolf Hitler (third from right) participating in a Nazi parade in Munich, c. 1930s.

Fascist parties and movements came to power in several countries between 1922 and 1945: the [National Fascist Party](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Fascist-Party) (Partito Nazionale Fascista) in Italy, led by Mussolini; the National Socialist German Workers' Party (Nationalsozialistische Deutsche Arbeiterpartei), or [Nazi Party,](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Nazi-Party) led by [Adolf Hitler](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Adolf-Hitler) and representing his [National Socialism](https://www.britannica.com/event/Nazism) movement; the Fatherland Front (Vaterländische Front) in [Austria,](https://www.britannica.com/place/Austria) led by [Engelbert Dollfuss](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Engelbert-Dollfuss) and supported by the [Heimwehr](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Heimwehr) (Home Defense Force), a major right-wing paramilitary organization; the National Union (União Nacional) in [Portugal,](https://www.britannica.com/place/Portugal) led by [António de Oliveira Salazar](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Antonio-de-Oliveira-Salazar) (which became fascist after 1936); the Party of Free Believers (Elefterofronoi) in [Greece,](https://www.britannica.com/place/Greece) led by [Ioannis Metaxas;](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ioannis-Metaxas) the [Ustaša](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Ustasa) ("Insurgence") in [Croatia,](https://www.britannica.com/place/Croatia) led by [Ante Paveli](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ante-Pavelic)ć; the National

Union (Nasjonal Samling) in [Norway,](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-Norway) which was in power for only a week—though its leader, [Vidkun Quisling,](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vidkun-Abraham-Lauritz-Jonsson-Quisling) was later made minister president under the German occupation; and the [military dictatorship](https://www.britannica.com/topic/military-dictatorship) of Admiral [Tojo Hideki](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Tojo-Hideki) in Japan.

[Spain'](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-Spain)s fascist movement, the [Falange](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Falange) ("Phalanx"), founded in 1933 by [José Antonio Primo de Rivera,](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Jose-Antonio-Primo-de-Rivera-marques-de-Estella) never came to power, but many of its members were absorbed into the military dictatorship of [Francisco Franco,](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Francisco-Franco) which itself displayed many fascist characteristics. In [Poland](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-Poland) the antisemitic Falanga, led by Boleslaw Piasecki, was influential but was unable to overthrow the [conservative](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/conservative) [regime](https://www.britannica.com/topic/regime) of [Józef Pi](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Jozef-Pilsudski)łsudski. [Vihtori Kosola'](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vihtori-Iisakki-Kosola)s [Lapua Movement](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Lapua-Movement) in [Finland](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-Finland) nearly staged a coup in 1932 but was checked by [conservatives](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/conservatives) backed by the army. The [Arrow](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Arrow-Cross-Party) [Cross Party](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Arrow-Cross-Party) (Nyilaskeresztes Párt) in [Hungary,](https://www.britannica.com/place/Hungary) led by [Ferenc](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ferenc-Szalasi) [Szálasi,](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ferenc-Szalasi) was suppressed by the conservative regime of [Miklós](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Miklos-Nagybanyai-Horthy) [Horthy](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Miklos-Nagybanyai-Horthy) until 1944, when Szálasi was made a puppet ruler under the German occupation. In [Romania](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-Romania) the [Iron Guard](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Iron-Guard) (Garda de Fier)—also called the League of Christian Defense, the Legion of the Archangel Michael, and All for the Fatherland—led by [Corneliu Codreanu,](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Corneliu-Codreanu) was dissolved by the dictatorial regime of [King Carol II](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Carol-II) in 1938. In 1939 Codreanu and several of his legionaries were arrested and "shot while trying to escape." In 1940 remnants of the Iron Guard reemerged to share power but were finally crushed by Romanian conservatives in February 1941.

In [France](https://www.britannica.com/place/France) the [Cross of Fire](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Croix-de-Feu) (Croix de Feu), later renamed the French Social Party (Parti Social Français), led by Colonel [François de La Rocque,](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Francois-de-La-Rocque) was the largest and fastest-growing party on the French [right](https://www.britannica.com/topic/right) between 1936 and 1938. In 1937 it was larger than the French communist and socialist parties [combined](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/combined) (one scholar estimated its membership between

700,000 and 1.2 million), and by 1939 it included some 3,000 mayors, about 1,000 municipal councilmen, and 12 parliamentary deputies. Other fascist movements in France included the short-lived Faisceau (1925–28), led by [Georges](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Valois) [Valois;](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Valois) the Young Patriots (Jeunesses Patriotes), led by [Pierre](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Pierre-Taittinger) [Taittinger;](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Pierre-Taittinger) French Solidarity (Solidarité Française), founded and financed by [François Coty](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Francois-Coty) and led by Jean Renaud; the Franks (Francistes), led by Marcel Bucard; the French Popular Party (Parti Populaire Français), led by Jacques Doriot; and French Action [\(Action Française\)](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Action-Francaise), led by [Charles Maurras.](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Charles-Maurras) After the German invasion in 1940, a number of French fascists served in the [Vichy](https://www.britannica.com/event/Vichy-France) regime of Marshal [Philippe Pétain.](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Philippe-Petain)

[Oswald MosleyB](https://cdn.britannica.com/16/133916-050-01D4245B/Oswald-Mosley-rally-London.jpg)ritish Union of Fascists leader Oswald Mosley at a fascist rally, London.

[The](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-United-Kingdom) [British Union of Fascists,](https://www.britannica.com/topic/British-Union-of-Fascists) led by [Oswald Mosley,](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Oswald-Mosley) had some 50,000 members. In [Belgium](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-Belgium) the [Rexist Party,](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Rexist-Party-of-Belgium) led by [Léon](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Leon-Degrelle) [Degrelle,](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Leon-Degrelle) won about 10 percent of the seats in the parliament in 1936. Russian fascist organizations were founded by [exiles](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/exiles) in [Manchuria,](https://www.britannica.com/place/Manchuria) the United States, and elsewhere; the largest of these groups were the Russian Fascist Party (VFP), led by Konstantin Rodzaevsky, and the All Russian Fascist Organization (VFO), led by Anastasy Vonsiatsky.

Outside Europe, popular support for fascism was greatest in South Africa and the [Middle East.](https://www.britannica.com/place/Middle-East) Several fascist groups were founded in [South Africa](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-South-Africa) after 1932, including the Gentile National Socialist Movement and its splinter group, the South African Fascists; the South African [National Democratic Party,](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Democratic-Action) known as the Blackshirts; and the pro-German Ox-Wagon Sentinel (Ossewabrandwag). By 1939 there were at least seven Arab "shirt" movements, including the Syrian People's Party, also called the Syrian National Socialist Party; the Iraqi Futuwa movement; and the Young [Egypt](https://www.britannica.com/place/Egypt) movement, also called the Green Shirts.

Several rival [protofascist](https://www.britannica.com/topic/protofascism) and fascist movements operated in [Japan](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-Japan) after 1918, and their activities helped to increase the influence of the military on the Japanese government. Among the most important of these groups were the Taisho Sincerity League (Taisho Nesshin'kai), the Imperial Way Faction (Kodoha), the Greater Japan National Essence Association (Dai Nippon Kokusui-kai), the Anti-Red Corps (Bokyo Gokoku-Dan), the Great Japan Political [Justice](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/Justice) Corps (Dai Nippon Seigi-Dan), the Blood Brotherhood League (Ketsumei-Dan), the Jimmu Association (Jimmu-Kai), the New Japan League (Shin-Nihon Domei), the Eastern Way Society (Towo Seishin-Kai), and the Great Japan Youth Party (Da-nihon Seinen-dan).

Following the [Mukden Incident](https://www.britannica.com/event/Mukden-Incident) and the wider invasion of Manchuria by Japanese troops in 1931, several fascist-oriented patriotic societies were formed in [China;](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-China) the largest of these groups, the Blue Shirts, formed an alliance with the [Kuomintang](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Nationalist-Party-Chinese-political-party) (National People's Party) under [Chiang Kai-shek.](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Chiang-Kai-shek) At Chiang's order in 1934, the Blue Shirts were temporarily put in charge of political indoctrination in the army and given limited control of its educational system.

European [fascism](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/fascism) had a number of imitators in [Latin America,](https://www.britannica.com/place/Latin-America) including the Nacis, founded in [Chile](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chile) by Jorge González von Mareés; the Gold Shirts, founded in [Mexico](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mexico) by Nicolás Rodríguez; and the Revolutionary Union (Unión Revolucionaria) of Peruvian dictator Luis Sánchez Cerro. The [Brazilian](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-Brazil) Integralist Action party (Ação Integralista Brasileira), which had some 200,000 members in the mid-1930s, was suppressed by the

Brazilian government in 1938 after a failed coup attempt.

In the [United States](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-United-States) the [Ku Klux Klan,](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Ku-Klux-Klan) a white supremacist organization founded at the end of the [Civil War](https://www.britannica.com/event/American-Civil-War) and revived in 1915, displayed some fascist characteristics. One of its offshoots, the Black Legion, had some 60,000 members in the early 1930s and committed numerous acts of [arson](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/arson) and bombing. In 1930 Catholic priest [Charles E. Coughlin](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Charles-E-Coughlin) began national radio broadcasts of sermons on political and economic subjects; his talks became increasingly antidemocratic and antisemitic, as did the journal he founded, [Social Justice](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Social-Justice-American-magazine). After running unsuccessfully for the U. S. presidency in 1936, Coughlin became an apologist for Hitler, Mussolini, and Franco. In 1942 Social Justice was banned from the U. S. mails for violating the Espionage Act, and in the same year the American Catholic church ordered Coughlin to stop his broadcasts. The pro-Nazi [German-American Bund,](https://www.britannica.com/topic/German-American-Bund) founded in 1933, staged military drills and mass rallies until it disintegrated with the U. S. entry into the [war](https://www.britannica.com/topic/war) in 1941.
