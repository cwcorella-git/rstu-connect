## ![](_page_2_Picture_5.jpeg)

## The stock exchange of Hendrick de Keyser

The **United East India Company** [\(Dutch:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_language) Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie [\[v](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Dutch)ərˈeːnɪɣd[ə](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Dutch) oːstˈɪ[ndis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Dutch)[ə](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Dutch) kɔ[mp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Dutch)ɑˈɲi]; abbr. **VOC** [veː[\(j\)o](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Dutch)ːˈseː]), commonly known as the **Dutch East**

**India Company**, was a [chartered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chartered_company) [trading company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trading_company) and one of the first [joint-stock companies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint-stock_companies) in the world[.\[3\]](#page-43-1)[\[4\]](#page-43-2) Established on 20 March 160[2\[5\]](#page-43-3) by the [States General of the Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_General_of_the_Netherlands) amalgamating [existing companies,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voorcompagnie) it was granted a 21-year [monopoly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monopoly) to carry out trade activities in Asia[.\[6\]](#page-43-4) Shares in the company could be purchased by any citizen of the [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Republic) [Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Republic) and bought and sold in open-air [secondary markets,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_markets) one of which became the [Amsterdam Stock Exchange.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amsterdam_Stock_Exchange) [\[7\]](#page-43-5) The company possessed quasi-governmental powers, including the ability to wage war, imprison and execute convicts,[\[8\]](#page-44-0) negotiate treaties, strike [its own coins,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coinage_of_the_Dutch_East_India_Company) and establish colonies. [\[9\]](#page-44-1) Because it traded across multiple colonies and countries from both the East and the West, the VOC is sometimes considered to have been the world's first [multinational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multinational_corporation) [corporation.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multinational_corporation) [\[10\]](#page-44-2)[\[11\]](#page-44-3)

Statistically, the VOC eclipsed all of its rivals in the Asian trade. Between 1602 and 1796, the VOC sent nearly a million Europeans to work in the Asia trade on 4,785 ships, and netted for their efforts more than 2.5 million tons of Asian trade goods and slaves. By contrast, the rest of Europe combined sent 882,412 people from 1500 to 1795. The fleet of the English, later British [East India Company,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_India_Company) the VOC's nearest competitor, was a distant second to its total traffic, with 2,690 ships and one-fifth the tonnage of goods carried by the VOC. The VOC enjoyed huge profits from its spice monopoly and slave trading activities through most of the 17th century.[\[12\]](#page-44-4) Having been established in 1602 to profit from the [Malukan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maluku_Islands)

spice trade, the VOC established a capital in the port city of [Jayakarta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jayakarta) in 1619 and changed its name to [Batavia,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batavia,_Dutch_East_Indies) now [Jakarta.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jakarta) Over the next two centuries, the company acquired additional ports as trading bases and safeguarded their interests by taking over surrounding territory[.\[13\]](#page-44-5) It remained an important trading concern and paid annual dividends that averaged to about 18% of the capital for almost 200 years.[\[14\]](#page-44-6) Weighed down by smuggling, corruption and growing administrative costs in the late 18th century, the company went bankrupt and was formally dissolved in 1799. Its possessions and debt were taken over by the government of the Dutch [Batavian Republic.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batavian_Republic)

In Dutch, the name of the company was the Vereenigde Nederlandsche Geoctroyeerde Oostindische Compagnie, abbreviated as the VOC, literally the 'United Dutch Chartered East India Company' (the United East India Company).[\[15\]](#page-44-7) The company's [monogram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monogram) logo consisted of a large capital 'V' with an O on the left and a C on the right half and was possibly the first globally recognised [corporate logo.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_logo) [\[16\]](#page-44-8) It appeared on corporate items, such as flags, cannons, and coins.[\[16\]](#page-44-8)[\[17\]](#page-45-0)

The first letter of the hometown of the chamber conducting the operation was placed on top. The monogram's versatility, flexibility, clarity, simplicity, symmetry, timelessness, and symbolism are considered notable characteristics of the VOC's professionally designed logo. Those elements ensured its success at a time when the concept of the [corporate identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_identity) was virtually unknown[.\[16\]](#page-44-8)[\[18\]](#page-45-1) An Australian [vintner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vintner) has used the VOC logo since the late 20th century, having re-registered the company's name for the purpose[.\[19\]](#page-45-2)

Around the world, and especially in English-speaking countries, the VOC is widely known as the 'Dutch East India Company'. The name 'Dutch East India Company' is used to make a distinction from the [British] [East India Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_India_Company) (EIC) and other East Indian companies, such as the [Danish East India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_East_India_Company) [Company,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_East_India_Company) [French East India Company,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_French_East_Indies_Company) [Portuguese East India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_East_India_Company)

[Company,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_East_India_Company) and the [Swedish East India Company.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_East_India_Company) The company's alternative names that have been used include the 'Dutch East Indies Company', 'United East India Company', 'Jan Company', or 'Jan Compagnie'[.\[20\]](#page-45-3)[\[21\]](#page-45-4)

Before the [Dutch Revolt,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Revolt) which began in 1566/68, the Flemish city of [Antwerp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antwerp) had played an important role as a distribution center in northern Europe. After 1591, the Portuguese used an international syndicate of the German [Fugger family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fugger_family) and [Welser family,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welser_family) as well as Spanish and Italian firms, which operated out of [Hamburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamburg) as the northern staple port to distribute their goods, thereby cutting Dutch merchants out of the trade. At the same time, the Portuguese trade system was unable to increase supply to satisfy growing demand, in particular the demand for pepper. Demand for spices was relatively [elastic;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Price_elasticity_of_demand) therefore, each lag in the supply of pepper caused a sharp rise in pepper prices.

In 1580, the [Portuguese crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Portugal) was [united](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_Union) in a [personal union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_union) with the [Spanish crown,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_crown) known as the [Iberian Union,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_Union) with which the [Dutch Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Republic) was at war. The [Portuguese Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_Empire) thus became an appropriate target for Dutch military incursions. These factors motivated Dutch merchants to enter the intercontinental spice trade themselves. Further, a number of Dutch merchants and explorers, such as [Jan Huyghen van](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Huyghen_van_Linschoten) [Linschoten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Huyghen_van_Linschoten) and [Cornelis de Houtman,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornelis_de_Houtman) obtained firsthand knowledge of the "secret" Portuguese trade routes and practices, providing further opportunity for the Dutch to enter the trade[.\[22\]](#page-45-5)

The stage was set for Dutch expeditions to the [Indonesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesia)

[islands,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesia) beginning with [James Lancaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Lancaster) in 1591, [Cornelis de](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornelis_de_Houtman) [Houtman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornelis_de_Houtman) in 1595 and in 1598, [Jacob Van Neck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_Corneliszoon_van_Neck) in 1598, Lancaster again in 1601, among others. During the four-ship exploratory expedition by [Frederick de Houtman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_de_Houtman) in 1595 to [Banten,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantam_(city)) the main pepper port of West Java, the crew clashed with Portuguese and indigenous Javanese. Houtman's expedition then sailed east along the north coast of [Java,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java) losing twelve crew members to a Javanese attack at Sidayu and killing a local ruler in [Madura.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madura) Half the crew were lost before the expedition made it back to the Netherlands in 1596, but with enough spices to make a considerable profit[.\[23\]](#page-45-6)

![](_page_7_Picture_3.jpeg)

The return of the [second Asia expedition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Dutch_Expedition_to_Indonesia) of [Jacob van Neck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_van_Neck) in 1599 by [Cornelis Vroom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornelis_Vroom)

In 1598, an increasing number of fleets were sent out by competing merchant groups from around the Netherlands. Some fleets were lost, but most were successful, with some voyages producing high profits. In 1598, a [fleet of eight ships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Dutch_Expedition_to_Indonesia) under [Jacob van Neck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_van_Neck) were the first Dutch fleet to reach the 'Spice Islands' of Maluku, also known as the Moluccas, cutting out the Javanese middlemen. The ships returned to Europe in 1599 and 1600 and the expedition made a 400 percent profit. [\[23\]](#page-45-6)

In 1600, the Dutch joined forces with the Muslim Hituese on [Ambon Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambon_Island) in an anti-Portuguese alliance, in return for which the Dutch were given the sole right to purchase spices from

Hitu. Dutch control of Ambon was achieved when the Portuguese surrendered their fort in Ambon to the Dutch-Hituese alliance. In 1613, the Dutch expelled the Portuguese from their [Solor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solor) fort, but a subsequent Portuguese attack led to a second change of hands. Following this second reoccupation, the Dutch again captured Solor in 1636[.\[24\]](#page-45-7)

East of [Solor,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solor) on the island of [Timor,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timor) Dutch advances were halted by an autonomous and powerful group of Portuguese Eurasians called the [Topasses.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topasses) They remained in control of the [Sandalwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandalwood) trade. Their resistance lasted throughout the 17th and 18th centuries, causing [Portuguese Timor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_Timor) to remain under the Portuguese sphere of control[.\[25\]](#page-45-8)[\[26\]](#page-45-9)

At the time, it was customary for a company to be funded only for the duration of a single voyage and to be liquidated upon the return of the fleet. Investment in these expeditions was a very high-risk venture, because of the usual dangers of piracy, disease and shipwreck, and because the interplay of [inelastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inelastic_demand) [demand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inelastic_demand) and relatively [elastic supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elastic_supply)[\[e\]](#page-41-3) of spices could make prices tumble, thereby ruining prospects of profitability. To manage such risk, the forming of a [cartel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartel) to control supply would seem logical. In 1600, the English were the first to adopt this approach by bundling their resources into a monopoly enterprise, the [English East India Company,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_India_Company) thereby threatening their Dutch competitors with ruin[.\[27\]](#page-45-10)

In 1602, the Dutch government followed suit, sponsoring the creation of a single "United East Indies Company" that was also granted monopoly over the Asian trade[.\[28\]](#page-46-0) For a time in the seventeenth century, it was able to monopolise the trade in nutmeg, mace, and cloves and to sell these spices across European kingdoms and Emperor [Akbar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akbar) the Great's [Mughal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire) [Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire) at 14–17 times the price it paid in [Indonesia.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesia) [\[29\]](#page-46-1)

While Dutch profits soared, the local economy of the Spice Islands was destroyed, because as a monopoly buyer, the VOC forced the prices paid to the local producers in the Spice Islands down to low levels. With a capital of 6,440,200 [guilders,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guilder)

[\[30\]](#page-46-2) the new company's charter empowered it to build forts, maintain armies, and conclude treaties with Asian rulers. It provided for a venture that continued for 21 years, with a financial accounting only at the end of each decade.[\[27\]](#page-45-10)

## ![](_page_10_Picture_3.jpeg)

Early map of the Maluku Islands made during the [Age of Discovery,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Discovery) by [Willem Blaeu,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willem_Blaeu) 1630. North is on the right, with [Ternate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_Ternate) as the rightmost followed by [Tidore,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_Tidore) both were known as the [Spice Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spice_Islands) (trade secret, outposts of the VOC).

In February 1603, the company seized the [Santa Catarina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Catarina_(ship)), a 1,500-ton Portuguese merchant [carrack,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrack) off the coast of the [Malay Peninsula.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malay_Peninsula) That same year, a joint Dutch-English force captured another Portuguese carrack off [Macau.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macau) [\[31\]](#page-46-3) The proceeds from the sale of the cargo of the two vessels was equivalent to more than 50% of the VOC's original subscription capital[.\[32\]](#page-46-4)

In 1603, the first permanent Dutch trading post in Indonesia was established in [Banten,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banten) [West Java.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Java) In 1611, another was established at [Jayakarta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jayakarta) (later "Batavia" and then "Jakarta"). [\[33\]](#page-46-5) In 1610, the VOC established the post of [governor-general](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governor-general_of_the_Dutch_East_Indies) to more firmly control their affairs in Asia. To advise and control the risk of [despotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Despotism) governors-general, a [Council of the Indies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_the_Indies_(Dutch)) (Raad van Indië) was created. The governor-general effectively became the main administrator of the VOC's activities in Asia. The Heeren XVII, a body of 17 shareholders representing different chambers, continued to officially have overall control. [\[24\]](#page-45-7)

![](_page_11_Picture_3.jpeg)

The Isle of Amboina, a [17th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/17th_century) print, probably English

The VOC headquarters were located in [Ambon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governorate_of_Ambon) during the tenures of the first three governors-general (1610–1619), but it was not a satisfactory location. Although it was at the centre of the spice production areas, it was far from the Asian trade routes and other VOC areas of activity ranging from Africa to India to Japan[.\[34\]](#page-46-6)[\[35\]](#page-47-0) A location in the west of the archipelago was thus sought. The Straits of Malacca were strategic but became dangerous following the Portuguese conquest, and the first permanent VOC settlement in Banten was controlled by a powerful local ruler and subject to stiff competition from Chinese and English traders[.\[24\]](#page-45-7)

In 1604, a second English [East India Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_India_Company) voyage commanded by [Sir Henry Middleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Henry_Middleton) reached the islands of [Ternate,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ternate) [Tidore,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidore) [Ambon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambon_Island) and [Banda.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banda_Islands) In Banda, they encountered severe VOC hostility, sparking Anglo-Dutch competition for access to spices. From 1611 to 1617, the English established trading posts at [Sukadana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sukadana) (southwest

[Kalimantan\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalimantan), [Makassar,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makassar) [Jayakarta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jayakarta) and [Jepara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jepara) in [Java,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java) and Aceh, [Pariaman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pariaman) and [Jambi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jambi) in [Sumatra,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumatra) which threatened Dutch ambitions for a monopoly on East Indies trade.[\[33\]](#page-46-5)

In 1620, diplomatic agreements in Europe ushered in a period of collaboration between the Dutch and English spice trades. [\[33\]](#page-46-5) This ended with the notorious [Amboyna massacre,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amboyna_massacre) where ten Englishmen were arrested, tried and beheaded for conspiracy against the Dutch government[.\[36\]](#page-47-1) Although this caused outrage in Europe and a diplomatic crisis, the English quietly withdrew from most of their Indonesian activities, except trading in Banten, and focused on other Asian interests.

## ![](_page_12_Picture_6.jpeg)

Dutch East India Company factory in [Hugli-Chuchura,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugli-Chuchura) [Mughal Bengal.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Bengal) Hendrik van Schuylenburgh, 1665.

In 1619, [Jan Pieterszoon Coen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Pieterszoon_Coen) was appointed governor-general of the VOC. He saw the possibility of the VOC becoming an

Asian power, both political and economic. On 30 May 1619, Coen, backed by a force of nineteen ships, stormed Jayakarta, driving out the Banten forces; and from the ashes established [Batavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batavia,_Dutch_East_Indies) as the VOC headquarters. In the 1620s almost the entire native population of the [Banda Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banda_Islands) was driven away, starved to death, or killed in an attempt to replace them with Dutch plantations[.\[37\]](#page-47-2) These plantations were used to grow [nutmeg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutmeg) for export. Coen hoped to settle large numbers of Dutch colonists in the East Indies, but implementation of this policy never materialised, mainly because very few Dutch were willing to emigrate to Asia[.\[38\]](#page-47-3)

Another of Coen's ventures was more successful. A major problem in the European trade with Asia at the time was that the Europeans could offer few goods that Asian consumers wanted, except silver and gold. European traders therefore had to pay for spices with the precious metals, which were in short supply in Europe, except for Spain and Portugal. The Dutch and English had to obtain it by creating a trade surplus with other European countries. Coen discovered the obvious solution for the problem: to start an intra-Asiatic trade system, whose profits could be used to finance the spice trade with Europe. In the long run this obviated the need for exports of precious metals from Europe, though at first it required the formation of a large trading-capital fund in the Indies. The VOC reinvested a large share of its profits to this end in the period up to 1630.[\[39\]](#page-47-4)

The VOC traded throughout Asia, benefiting mainly from [Bengal.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengal) Ships coming into Batavia from the Netherlands carried supplies for VOC settlements in Asia. Silver and copper from Japan were used to trade with the world's wealthiest empires, [Mughal India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_India) and [Qing China,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qing_China) for silk, cotton, porcelain, and textiles. These products were either traded within Asia for the coveted spices or brought back to Europe. The VOC was also instrumental in introducing European ideas and technology to Asia. The company supported Christian missionaries and traded modern technology with China and Japan. A more peaceful VOC trade post on [Dejima,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dejima) an [artificial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_island) [island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_island) off the coast of [Nagasaki,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagasaki,_Nagasaki) was for more than two hundred years the only place where Europeans were permitted to trade with [Japan.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan) [\[40\]](#page-47-5) When the VOC tried to use military force to make [Ming dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ming_dynasty) China open up to Dutch trade, the Chinese defeated the Dutch in [a war over the Penghu islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino%E2%80%93Dutch_conflicts) from 1623 to 1624, forcing the VOC to abandon [Penghu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penghu) for [Taiwan.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan) The Chinese defeated the VOC again at the [Battle of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Liaoluo_Bay) [Liaoluo Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Liaoluo_Bay) in 1633.

The Vietnamese [Nguyen lords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nguyen_lords) defeated the VOC in [a 1643](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Gianh_River_(1643)) [battle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Gianh_River_(1643)) during the Trị[nh–Nguy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tr%E1%BB%8Bnh%E2%80%93Nguy%E1%BB%85n_War)ễn War, blowing up a Dutch ship. The Cambodians defeated the VOC in the [Cambodian–Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambodian%E2%80%93Dutch_War) [War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambodian%E2%80%93Dutch_War) from 1643 to 1644 on the Mekong River.

## ![](_page_14_Picture_4.jpeg)

Dutch settlement in [Bengal Subah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengal_Subah)

In 1640, the VOC obtained the port of [Galle,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galle) [Ceylon,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Ceylon) from the Portuguese and broke the latter's monopoly of the [cinnamon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinnamon)

trade. In 1658, [Gerard Pietersz Hulft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerard_Pietersz_Hulft) laid siege to [Colombo,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colombo) which was captured with the help of King [Rajasinghe II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rajasinghe_II) of [Kandy.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kandy) By 1659, the Portuguese had been expelled from the coastal regions, which were then occupied by the VOC, securing for it the monopoly over cinnamon. To prevent the Portuguese or the English from ever recapturing [Sri Lanka,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sri_Lanka) the VOC went on to conquer the entire [Malabar Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Malabar) from the Portuguese, almost entirely driving them from the west coast of India.

In 1652, [Jan van Riebeeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_van_Riebeeck) established a resupply outpost at the Cape of Storms (the southwestern tip of Africa, now [Cape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Town) [Town,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Town) South Africa) to service company ships on their journey to and from East Asia. The cape was later renamed [Cape of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_of_Good_Hope) [Good Hope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_of_Good_Hope) in honour of the outpost's presence. Although noncompany ships were welcome to use the station, they were charged exorbitantly. This post later became a full-fledged colony, the [Cape Colony,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Cape_Colony) when more Dutch and other Europeans started to settle there.

Through the seventeenth century VOC trading posts were also established in [Persia,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persia) [Bengal,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Bengal) [Malacca,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Malacca) [Siam,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thailand) [Formosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Formosa) (now Taiwan), as well as the [Malabar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Malabar) and [Coromandel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Coromandel) coasts in India. Direct access to mainland China came in 1729 when a [factory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canton_Factories) was established in [Canton.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guangzhou) [\[41\]](#page-47-6) In 1662, however, [Koxinga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koxinga) expelled the Dutch from Taiwa[n\[42\]](#page-47-7) (see [History of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Taiwan) [Taiwan\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Taiwan).

In 1663, the VOC signed the "Painan Treaty" with several local lords in the [Painan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Painan) area that were revolting against the [Aceh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aceh_Sultanate) [Sultanate.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aceh_Sultanate) The treaty allowed the VOC to build a trading post in the area and eventually to monopolise the trade there, especially the gold trade[.\[43\]](#page-47-8)

By 1669, the VOC was the richest private company the world had ever seen, with over 150 merchant ships, 40 warships, 50,000 employees, a private army of 10,000 soldiers, and a [dividend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dividend) payment of 40% on the original investment[.\[f\]](#page-41-4)

Many of the VOC employees inter-mixed with the indigenous peoples and expanded the population of [Indos in pre-colonial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indos_in_pre-colonial_history) [history.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indos_in_pre-colonial_history) [\[44\]](#page-47-9)[\[45\]](#page-48-0)

Around 1670, two events caused the growth of VOC trade to stall. In the first place, the highly profitable trade with Japan started to decline. The loss of the outpost on Formosa to [Koxinga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koxinga) in the 1662 [siege of Fort Zeelandia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Fort_Zeelandia) and related internal turmoil in [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China) (where the [Ming dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ming_dynasty) was being replaced with the China's [Qing dynasty\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qing_dynasty) brought an end to the silk trade after 1666. Though the VOC substituted [Mughal Bengal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Bengal)'s for Chinese silk, other forces affected the supply of Japanese silver and gold. The [shogunate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shogunate) enacted a number of measures to limit the export of these precious metals, in the process limiting VOC opportunities for trade, and severely worsening the terms of trade. Therefore, Japan ceased to function as the linchpin of the intra-Asiatic trade of the VOC by 1685.[\[46\]](#page-48-1)

Even more importantly, the [Third Anglo-Dutch War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Anglo-Dutch_War) temporarily interrupted VOC trade with Europe. This caused a spike in the price of pepper, which enticed the English East India Company (EIC) to enter this market aggressively in the years after 1672. Previously, one of the tenets of the VOC pricing policy was to slightly over-supply the pepper market, so as to depress prices below the level where interlopers were encouraged to enter the market (instead of striving for short-term [profit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Profit_maximization) [maximisation\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Profit_maximization). The wisdom of such a policy was illustrated when a fierce price war with the EIC ensued, as that company flooded the market with new supplies from India. In this struggle for market share, the VOC (which had much larger financial resources) could wait out the EIC. Indeed, by 1683, the latter came close to bankruptcy; its share price plummeted from 600 to 250; and its president [Josiah Child](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josiah_Child) was temporarily forced from office[.\[47\]](#page-48-2)

However, the writing was on the wall. Other companies, like the [French East India Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_XIV%27s_East_India_Company) and the [Danish East India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_East_India_Company) [Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_East_India_Company) also started to make inroads on the Dutch system. The VOC therefore closed the theretofore flourishing open pepper emporium of [Bantam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantam_(city)) by a treaty of 1684 with the Sultan. Also, on the [Coromandel Coast,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Coromandel) it moved its chief stronghold from [Pulicat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulicat) to [Nagapattinam,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagapattinam) so as to secure a monopoly on the pepper trade to the detriment of the French and the Danes[.\[g\]](#page-42-0) However, the importance of these traditional commodities in the Asian-European trade was diminishing rapidly at the time. The military outlays that the VOC needed to make to enhance its monopoly were not justified by the increased profits of this declining trade[.\[49\]](#page-48-3)

Nevertheless, this lesson was slow to sink in and at first the VOC made the strategic decision to improve its military position on the [Malabar Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Malabar) (hoping thereby to curtail English influence in the area, and end the drain on its resources from the cost of the Malabar garrisons) by using force to compel the [Zamorin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saamoothiri) of [Calicut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kozhikode) to submit to Dutch domination. In 1710, the Zamorin was made to sign a treaty with the VOC undertaking to trade exclusively with the VOC and expel other European traders. For a brief time, this appeared to improve the company's prospects. However, in 1715, with EIC encouragement, the Zamorin renounced the treaty. Though a Dutch army managed to suppress this insurrection temporarily, the Zamorin continued to trade with the English and the French, which led to an appreciable upsurge in English and French traffic. The VOC decided in 1721 that it was no longer worth the trouble to try to dominate the [Malabar pepper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malabar_pepper) and spice trade. A strategic decision was taken to scale down the Dutch military presence and in effect yield the area to EIC influence[.\[50\]](#page-48-4)

![](_page_18_Picture_3.jpeg)

[Eustachius De Lannoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eustachius_De_Lannoy) of the Dutch East India Company surrenders to Maharaja [Marthanda Varma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marthanda_Varma) of the Indian [Kingdom of Travancore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Travancore) after the [Battle of Colachel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Colachel) (depiction at [Padmanabhapuram Palace\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Padmanabhapuram_Palace).

In the 1741 [Battle of Colachel,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Colachel) warriors of [Travancore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travancore) under Raja [Marthanda Varma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marthanda_Varma) defeated the Dutch. The Dutch commander Captain [Eustachius De Lannoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eustachius_De_Lannoy) was captured. Marthanda Varma agreed to spare the Dutch captain's life on condition that he joined his army and trained his soldiers on modern lines. This defeat in the [Travancore–Dutch War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travancore%E2%80%93Dutch_War) is considered the earliest example of an organised Asian power overcoming European military technology and tactics; and it signalled the decline of Dutch power in India.[\[h\]](#page-42-1)

The attempt to continue as before as a low volume-high profit business enterprise with its core business in the spice trade had therefore failed. The company had however already (reluctantly) followed the example of its European competitors in diversifying into other Asian commodities, like tea, coffee, cotton, textiles, and sugar. These commodities provided a lower profit margin and therefore required a larger sales volume to generate the same amount of revenue. This structural change in the commodity composition of the VOC's trade started in the early 1680s, after the temporary collapse of the EIC around 1683 offered an excellent opportunity to enter these markets. The actual cause for the change lies, however, in two structural features of this new era.

In the first place, there was a revolutionary change in the tastes affecting European demand for Asian textiles, coffee and tea, around the turn of the 18th century. Secondly, a new era of an abundant supply of capital at low interest rates suddenly opened around this time. The second factor enabled the company easily to finance its expansion in the new areas of commerce[.\[i\]](#page-42-2) Between the 1680s and 1720s, the VOC was therefore able to equip and man an appreciable expansion of its fleet, and acquire a large amount of precious metals to finance the purchase of large amounts of Asian commodities, for shipment to Europe. The overall effect was approximately to double the size of the company[.\[53\]](#page-48-5)

The tonnage of the returning ships rose by 125 percent in this period. However, the company's revenues from the sale of goods landed in Europe rose by only 78 percent. This reflects the basic change in the VOC's circumstances that had occurred: it now operated in new markets for goods with an elastic demand, in which it had to compete on an equal footing with other suppliers. This made for low profit margins.[\[54\]](#page-48-6) The business information systems of the time made this difficult to discern for the managers of the company, which may partly explain the mistakes they made from hindsight. This lack of information might have been counteracted (as in earlier times in the VOC's history) by the business acumen of the directors. By this time these were almost exclusively recruited from the political [regent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regenten) class, which had long since lost its close relationship with merchant circles[.\[55\]](#page-48-7)

Low profit margins in themselves do not explain the deterioration of revenues. To a large extent the costs of the operation of the VOC had a "fixed" character (military establishments; maintenance of the fleet and such). Profit levels might therefore have been maintained if the increase in the scale of trading operations that in fact took place had resulted in [economies of scale.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economies_of_scale) However, though larger ships transported the growing volume of goods, labour productivity did not go up sufficiently to realise these. In general the company's overhead rose in step with the growth in trade volume; declining gross margins translated directly into a decline in profitability of the invested capital. The era of expansion was one of "profitless growth"[.\[56\]](#page-48-8)

Specifically: "[t]he long-term average annual profit in the VOC's 1630–70 'Golden Age' was 2.1 million guilders, of which just under half was distributed as dividends and the remainder reinvested. The long-term average annual profit in the 'Expansion Age' (1680–1730) was 2.0 million guilders, of which three-quarters was distributed as dividend and one-quarter reinvested. In the earlier period, profits averaged 18 percent of total revenues; in the latter period, 10 percent. The annual return of invested capital in the earlier period stood at approximately 6 percent; in the latter period, 3.4 percent."[\[56\]](#page-48-8)

Nevertheless, in the eyes of investors the VOC did not do too badly. The share price hovered consistently around the 400 mark from the mid-1680s (excepting a hiccup around the [Glorious Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glorious_Revolution) in 1688), and they reached an all-time high of around 642 in the 1720s. VOC shares then yielded a return of 3.5 percent, only slightly less than the yield on Dutch government bonds[.\[57\]](#page-48-9)

## ![](_page_22_Picture_2.jpeg)

The British East Indiaman [General Goddard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Goddard_(1782_ship)) capturing seven VOC East Indiamen on 14 June 1795

After 1730, the fortunes of the VOC started to decline. Five major contributing factors are attributed to its decay in the 50 years between 1730 and 1780[:\[58\]](#page-48-10)

- There was a steady erosion of intra-Asiatic trade because of changes in the Asiatic political and economic environment that the VOC could do little about.[[specify](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources)] These factors gradually squeezed the company out of Persia, [Suratte,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Suratte) the Malabar Coast, and Bengal. The company had to confine its operations to the belt it physically controlled, from Ceylon through the Indonesian archipelago. The volume of this intra-Asiatic trade, and its profitability, therefore had to shrink.
- The way the company was organised in Asia (centralised on its hub in Batavia), which initially had offered advantages in gathering market information, began to cause disadvantages in the 18th century because of the inefficiency of first shipping everything to this central point. This disadvantage was most keenly felt in the tea trade, where competitors like the EIC and the [Ostend Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ostend_Company) shipped directly from China to Europe.
- The "venality" of the VOC's personnel (in the sense of corruption and non-performance of duties), though a problem for all East India Companies at the time, seems to have plagued the VOC on a larger scale than its competitors. To be sure, the company was not a "good employer". Salaries were low, and "private-account trading" was officially not allowed. It proliferated in the 18th century to the detriment of the company's performance[.\[j\]](#page-42-3) From about the 1790s onward, the phrase perished under corruption (vergaan onder corruptie, also abbreviated VOC in Dutch) came to summarise the company's future.

- A problem that the VOC shared with other companies was the high mortality and morbidity rates among its employees. This decimated the company's ranks and enervated many of the survivors.
- A further issue was caused by the VOC's [dividend policy.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dividend_policy) The dividends distributed by the company had exceeded the surplus it garnered in Europe in every decade from 1690 to 1760 except 1710–1720. However, in the period up to 1730 the directors shipped resources to Asia to build up the trading capital there. Consolidated bookkeeping therefore probably would have shown that total profits exceeded dividends. In addition, between 1700 and 1740 the company retired 5.4 million guilders of long-term debt. The company therefore was still on a secure financial footing in these years. This changed after 1730. While profits plummeted the bewindhebbers only slightly decreased dividends from the earlier level. Distributed dividends were therefore in excess of earnings in every decade but one (1760–1770). To accomplish this, the Asian capital stock had to be drawn down by 4 million guilders between 1730 and 1780, and the [liquid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_liquidity) capital available in Europe was reduced by 20 million guilders in the same period. The directors were therefore constrained to replenish the company's liquidity by resorting to short-term financing from anticipatory loans, backed by expected revenues from home-bound fleets[.\[59\]](#page-48-11)

Despite these problems, the VOC in 1780 remained an enormous operation. Its capital in the Republic, consisting of ships and goods in inventory, totalled 28 million guilders; its capital in Asia, consisting of the liquid trading fund and goods en route to Europe, totalled 46 million guilders. Total capital, net of outstanding debt, stood at 62 million guilders. The prospects of the company at this time therefore were not hopeless, had one of the plans for reform been undertaken successfully. However, the [Fourth Anglo-Dutch War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Anglo-Dutch_War) intervened. British naval attacks in Europe and Asia reduced the VOC fleet by half; removed valuable cargo from its control; and eroded its remaining power in Asia. The direct losses of the VOC during the war can be calculated at 43 million guilders. Loans to keep the company operating reduced its net assets to zero.[\[60\]](#page-49-0)

From 1720 on, the market for sugar from Indonesia declined as the competition from cheap sugar from [Brazil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazil) increased. European markets became saturated. Dozens of Chinese sugar traders went bankrupt, which led to massive unemployment, which in turn led to gangs of unemployed [coolies.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coolies) The Dutch government in Batavia did not adequately respond to these problems. In 1740, rumours of deportation of the gangs from the Batavia area led to widespread rioting. The Dutch military searched houses of Chinese in Batavia for weapons. When a house accidentally burnt down, military and impoverished citizens started slaughtering and pillaging the Chinese community[.\[61\]](#page-49-1) This [massacre of the Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1740_Batavia_massacre) was deemed sufficiently serious for the board of the VOC to start an official investigation into the Government of the Dutch East Indies for the first time in its history.

After the [Fourth Anglo-Dutch War,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Anglo-Dutch_War) the VOC's financial issues worsened considerably. After vain attempts at reorganisation by the provincial States of [Holland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holland) and [Zeeland,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeeland) the board of directors in the VOC were sacked in 1796 and the company's management was handed over to a Committee for Affairs relating to East India Trade and Possessions (Dutch: Comité tot de zaken van de Oost-Indische handel en bezittingen).[\[62\]](#page-49-2)[\[63\]](#page-49-3) The VOC charter was renewed several times, but was allowed to expire on 31 December 1799[.\[64\]](#page-49-4) Most of the possessions of the former VOC were subsequently occupied by Great Britain during the [Napoleonic wars,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_wars) but after the new [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_of_the_Netherlands) [of the Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_of_the_Netherlands) was created by the [Congress of Vienna,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_of_Vienna) some of these were restored to this successor state of the Dutch Republic by the [Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1814.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Dutch_Treaty_of_1814)

## **Organisational structure**

### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dutch_East_India_Company&action=edit§ion=8)

## ![](_page_25_Picture_6.jpeg)

The **Gentlemen Seventeen**, in the Directors' Room at the [East India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_India_House_(Amsterdam))

[House,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_India_House_(Amsterdam)) Amsterdam.

While the VOC mainly operated in what later became the [Dutch East Indies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_East_Indies) (modern Indonesia), the company also had important operations elsewhere. It employed people from different continents and origins in the same functions and working environments. Although it was a Dutch company, its employees included not only people from the Netherlands, but also many from Germany and other countries. Besides the diverse north-west European [workforce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Workforce) recruited by the VOC in the [Dutch Republic,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Republic) the VOC made extensive use of local Asian labour markets. As a result, the personnel of the various VOC offices in Asia consisted of European and Asian employees. Asian or [Eurasian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasian) workers could be employed as sailors, soldiers, writers, carpenters, smiths, or as simple unskilled workers[.\[65\]](#page-49-5) At the height of its existence, the VOC had 25,000 employees who worked in Asia and 11,000 who were en route. [\[66\]](#page-49-6) Also, while most of its [shareholders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shareholder) were Dutch, about a quarter of the initial shareholders were Zuid-Nederlanders (people from an area that includes modern Belgium and [Luxembourg\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luxembourg), and there were also a few dozen Germans[.\[67\]](#page-49-7)

The VOC had two types of shareholders: the participanten, who could be seen as non-managing members, and the 76 bewindhebbers (later reduced to 60) who acted as managing directors. This was the usual set-up for Dutch [joint-stock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint-stock_company) [companies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint-stock_company) at the time. The innovation in the case of the VOC was that the liability of not just the participanten but also of the bewindhebbers was limited to the paid-in capital (usually, bewindhebbers had unlimited liability). The VOC therefore was a [limited liability company.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limited_company) Also, the capital would be permanent during the lifetime of the company. As a consequence, investors that wished to liquidate their interest in the interim could only do this by selling their share to others on the [Amsterdam Stock Exchange.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euronext_Amsterdam) [\[68\]](#page-49-8) Confusion of confusions, a 1688 dialogue by the Sephardi Jew [Joseph de la](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_de_la_Vega) [Vega](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_de_la_Vega) analysed the workings of this one-stock exchange.

The VOC consisted of six Chambers (Kamers) in port cities: [Amsterdam,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amsterdam) [Delft,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delft) [Rotterdam,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotterdam) [Enkhuizen,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enkhuizen) [Middelburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middelburg,_Zeeland) and [Hoorn.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoorn) Delegates of these chambers convened as the Heeren XVII (the Lords Seventeen). They were selected from the bewindhebber-class of shareholders[.\[27\]](#page-45-10)

Of the Heeren XVII, eight delegates were from the Chamber of Amsterdam (one short of a majority on its own), four from the Chamber of Zeeland, and one from each of the smaller Chambers, while the seventeenth seat was alternatively from the Chamber of Middelburg-Zeeland or rotated among the five small Chambers. Amsterdam had thereby the decisive voice. The Zeelanders in particular had misgivings about this arrangement at the beginning. The fear was not unfounded, because in practice it meant Amsterdam stipulated what happened.

## Various VOC soldier uniforms, c. 1783

Among the early shareholders of the VOC, immigrants played an important role. Under the 1,143 tenderers were 39 [Germans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_people) and no fewer than 301 from the [Southern Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Netherlands) (roughly present Belgium and Luxembourg, then under [Habsburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habsburg) rule), of whom [Isaac le Maire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_le_Maire) was the largest subscriber with ƒ85,000. VOC's total [capitalisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_capitalization) was ten times that of its British rival.

The Heeren XVII (Lords Seventeen) met alternately six years in

Amsterdam and two years in Middelburg-Zeeland. They defined the VOC's general policy and divided the tasks among the Chambers. The Chambers carried out all the necessary work, built their own ships and warehouses and traded the merchandise. The Heeren XVII sent the ships' masters off with extensive instructions on the route to be navigated, prevailing winds, currents, shoals and landmarks. The VOC also produced its own [charts.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nautical_charts)

In the context of the [Dutch–Portuguese War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch%E2%80%93Portuguese_War) the company established its headquarters in Batavia, [Java](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java_island) (now [Jakarta,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jakarta) [Indonesia\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesia). Other colonial outposts were also established in the [East Indies,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Indies) such as on the [Maluku Islands,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maluku_Islands) which include the [Banda Islands,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banda_Islands) where the VOC forcibly maintained a monopoly over [nutmeg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutmeg) and [mace.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mace_(spice)) Methods used to maintain the monopoly involved [extortion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extortion) and the violent suppression of the native population, including [mass murder.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_murder) [\[70\]](#page-49-10) In addition, VOC representatives sometimes used the tactic of burning spice trees to force indigenous populations to grow other crops, thus artificially cutting the supply of spices like nutmeg and cloves. [\[71\]](#page-49-11)

## ![](_page_30_Picture_2.jpeg)

Both sides of a [duit,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duit) a [coin minted in 1735 by the VOC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coinage_of_the_Dutch_East_India_Company)

The seventeenth-century Dutch businessmen, especially the VOC investors, were possibly history's first recorded investors to seriously consider the problems of [corporate governance.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_governance) [\[72\]](#page-50-0)[\[73\]](#page-50-1) [Isaac Le Maire,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Le_Maire) who is known as history's first recorded [short seller,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_seller) was also a sizeable shareholder of the VOC. In 1609, he complained of the VOC's poor corporate governance. On 24 January 1609, Le Maire filed a petition against the VOC, marking the first recorded expression of [shareholder activism.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shareholder_activism) In what is the first recorded corporate governance dispute, Le Maire formally charged that the VOC's board of directors (the Heeren XVII) sought to "retain another's money for longer or use it ways other than the latter wishes" and petitioned for the liquidation of the VOC in accordance with standard business practice[.\[74\]](#page-50-2)[\[75\]](#page-50-3)[\[76\]](#page-50-4) Initially the largest single shareholder in the VOC and a bewindhebber sitting on the board of governors, Le Maire apparently attempted to divert the firm's profits to himself by undertaking 14 expeditions under his own accounts instead of those of the company. Since his large shareholdings were not accompanied by greater voting power, Le Maire was soon ousted by other governors in 1605 on charges of embezzlement, and was forced to sign an agreement not to compete with the VOC. Having retained stock in the company following this incident, in 1609 Le Maire would become the author of what is celebrated as "first recorded expression of shareholder advocacy at a publicly traded company".[\[77\]](#page-50-5)[\[78\]](#page-50-6) [\[79\]](#page-51-0)

In 1622, the history's first recorded [shareholder revolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shareholder_revolt) also happened among the VOC investors who complained that the company account books had been "smeared with bacon" so that they might be "eaten by dogs." The investors demanded a "reeckeninge," a proper financial audit[.\[80\]](#page-51-1) The 1622 campaign by the [shareholders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shareholder) of the VOC is a testimony of genesis of [corporate social responsibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_social_responsibility) (CSR) in which shareholders staged protests by distributing pamphlets and complaining about management self enrichment and secrecy[.\[81\]](#page-51-2)

## **Main trading posts, settlements, and colonies**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dutch_East_India_Company&action=edit§ion=10)

The company's global headquarters were in [Amsterdam,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amsterdam) with other posts in the Dutch cities of [Delft,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delft) [Enkhuizen,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enkhuizen) [Hoorn,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoorn) [Middelburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middelburg,_Zeeland) and [Rotterdam.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotterdam) In Africa, the trading posts and colonies were in [Dutch Mauritius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Mauritius) in 1638-1658 and 1664–1710, and in the [Dutch Cape Colony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Cape_Colony) between 1652 and 1806. The South Asian and Indonesian posts and colonies of the company were in [Batavia, Dutch East Indies,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batavia,_Dutch_East_Indies) [Dutch Coromandel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Coromandel) in 1806– 1825, [Dutch Suratte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Suratte) in 1616-1825, [Dutch Bengal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Bengal) from 1827 to 1825, [Dutch Ceylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Ceylon) from 1640 to 1796, and [Dutch Malabar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Malabar) from 1661 to 1795.

The Japanese post was in [Hirado, Nagasaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hirado,_Nagasaki) from 1609 to 1641, but was relocated to [Dejima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dejima) from 1641 to 1853. In Taiwan, the company had bases in [Anping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anping,_Tainan) [\(Fort Zeelandia\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Zeelandia_(Taiwan)), [Tainan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tainan) [\(Fort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Provincia) [Provincia\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Provincia), [Wang-an, Penghu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wang-an,_Penghu) on the [Pescadores Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pescadores_Islands) (Fort Vlissingen; 1620–1624), [Keelung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keelung) [\(Fort Noord-Holland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santisima_Trinidad_(Taiwan)) and Fort Victoria) and [Tamsui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamsui) [\(Fort Antonio\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Antonio).

In Malaysia, the company was based in [Dutch Malacca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Malacca) in 1641-1795 and 1818–1825. In Thailand, the company was based in [Ayutthaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayutthaya_Kingdom) in 1608–1767. In Vietnam, the company was based in [Hanoi/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanoi)Tonkin in 1636–1699, and in Hộ[i An](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%E1%BB%99i_An) in 1636–1741.

The VOC's history (and especially its dark side) has always been a potential source of controversy. In 2006 when the [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Prime_Minister) [Prime Minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Prime_Minister) [Jan Pieter Balkenende](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Pieter_Balkenende) referred to the pioneering entrepreneurial spirit and [work ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Work_ethic) of the [Dutch people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_people) and [Dutch Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Republic) in [their Golden Age,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Golden_Age) he coined the term "VOC mentality" ([VOC-mentaliteit](https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/VOC-mentaliteit) in Dutch)[.\[l\]](#page-43-7)

For Balkenende, the VOC represented Dutch [business acumen,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_acumen) [entrepreneurship,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entrepreneurship) adventurous spirit, and decisiveness. However, it unleashed a wave of criticism, since such romantic views about the Dutch Golden Age ignores the inherent historical associations with colonialism, exploitation and violence. Balkenende later stressed that "it had not been his intention to refer to that at all"[.\[85\]](#page-51-5) But in spite of criticisms, the "VOC-mentality", as a characteristic of the selective historical perspective on the Dutch Golden Age, has been considered a key feature of Dutch cultural policy for many years.[\[85\]](#page-51-5)

The company has been criticised for the results of its quasiabsolute commercial monopoly, [colonialism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonialism) [exploitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exploitation_of_labour) (including use of [slave labour\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slave_labour), [slave trade,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_slavery) use of violence, [environmental destruction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_destruction) (including [deforestation\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation), and for its overly [bureaucratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureaucratic) organisational structure[.\[86\]](#page-51-6)

Batavia, corresponding to present day Jakarta, was the headquarters of the Dutch East India Company, and had a strict social hierarchy in the colony. According to Marsely L. Kahoe in The Journal of Historians of Netherlandish Art, "it is misleading to understand Batavia, as some scholars have, as representing a pragmatic and egalitarian order that was later corrupted by the colonial situation. In fact, the social stratification and segregation of Batavia derived in certain ways directly from its Dutch plan.["\[87\]](#page-52-0)

There was an extraordinarily high mortality rate among employees of the VOC due to shipwrecks, illnesses such as scurvy and dysentery, and clashes with rival trading companies and pirates[.\[88\]](#page-52-1) Between 1602 and 1795, about one million seamen and craftsmen departed from Holland, but only 340,000 returned. J. L. van Zanden writes that "the VOC 'consumed' approximately 4,000 people per year.["\[89\]](#page-52-2)

#### **Colonialism, monopoly and violence**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dutch_East_India_Company&action=edit§ion=13)

Your Honours know by experience that trade in Asia must be driven and maintained under the protection and favour of Your Honours' own weapons, and that the weapons must be paid for by the profits from the trade; so that we cannot carry on trade without war nor war without trade.

The VOC charter allowed it to act as a quasi-sovereign state and engaged in brutal conquests[.\[91\]](#page-52-3) One example is the [Dutch conquest of the Banda Islands,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_conquest_of_the_Banda_Islands) between 1609 and 1621, after the islands resisted the nutmeg monopoly. The Dutch launched punitive expeditions that resulted in the neardestruction of Bandanese society[.\[92\]](#page-52-4) They invaded the main [Bandanese island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banda_Islands) of [Lontor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lontor_(island)) in 1621. 2,800 Bandanese were killed, mostly from famine, and 1,700 were enslaved during the attack[.\[93\]](#page-52-5)

The total population of the islands was estimated at 15,000 people before the conquest. Although the exact number remains uncertain, it is estimated that around 14,000 people were killed, enslaved or fled elsewhere, with only 1,000 Bandanese surviving in the islands, and were spread throughout the nutmeg groves as forced labourers[.\[94\]](#page-53-0) The treatment of slaves was harsh and the native Bandanese population dropped to 1,000 by 1681[.\[91\]](#page-52-3) 200 slaves were imported annually to sustain the slave population at a total of 4,000[.\[95\]](#page-53-1)

In 1623 on [Ambon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambon_Island) (Amboina), VOC officials arrested, tortured, and executed ten English East India Company employees along with Japanese and Portuguese auxiliaries for an alleged conspiracy, an episode known as the [Amboyna massacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amboyna_massacre) that ended Anglo-Dutch cooperation in the Moluccas and showcased the Company's reliance on judicial coercion to police its monopolies[.\[96\]\[](#page-53-2)page [needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources)]

In 1740, amid a severe downturn in Batavia's sugar economy, VOC troops and allied militias killed thousands of ethnic Chinese residents; scholarly estimates range from about 5,000 to over 10,000 deaths in what became known as the [Batavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1740_Batavia_massacre) [massacre.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1740_Batavia_massacre) [\[97\]](#page-53-3)

Earlier, in 1636, VOC forces mounted a punitive expedition on [Lamey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamey_Island_Massacre) (Lamay) Island off Taiwan after shipwreck incidents; most inhabitants were killed or deported and the island was effectively depopulated[.\[98\]](#page-53-4)

## **Dutch slave trade and slavery under the VOC colonial rule**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dutch_East_India_Company&action=edit§ion=14)

By the time the settlement was established at the [Cape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Cape_Colony) in 1652, the VOC already had a long experience of practicing [slavery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery) in the [East Indies.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Indies) [Jan van Riebeeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_van_Riebeeck) concluded within two months of the establishment of the Cape settlement that [slave labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slave_labor) would be needed for the hardest and dirtiest work.

Initially, the VOC considered enslaving men from the indigenous [Khoikhoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khoikhoi) population, but the idea was rejected on the grounds that such a policy would be both costly and dangerous. Most Khoikhoi had chosen not to labor for the Dutch because of low wages and harsh conditions. In the beginning, the settlers traded with the Khoikhoi, but the harsh working conditions and low wages imposed by the Dutch led to a series of wars. The European population remained under 200 during the settlement's first five years, and war against neighbors numbering more than 20,000 would have been foolhardy. Moreover, the Dutch feared that Khoikhoi people, if enslaved, could always escape into the local community, whereas foreigners would find it much more difficult to escape and survive or avoid recapture[.\[99\]](#page-53-5)

Between 1652 and 1657, a number of unsuccessful attempts were made to obtain men from the [Dutch East Indies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_East_Indies) and from [Mauritius.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mauritius) In 1658, however, the VOC landed two shiploads of slaves at the Cape, one containing more than 200 people brought from [Dahomey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahomey) (later Benin), the second with almost 200 people, most of them children, captured from a Portuguese slaver off the coast of Angola. Except for a few individuals, these were to be the only slaves ever brought to the Cape from West Africa[.\[99\]](#page-53-5) From 1658 to the end of the company's rule, many more slaves were brought regularly to the Cape in various ways, chiefly by Company-sponsored slaving voyages and slaves brought to the Cape by its return fleets. From these sources and by natural growth, the slave population increased from zero in 1652 to about 1,000 by 1700. During the 18th century, the slave population increased dramatically to 16,839 by 1795. After the slave trade was initiated, all of the slaves imported into the Cape until the British stopped the trade in 1807 were from East Africa, Mozambique, Madagascar, and South and Southeast Asia. Large numbers were brought from [Ceylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Ceylon) and the Indonesian archipelago. Prisoners from other countries in the VOC's empire were also enslaved. The slave population, which exceeded that of the European settlers until the first quarter of the nineteenth century, was overwhelmingly male and was thus dependent on constant imports of new slaves to maintain and to augment its size[.\[99\]](#page-53-5)

By the 1660s the Cape settlement was importing slaves from Ceylon, Malaya (Malaysia), and Madagascar to work on the farms[.\[100\]](#page-53-6) Conflict between Dutch farmers and Khoikhoi broke out once it became clear to the latter that the Dutch were there to stay and that they intended to encroach on the lands of the pastoralists. In 1659 Doman, a Khoikhoi who had worked as a translator for the Dutch and had even traveled to Java, led an armed attempt to expel the Dutch from the Cape peninsula. The attempt was a failure, although warfare dragged on until an inconclusive peace was established a year later. During the following decade, pressure on the Khoikhoi grew as more of the Dutch became free burghers, expanded their landholdings, and sought pastureland for their growing herds. War broke out again in 1673 and continued until 1677, when Khoikhoi resistance was destroyed by a combination of superior European weapons and Dutch manipulation of divisions among the local people. Thereafter, Khoikhoi society in the western Cape disintegrated. Some people found jobs as shepherds on

European farms; others rejected foreign rule and moved away from the Cape. The final blow for most came in 1713 when a Dutch ship brought smallpox to the Cape. Hitherto unknown locally, the disease ravaged the remaining Khoikhoi, killing 90 percent of the population[.\[99\]](#page-53-5) Throughout the eighteenth century, the settlement continued to expand through internal growth of the European population and the continued importation of slaves. The approximately 3,000 Europeans and slaves at the Cape in 1700 had increased by the end of the century to nearly 20,000 Europeans, and approximately 25,000 slaves[.\[99\]](#page-53-5)

## **Archives and records**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dutch_East_India_Company&action=edit§ion=15)

The VOC's operations (trading posts and colonies) produced not only warehouses packed with spices, coffee, tea, textiles, porcelain and silk, but also shiploads of documents. Data on political, economic, cultural, religious, and social conditions spread over an enormous area circulated between the VOC establishments, the administrative centre of the trade in [Batavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batavia,_Dutch_East_Indies) (modern-day [Jakarta\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jakarta), and the board of directors (the [Heeren XVII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heeren_XVII) [\[nl\]](https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heren_XVII)/Gentlemen Seventeen) in the Dutch Republic. [\[101\]](#page-53-7) Twenty-five million pages of VOC records survive, held in Jakarta; [Colombo,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colombo) Sri Lanka; [Chennai,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chennai) India; [Cape Town,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Town) South Africa; and [The Hague,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hague) the Netherlands. In 2003, [UNESCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNESCO) added this collection of records to its [Memory of the World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory_of_the_World_Programme) [international register.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory_of_the_World_Programme) [\[102\]](#page-54-0)
