## ![](_page_1_Picture_6.jpeg)

The "**Battle Hymn of the Republic**" is an [American patriotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_patriotic_music) [song](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_patriotic_music) written by the [abolitionist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionist) writer [Julia Ward Howe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_Ward_Howe) during the [American Civil War.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War)

Howe adapted her song from the soldiers' song ["John Brown's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Brown%27s_Body) [Body"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Brown%27s_Body) in November 1861, and sold it for \$4 to [The Atlantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Atlantic_Monthly) [Monthly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Atlantic_Monthly)[\[1\]](#page-22-0) in February 1862. In contrast to the lyrics of the soldiers' song, her version links the Union cause with God's vengeance at the [Day of Judgment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Day_of_Judgment) (through allusions to biblical passages such as [Isaiah 63:1-6,](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Bible_(American_Standard)/Isaiah#63:1) [Revelation 19](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revelation_19) and [Revelation 14:14-19\)](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Bible_(American_Standard)/Revelation#14:14).

Julia Ward Howe was married to [Samuel Gridley Howe,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Gridley_Howe) a scholar in education of the blind. Both Samuel and Julia were also active leaders in anti-slavery politics and strong supporters of the Union. Samuel was a member of the [Secret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secret_Six)

[Six,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secret_Six) the group who funded [John Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Brown_(abolitionist))'s work[.\[2\]](#page-22-1)

The tune and some of the lyrics of "John Brown's Body" came from a much older folk [hymn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hymn) called "Say, Brothers will you Meet Us", also known as "Glory Hallelujah", which has been developed in the [oral hymn tradition of revivalist camp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camp_meeting#Music_and_hymn_singing) [meetings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camp_meeting#Music_and_hymn_singing) of the late 1700s, though it was first published in the early 1800s. In the first known version, "Canaan's Happy Shore", the text includes the verse "Oh! Brothers will you meet me (3×)/On [Canaan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canaan)'s happy shore?["\[3\]:](#page-22-2)21 and chorus "There we'll shout and give Him glory (3×)/For glory is His own."[\[4\]](#page-22-3) This developed into the familiar "Glory, glory, hallelujah" chorus by the 1850s. The tune and variants of these words spread across both the southern and northern [United States.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_the_United_States) [\[5\]](#page-22-4)

### **As the "John Brown's Body" song**

# [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Battle_Hymn_of_the_Republic&action=edit§ion=3)

At a flag-raising ceremony at [Fort Warren,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Warren_(Massachusetts)) near Boston, Massachusetts, on Sunday, May 12, 1861, the song ["John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Brown%27s_Body) [Brown's Body"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Brown%27s_Body), using the "Oh! Brothers" tune and the "Glory, Hallelujah" chorus, was publicly played "perhaps for the first time".[[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)] The [American Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War) had begun the previous month.

In 1890, George Kimball wrote his account of how the [2nd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/43rd_Massachusetts_Infantry_Regiment) [Infantry Battalion of the Massachusetts militia,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/43rd_Massachusetts_Infantry_Regiment) known as the "Tiger" Battalion, collectively worked out the lyrics to "John Brown's Body". Kimball wrote:

We had a jovial Scotchman in the battalion, named John

Brown. ... [A]nd as he happened to bear the identical name of [the old hero of Harper's Ferry,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Brown%27s_raid_on_Harpers_Ferry) he became at once the butt of his comrades. If he made his appearance a few minutes late among the working squad, or was a little tardy in falling into the company line, he was sure to be greeted with such expressions as "Come, old fellow, you ought to be at it if you are going to help us free the slaves," or, "This can't be John Brown--why, John Brown is dead." And then some wag would add, in a solemn, drawling tone, as if it were his purpose to give particular emphasis to the fact that John Brown was really, actually dead: "Yes, yes, poor old John Brown is dead; his body lies mouldering in the grave.["\[6\]](#page-22-5)

According to Kimball, these sayings became [by-words](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proverb) among the soldiers and, in a communal effort--similar in many ways to the spontaneous composition of camp meeting songs described above--were gradually put to the tune of "Say, Brothers":

Finally ditties composed of the most nonsensical, doggerel rhymes, setting for the fact that John Brown was dead and that his body was undergoing the process of decomposition, began to be sung to the music of the hymn above given. These ditties underwent various ramifications, until eventually the lines were reached,--

"John Brown's body lies a-mouldering in the grave, His soul's marching on."

## And,--

"He's gone to be a soldier in the army of the Lord, His soul's marching on."

These lines seemed to give general satisfaction, the idea that Brown's soul was "marching on" receiving recognition at once as having a germ of inspiration in it. They were sung over and over again with a great deal of gusto, the "Glory, hallelujah" chorus being always added[.\[6\]](#page-22-5)

Some leaders of the battalion, feeling the words were coarse and irreverent, tried to urge the adoption of more fitting lyrics, but to no avail. The lyrics were soon prepared for publication by members of the battalion, together with publisher C. S. Hall. They selected and polished verses they felt appropriate, and may even have enlisted the services of a local poet to help polish and create verses[.\[7\]](#page-22-6)

The official histories of the old First Artillery and of the 55th Artillery (1918) also record the Tiger Battalion's role in creating the John Brown Song, confirming the general thrust of Kimball's version with a few additional details[.\[8\]](#page-22-7)[\[9\]](#page-22-8)

### **Creation of the "Battle Hymn"**

### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Battle_Hymn_of_the_Republic&action=edit§ion=4)

## ![](_page_4_Picture_7.jpeg)

## Julia Ward Howe, 1897

Kimball's battalion was dispatched to [Murray,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murray,_Kentucky) Kentucky, early in the [Civil War,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War) and [Julia Ward Howe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_Ward_Howe) heard this song during a public review of the troops outside Washington, D. C., on [Upton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upton%27s_Hill) [Hill,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upton%27s_Hill) Virginia. [Rufus R. Dawes,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rufus_R._Dawes) then in command of Company "K" of the 6th [Wisconsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisconsin) Volunteer Infantry, stated in his memoirs that the man who started the singing was Sergeant John Ticknor of his company. Howe's companion at the review, [the Reverend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Reverend) [James Freeman Clarke,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Freeman_Clarke) [\[10\]](#page-22-9) suggested to Howe that she write new words for the fighting men's song. Staying at the [Willard Hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willard_Hotel) in Washington on the night of November 18, 1861, Howe wrote the verses to the "Battle Hymn of the Republic"[.\[11\]](#page-23-0) Of the writing of the lyrics, Howe remembered:

I went to bed that night as usual, and slept, according to my wont, quite soundly. I awoke in the gray of the morning twilight; and as I lay waiting for the dawn, the long lines of the desired poem began to twine themselves in my mind. Having thought out all the stanzas, I said to myself, "I must get up and write these verses down, lest I fall asleep again and forget them." So, with a sudden effort, I sprang out of bed, and found in the dimness an old stump of a pencil which I remembered to have used the day before. I scrawled the verses almost without looking at the paper[.\[12\]](#page-23-1)

Howe's "Battle Hymn of the Republic" was first published on the front page of [The Atlantic Monthly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Atlantic_Monthly) of February 1862. The sixth verse written by Howe, which is less commonly sung, was not published at that time.

## ![](_page_6_Figure_2.jpeg)

As originally published 1862 in [The Atlantic Monthly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Atlantic_Monthly)

The song was also published as a broadside in 1863 by the Supervisory Committee for Recruiting Colored Regiments in [Philadelphia.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia)

Both "John Brown" and "Battle Hymn of the Republic" were published in Father Kemp's Old Folks Concert Tunes in 1874 and reprinted in 1889. Both songs had the same chorus with an additional "Glory" in the second line: "Glory! Glory! Glory! Hallelujah!["\[13\]](#page-23-2)

"Canaan's Happy Shore" has a [verse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verse_(poetry)) and [chorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chorus_(song)) of equal metrical length and both verse and chorus share an identical [melody](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melody) and rhythm. "John Brown's Body" has more syllables in its verse and uses a more rhythmically active variation of the "Canaan" melody to accommodate the additional words in the verse. In Howe's lyrics, the words of the verse are packed into a yet longer line, with even more syllables than "John Brown's Body." The verse still uses the same underlying melody as the refrain, but the addition of many dotted rhythms to the underlying melody allows for the more complex verse to fit the same melody as the comparatively short refrain.

# **Popularity and widespread use**

### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Battle_Hymn_of_the_Republic&action=edit§ion=11)

In the years since the [Civil War,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War) "The Battle Hymn of the Republic" has been used frequently as an American patriotic song[.\[27\]](#page-24-5)

### **Cultural influences**

### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Battle_Hymn_of_the_Republic&action=edit§ion=12)

The lyrics of "Battle Hymn of the Republic" appear in Dr. [Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Luther_King_Jr.) [Luther King Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Luther_King_Jr.)'s sermons and speeches, most notably in his speech ["How Long, Not Long"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_Long,_Not_Long) from the steps of the [Alabama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alabama) State Capitol building on March 25, 1965, after the [successful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selma_to_Montgomery_marches#March_to_Montgomery) [Selma to Montgomery march,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selma_to_Montgomery_marches#March_to_Montgomery) and in his final sermon ["I've](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I%27ve_Been_to_the_Mountaintop) [Been to the Mountaintop"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I%27ve_Been_to_the_Mountaintop), delivered in [Memphis, Tennessee,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memphis,_Tennessee) on the evening of April 3, 1968, the night before his assassination. The speech ends with the first lyrics of the "Battle Hymn": "Mine eyes have seen the glory of the coming of the Lord."

Bishop [Michael B. Curry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Curry_(bishop)) of [North Carolina,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Carolina) after his election as the first [African American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Americans) [Presiding Bishop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presiding_Bishop_and_Primate_of_the_Episcopal_Church_in_the_United_States_of_America) of [The Episcopal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Episcopal_Church_(United_States)) [Church,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Episcopal_Church_(United_States)) delivered a [sermon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sermon) to the Church's [General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Convention_of_the_Episcopal_Church_in_the_United_States_of_America) [Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Convention_of_the_Episcopal_Church_in_the_United_States_of_America) on July 3, 2015, in which the lyrics of the "Battle Hymn" framed the message of God's love. After proclaiming "Glory, glory, hallelujah, His truth is marching on", a letter from President [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama) was read, congratulating Bishop

Curry on his historic election[.\[28\]](#page-24-6) Curry is known for quoting the "Battle Hymn" during his sermons.

The tune has played a role in many movies where patriotic music has been required, including the 1970 [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) war comedy [Kelly's Heroes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelly%27s_Heroes), and the 1999 sci-fi western [Wild Wild](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wild_Wild_West) [West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wild_Wild_West). Words from the first verse gave [John Steinbeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Steinbeck)'s wife Carol Steinbeck the title of his 1939 masterpiece [The Grapes of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Grapes_of_Wrath) [Wrath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Grapes_of_Wrath). [\[29\]](#page-24-7) The title of [John Updike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Updike)'s [In the Beauty of the Lilies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_the_Beauty_of_the_Lilies) also came from this song, as did Terrible Swift Sword and Never Call Retreat, two volumes in [Bruce Catton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_Catton)'s Centennial History of the Civil War. Terrible Swift Sword is also the name of a [board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Board_wargame) [wargame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Board_wargame) simulating the [Battle of Gettysburg.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Gettysburg) [\[30\]](#page-24-8)

The opening line of the song is referenced in [The Smiths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Smiths)' 1983 song "These Things Take Time," where it is quoted as "Mine eyes have seen the glory of the sacred wunderkind".[\[31\]](#page-24-9)

Words from the second last line of the last verse are paraphrased in [Leonard Cohen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Cohen)'s song "Steer Your Way"[.\[32\]](#page-24-10) It was originally published as a poem in [The New Yorker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Yorker) magazine[.\[33\]](#page-25-0) "As He died to make men holy, let us die to make men free" becomes "As He died to make men holy, let us die to make things cheap".

# **In association with football/soccer**

# [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Battle_Hymn_of_the_Republic&action=edit§ion=13)

The refrain "Glory, glory, hallelujah!" has been adopted by fans of a number of sporting teams, most notably in the English and Scottish Premier Leagues. The popular use of the tune by

[Tottenham Hotspur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tottenham_Hotspur_F. C._Under-21s_and_Academy) can be traced to September 1961 during the [1961-62 European Cup.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1961%E2%80%9362_European_Cup) Their first opponents in the competition were the Polish side [Górnik Zabrze,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%B3rnik_Zabrze) and the Polish press described the Spurs team as "no angels" due to their rough tackling. In the return leg at [White Hart Lane,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Hart_Lane) some fans then wore angel costumes at the match holding placards with slogans such as "Glory be to shining White Hart Lane", and the crowded started singing the refrain "Glory, glory, hallelujah" as Spurs beat the Poles 8-1, starting the tradition at Tottenham. [\[34\]](#page-25-1) It was released as the B-side to ["Ossie's Dream"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ossie%27s_Dream_(Spurs_Are_on_Their_Way_to_Wembley)) for the [1981 FA Cup final.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1981_FA_Cup_final)

The theme was then picked up by Hibernian, with Hector Nicol's release of the track "Glory, glory to the Hibees" in 1963. [\[35\]](#page-25-2)[\[36\]](#page-25-3) "Glory, Glory Leeds United" was a popular chant during [Leeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leeds_United_F. C.)' [1969-70 FA Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1969%E2%80%9370_FA_Cup) run all the way to the [final.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1970_FA_Cup_final) [Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_United_F. C.) [United](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_United_F. C.) fans picked it up as "Glory, Glory Man United" prior to the [1983 FA Cup final.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1983_FA_Cup_final)

"We're not Brazil, we're Northern Ireland" is a self-deprecating song popular with fans of the [Northern Ireland football team.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland_national_football_team)

The words by Stewart Mc Afe[e\[37\]](#page-25-4) celebrate the team's historic successes ("Mine eyes have seen the glory of [España '82"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1982_FIFA_World_Cup)), whilst acknowledging in a witty way that it is not among the giants of world football. A studio recording sung by the team and featuring local broadcasters George Jones and [Jackie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackie_Fullerton) [Fullerton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackie_Fullerton) was released in 2006[.\[38\]](#page-25-5)

As a result of its popularity with these and other British teams, use of the tune has spread internationally and to other sporting codes. An example of its reach is its popularity with fans of the

Australian Rugby League team, the [South Sydney Rabbitohs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Sydney_Rabbitohs) (Glory, Glory to South Sydney) and to [A-League Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A-League_Men) team [Perth Glory.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perth_Glory_FC) [Brighton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brighton_%26_Hove_Albion_F. C.) fans celebrate their 1970s legend by singing "Mine eyes have seen the glory of the coming of the Lord, he played for Brighton and Hove Albion and his name is [Peter Ward"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Ward_(footballer,_born_1955)).[[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)]

# **Other songs set to this tune**

# [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Battle_Hymn_of_the_Republic&action=edit§ion=14)

Some songs make use of both the melody and elements of the lyrics of "Battle Hymn of the Republic", either in tribute or as a parody:

- ["Marching Song of the First Arkansas"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marching_Song_of_the_First_Arkansas) is a [Civil War-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War)era song that has a similar lyrical structure to "Battle Hymn of the Republic". It has been described as "a powerful early statement of [black pride,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_pride) militancy, and desire for full equality, revealing the aspirations of black soldiers for [Reconstruction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconstruction_era_of_the_United_States) as well as anticipating the spirit of the [civil rights movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_rights_movement) of the 1960s"[.\[39\]](#page-25-6)
- • The tune has been used with alternative lyrics numerous times. The [University of Georgia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Georgia)'s rally song, ["Glory Glory to](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glory,_Glory_(fight_song)) [Old Georgia"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glory,_Glory_(fight_song)), is based on the patriotic tune, and has been sung at [American college football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_football) games since 1909. Other college teams also use songs set to the same tune. One such is "Glory, Glory to Old [Auburn"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auburn,_Alabama) at [Auburn University.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auburn_University) Another is "Glory Colorado", traditionally played by the band and sung after [touchdowns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Touchdown) scored by the [Colorado Buffaloes.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorado_Buffaloes) "Glory Colorado" has been a fight song at the University of Colorado

(Boulder) for more than one hundred years.

- In 1901 [Mark Twain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Twain) wrote ["The Battle Hymn of the Republic,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Battle_Hymn_of_the_Republic,_Updated) [Updated"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Battle_Hymn_of_the_Republic,_Updated), with the same tune as the original, as a comment on the [Philippine-American War.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine%E2%80%93American_War) It was later recorded by the [Chad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chad_Mitchell_Trio) [Mitchell Trio.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chad_Mitchell_Trio)
- • "The Burning of the School" is a well-known parody of the song. [\[40\]](#page-25-7)
- The [United States Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army) [paratrooper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paratrooper) song, ["Blood on the](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_on_the_Risers) [Risers"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_on_the_Risers), first sung in [World War II,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) includes the lyrics "Gory, gory" in the lyrics, based on the original's "Glory, glory".
- A number of [terrace songs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Football_chant) (in association football) are sung to the tune in Britain. Most frequently, fans chant ["Glory, Glory..."](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glory_Glory_(football_chant)) [plus their team's name:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glory_Glory_(football_chant)) the chants have been recorded and released officially as songs by [Hibernian,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hibernian_F. C.) [Tottenham,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tottenham_Hotspur_F. C.) [Leeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leeds_United_F. C.) [United](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leeds_United_F. C.) and [Manchester United.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_United_F. C.) The [1994 World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1994_FIFA_World_Cup) official song "Gloryland" interpreted by Daryl Hall and the Sounds of Blackness has the tune of "Battle Hymn of the Republic".[\[41\]](#page-25-8) In Argentina the St. Alban's former Pupils Assn (Old Philomathian Club) used the tune for its "Glory Glory Philomathians" as well. While not heard often nowadays it is still a cherished song for the Old Philomathians.
- • In Australia, the song is used by rugby league club the [South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Sydney_Rabbitohs) [Sydney Rabbitohs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Sydney_Rabbitohs) - "Glory Glory to South Sydney". Each verse ends with, "They wear the Red and Green"[.\[42\]](#page-25-9)
- • The parody song "Jesus Can't Play Rugby", popular at informal sporting events, uses the traditional melody under improvised lyrics. Performances typically feature a call-and-response structure, wherein one performer proposes an amusing reason why [Jesus Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus_Christ) might be disqualified from playing [rugby--](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rugby_football)e.g. "Jesus can't play rugby 'cause [his dad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_in_Christianity) will [rig the game"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_intervention) which is then repeated back by other participants (mirroring the repetitive structure of "John Brown's Body"), before ending with the tongue-in-cheek proclamation "Jesus saves, Jesus saves, Jesus saves". A chorus may feature the repeated call of "Free beer for all the ruggers", or, after concluding the final verse, "Jesus, we're only kidding"[.\[43\]](#page-25-10)

- • A variant of this version is popular at [Hash House Harrier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hash_House_Harriers) events, proposing reasons why Jesus might be disqualified from running a hash trail--e.g. "Jesus can't go Hashing 'cause the flour [falls through his hands"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_Holy_Wounds) or "Jesus can't go Hashing 'cause [he turns the beer to wine"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wedding_at_Cana). The chorus is similarly changed to "Free beer for all the Hashers", and finally "Jesus, we're only kidding"[.\[44\]](#page-25-11)
- • A [protest song](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protest_song) titled "Gloria, Gloria Labandera" (lit. "Gloria the Laundrywoman") was used by supporters of former Philippine president [Joseph Estrada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Estrada) to mock [Gloria Macapagal Arroyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gloria_Macapagal_Arroyo) after the latter assumed the presidency following Estrada's ouster from office, further deriving the "labandera" parallels to alleged [money laundering.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Money_laundering) [\[45\]](#page-25-12) While Arroyo did not mind the nickname and went on to use it for her projects, the Catholic Church took umbrage to the parody lyrics and called it "obscene"[.\[46\]](#page-26-0)
- • The song "The White Man Marches On" is a rendition using the same melody, written by white supremacist [Johnny Rebel.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnny_Rebel_(singer)) This version contains explicit racist and antisemitic lyrics. It is famously know for having been used in a scene of the 1998 film [American History X](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_History_X)

- Former Christian singer [Audrey Assad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audrey_Assad) recorded a rewritten version in 2019 called "Your Peace Will Make Us One", keeping the religious themes but removing the violent references. Other songs simply use the melody, i.e. the melody of "John Brown's Body", with no lyrical connection to "The Battle Hymn of the Republic":
- • ["Solidarity Forever"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solidarity_Forever), a marching song for [organized labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organized_labor) in the 20th century[.\[47\]](#page-26-1)
- The anthem of the American [consumers' cooperative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumers%27_cooperative) movement, ["The Battle Hymn of Cooperation"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Battle_Hymn_of_Cooperation), written in 1932.
- The tune has been used as a marching song in the Finnish military with the words "Kalle-Kustaan muori makaa hiljaa haudassaan, ja yli haudan me marssimme näin" ("Carl Gustaf's hag lies silently in her grave, and we're marching over the grave like this")[.\[48\]](#page-26-2)
- • The Finnish Ice Hockey fans can be heard singing the tune with the lyrics "Suomi tekee kohta maalin, eikä kukaan sille mitään voi" ("Finland will soon score, and no one can do anything about it")[.\[49\]](#page-26-3)
- • The Estonian song "Kalle Kusta" uses the melody as well.
- The Swedish [drinking song](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snapsvisa) Halta Lotta (lit. "Limping Lotta") referring to a pub in [Gothenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothenburg) - uses the melody. The song tells how much a drink is worth at the pub in question (either 8 or 15 [öre,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%96re) depending on the version), how one can pay with kisses if one cannot afford a drink, how the recipient of these kisses is the [landlady](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landlady)'s sister given that the landlady is dead, where the landlady is buried and how her [grave is desecrated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desecration_of_graves) by urinating dogs and how her body decays, eventually leading to the [nationalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalization) of the pub, which [drives the prices up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Price_gouging) to 50 öre[.\[50\]](#page-26-4)

- • The folk dance "Gólya" ("Stork"), known in several Hungarianspeaking communities in Transylvania (Romania), as well as in Hungary proper, is set to the same tune. The same dance is found among the [Csángós](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cs%C3%A1ng%C3%B3) of Moldavia with a different tune, under the name "Hojna"; with the Moldavian melody generally considered original, and the "Battle Hymn" tune a later adaptation.[[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)]
- • The melody is used in [French Canadian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Canadians) Christmas [carol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carol_(music)) called "Glory, Alleluia", covered by [Celine Dion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celine_Dion) and others[.\[51\]](#page-26-5)
- The melody is used in the marching song of the Assam Regiment of the Indian Army: ["Badluram Ka Badan"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Badluram_Ka_Badan), or "Badluram's Body", its chorus being "Shabash Hallelujah" instead of "Glory Hallelujah". The word "Shabash" in Hindustani means "congratulations" or "well done".

- The song ["Up Went Nelson"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Up_Went_Nelson), celebrating the destruction of [Nelson's Pillar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nelson%27s_Pillar) in [Dublin,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin) is sung to this tune.
- • The Discordian Handbook [Principia Discordia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principia_Discordia) has a version of the song called Battle Hymn of the Eristocracy[.\[52\]](#page-26-6) It has been recorded for example by [Aarni.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aarni) [\[53\]](#page-26-7)

- The [Subiaco Football Club,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subiaco_Football_Club) in the [West Australian Football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Australian_Football_League) [League,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Australian_Football_League) uses the song for their team song. Also, the [Casey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casey_Demons) [Demons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casey_Demons) in the [Victorian Football League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_Football_League) also currently use the song. As well as these two clubs, the [West Torrens Football Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Torrens_Football_Club) used the song until 1990, when their successor club, [Woodville-West Torrens,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodville-West_Torrens_Football_Club) currently use this song in the [South Australian National Football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Australian_National_Football_League) [League.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Australian_National_Football_League) The [Broadbeach Cats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadbeach_Cats) also employ this melody for their theme song. Clarence Kangaroos and Wanderer Eagles use this as well.
