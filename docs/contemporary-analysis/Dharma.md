# *Dharma*

*Dharma* (/ˈdɑː[rm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English)ə/; [Sanskrit:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_language) [धर्म](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E0%A4%A7%E0%A4%B0%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%AE#Sanskrit), pronounced [dʱɐ[rm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Sanskrit)ɐ] [ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Dharma.ogg)) is a key concept in various [Indian religions.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_religions) The term *dharma* does not have a single, clear [translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Untranslatability) and conveys a multifaceted idea. Etymologically, it comes from the Sanskrit *dhr-*, meaning *to hold* or *to support*, thus referring to the law that sustains things—from one's life to society, and to the Universe at large. In its most commonly used sense, dharma refers to an individual's moral responsibilities or duties; the dharma of a farmer differs from the dharma of a soldier, thus making the concept of dharma dynamic. As with the other components of the Puruṣ[ārtha,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puru%E1%B9%A3%C4%81rtha) the concept of *dharma* is pan-Indian. The antonym of dharma is *[adharma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adharma)*.

In [Hinduism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism) *dharma* denotes behaviour that is considered to be in accord with Ṛ*[ta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E1%B9%9Ata)*—the "order and custom" that makes [life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life) and universe possible.\[note 1] This includes duties, rights, laws, conduct, virtues, and ethics to form the "righteous way of living". *Dharma* is believed to have a transtemporal validity, and is one of the Puruṣārtha. The concept of *dharma* was in use in the [historical Vedic religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_Vedic_religion) (1500–500 BCE), and its meaning and conceptual scope has evolved over several millennia.

In [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism), *dharma* [\(Pali:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pali_language) *dhamma*) refers to the [teachings of the Buddha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Buddha#Teachings_and_views) and to the true nature of reality (which the teachings point to). In [Buddhist philosophy,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_philosophy) *dhamma/dharma* is also the term for specific "[phenomena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomena)" and for the [ultimate truth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_truths_doctrine). \[note 2] *Dharma* [in Jainism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharma_(Jainism)) refers to the teachings of [Tirthankara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tirthankara) (Jina) and the body of doctrine pertaining to purification and moral transformation. In [Sikhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhism), *dharma* indicates the path of righteousness, proper religious practices, and performing moral duties.

# **Etymology**

The word *dharma* (/ˈdɑː[rm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English)ə/) [\[3\]](#page-11-2) has roots in the [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit) *dhr-*, which means *to hold* or *to support*, and is related to Latin *firmus* (firm, stable).[\[4\]](#page-11-3) From this, it takes the meaning of "what is established or firm", and hence "law". It is derived from an older [Vedic Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedic_Sanskrit) *n*-stem *dharman-*, with a literal meaning of "bearer, supporter", in a [religious sense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_Vedic_religion) conceived as an aspect of [Rta.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rta) [\[5\]](#page-11-4)

In the [Rigveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigveda), the word appears as an *n*-stem, *dhárman-*, with a range of meanings encompassing "something established or firm" (in the literal sense of prods or poles). Figuratively, it means "sustainer" and "supporter" (of [deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_deities)). It is semantically similar to the Greek *[themis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Themis)* ("fixed decree, statute, law").[\[6\]](#page-11-5)

In the 3rd century BCE the Mauryan Emperor Ashoka translated dharma into Greek and Aramaic and he used the Greek word eusebeia (εὐσέβεια, piety, spiritual maturity, or godliness) in the Kandahar Bilingual Rock Inscription and the Kandahar Greek Edicts. [7] In the former, he used the Aramaic word קשיטא (qšy ṭ'; truth, rectitude). [8]

### **Definition**

Dharma is a concept of central importance in Indian philosophy and Indian religions. [15] It has multiple meanings in Hinduism, Buddhism, Sikhism and Jainism. [16] It is difficult to provide a single concise definition for *dharma*, as the word has a long and varied history and straddles a complex set of meanings and interpretations. [17] There is no equivalent single-word synonym for *dharma* in western languages. [18]

There have been numerous, conflicting attempts to translate ancient Sanskrit literature with the word *dharma* into German, English and French. The concept, claims Paul Horsch, has caused exceptional difficulties for modern commentators and translators. [19] For example, while Grassmann's translation of Rig-Veda identifies seven different meanings of dharma, [20] Karl Friedrich Geldner in his translation of the Rig-Veda employs 20 different translations for dharma, including meanings such as "law", "justice", "righteousness", "order", "duty", "custom", "quality", and "model", among others. [19] However, the word *dharma* has become a widely accepted loanword in English, and is included in all modern unabridged English dictionaries.

The term *dharma* derives from the Sanskrit root "dhr", which means "to support, hold, or bear". It is the thing that regulates the course of change by not participating in change, but that principle which remains constant. [21] *Monier-Williams Sanskrit-English Dictionary*, the widely

cited resource for definitions and explanation of Sanskrit words and concepts of Hinduism, offers numerous definitions of the word *dharma*, such as that which is established or firm, steadfast decree, statute, law, practice, custom, duty, right, justice, virtue, morality, ethics, religion, religious merit, good works, nature, character, quality, property. Yet, each of these definitions is incomplete, while the combination of these translations does not convey the total sense of the word. In common parlance, *dharma* means "right way of living" and "path of rightness".[\[21\]](#page-13-4) Dharma also has connotations of order, and when combined with the word *sanātana*, it can also be described as eternal truth.[\[23\]](#page-13-6)

The meaning of the word *dharma* depends on the context, and its meaning has evolved as ideas of Hinduism have developed through history. In the earliest texts and ancient myths of Hinduism, *dharma* meant cosmic law, the rules that created the universe from chaos, as well as rituals; in later [Vedas,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas) [Upanishads,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upanishads) [Puranas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puranas) and the [Epics,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_epic_poetry) the meaning became refined, richer, and more complex, and the word was applied to diverse contexts.[\[24\]](#page-13-7) In certain contexts, *dharma* designates human behaviours considered necessary for order of things in the universe, principles that prevent chaos, behaviours and action necessary to all life in nature, society, family as well as at the individual level.[\[1\]](#page-11-6)[\[24\]](#page-13-7)[\[25\]](#page-13-8)\[note 1] *Dharma* encompasses ideas such as duty, rights, character, vocation, religion, customs and all behaviour considered appropriate, correct or morally upright.[\[26\]](#page-13-9) For further context, the word *varnasramdharma* is often used in its place, defined as dharma specifically related to the stage of life one is in.[\[27\]](#page-13-10) The concept of *Dharma* is believed to have a transtemporal validity. [\[28\]](#page-13-11)

The antonym of *dharma* is *[adharma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adharma)* (Sanskrit: अधर्म),[\[29\]](#page-13-12) meaning that which is "not dharma". As with *dharma*, the word *adharma* includes and implies many ideas; in common parlance, adharma means that which is against nature, immoral, unethical, wrong or unlawful.[\[30\]](#page-14-0)

In Buddhism, *dharma* incorporates the teachings and doctrines of the founder of Buddhism, [the Buddha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gautama_Buddha).

# **History**

According to [Pandurang Vaman Kane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pandurang_Vaman_Kane), author of the book *[History of Dharmaśāstra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Dharma%C5%9B%C4%81stra)*, the word *dharma* appears at least fifty-six times in the hymns of the [Rigveda,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigveda) as an adjective or noun. According to Paul Horsch, the word *dharma* has its origin in Vedic Hinduism.[\[19\]](#page-13-2) The hymns of the [Rigveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigveda) claim [Brahman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahman) created the universe from chaos, they hold (dhar-) the earth and sun and stars apart, they support (dhar-) the sky away and distinct from earth, and they stabilise (dhar-) the quaking mountains and plains.[\[19\]](#page-13-2)[\[31\]](#page-14-1)

The [Deities,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_deities) mainly [Indra,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indra) then deliver and hold order from disorder, harmony from chaos, stability from instability – actions recited in the Veda with the root of word dharma.[\[24\]](#page-13-7) In hymns composed after the mythological verses, the word dharma takes expanded meaning as a [cosmic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_cosmology) principle and appears in verses independent of [deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_deities). It evolves into a concept, claims Paul Horsch, that has a dynamic functional sense in [Atharvaveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atharvaveda) for example, where it becomes the cosmic law that links cause and effect through a subject.[\[19\]](#page-13-2) Dharma, in these ancient texts, also takes a ritual meaning. The ritual is connected to the cosmic, and "dharmani" is equated to ceremonial devotion to the principles that deities used to create order from disorder, the world from chaos.[\[32\]](#page-14-2)

Past the ritual and cosmic sense of dharma that link the current world to mythical universe, the concept extends to an ethical-social sense that links human beings to each other and to other life forms. It is here that dharma as a concept of law emerges in Hinduism.[\[33\]](#page-14-3)[\[34\]](#page-14-4)

Dharma and related words are found in the oldest Vedic literature of [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism), in later Vedas, Upanishads, Puranas, and the Epics; the word dharma also plays a central role in the literature of other Indian religions founded later, such as Buddhism and Jainism.[\[24\]](#page-13-7) According to Brereton, *Dharman* occurs 63 times in [Rig-veda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigveda); in addition, words related to Dharman also appear in Rig-veda, for example once as dharmakrt, 6 times as *satyadharman*, and once as *dharmavant*, 4 times as *dharman* and twice as *dhariman*. [\[35\]](#page-14-6)

[Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-European_studies) parallels for "dharma" are known, but the only Iranian equivalent is Old Persian *darmān*, meaning "remedy". This meaning is different from the [Indo-Aryan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Aryan_languages) *dhárman*, suggesting that the word "dharma" did not play a major role in the [Indo-Iranian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Iranians) period. Instead, it was primarily developed more recently under the Vedic tradition.[\[35\]](#page-14-6)

It is thought that the *[Daena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daena)* of [Zoroastrianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism), also meaning the "eternal Law" or "religion", is related to Sanskrit "dharma".[\[36\]](#page-14-7) Ideas in parts overlapping to *Dharma* are found in other ancient cultures: such as Chinese [Tao,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tao) Egyptian [Maat,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maat) Sumerian [Me](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Me_(mythology)). [\[21\]](#page-13-4)

#### **Eusebeia and dharma**

In the mid-20th century, an inscription of the Indian [Emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Asoka) [Asoka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Asoka) from the year 258 BCE was discovered in Afghanistan, the [Kandahar Bilingual Rock Inscription.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kandahar_Bilingual_Rock_Inscription) This rock inscription contains [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) and [Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramaic_language) text. According to [Paul Hacker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Hacker_(Indologist)), [\[37\]](#page-14-5) on the rock appears a Greek rendering for the Sanskrit word dharma: the word [eusebeia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eusebeia). [\[37\]](#page-14-5)

Scholars of Hellenistic Greece explain eusebeia as a complex concept. Eusebia means not only to venerate [deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_deities), but also spiritual maturity, a reverential attitude toward life, and includes the right conduct toward one's parents, siblings and children, the right conduct between husband and wife, and the conduct between biologically unrelated people. This rock inscription, concludes Paul Hacker, suggests dharma in India, about 2300 years ago, was a central concept and meant not only religious ideas, but ideas of right, of good, of one's duty toward the human community. [\[37\]](#page-14-5)[\[38\]](#page-14-8)

# **Jainism**

Within Jainism the meaning of Dharma is chiefly associated with the teachings of the [Tirthankaras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tirthankaras). These teachings are understood to ford a passage across [Samsara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samsara). [\[44\]](#page-14-14)

The word *dharma* in Jainism is found in all its key texts. It has a contextual meaning and refers to a number of ideas. In the broadest sense, it means the teachings of the [Jinas,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arihant_(Jainism)) [\[1\]](#page-11-6) or teachings of any competing spiritual school,[\[45\]](#page-14-15) a supreme path,[\[46\]](#page-14-16) socio-religious duty, [\[47\]](#page-14-17) and that which is the highest *mangala* (holy).[\[48\]](#page-14-18)

The *[Tattvartha Sutra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tattvartha_Sutra)*, a major [Jain text](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_text), mentions *daśa dharma* (lit. 'ten *dharma*s') with referring to ten righteous virtues: forbearance, modesty, straightforwardness, purity, truthfulness, self-restraint, austerity, renunciation, non-attachment, and celibacy. [\[49\]](#page-14-19) Ācārya Amṛtacandra, author of the Jain text, *Puru*ṣ*ārthasiddhyupāya* writes:[\[50\]](#page-14-20)

A right believer should constantly meditate on virtues of dharma, like supreme modesty, in order to protect the Self from all contrary dispositions. He should also cover up the shortcomings of others.

## —*Puru*ṣ*ārthasiddhyupāya* (27)

### *Dharmāstikāya*

The term *dharmāstikāya* ([Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_language): धर्मा स्ति का य) also has a specific [ontological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontology) and soteriological meaning in Jainism, as a part of its theory of six *dravya* (substance or a reality). In the Jain tradition, existence consists of *[jīva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C4%ABva_(Jainism))* (soul, *[ātman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%80tman_(Hinduism))*) and *[ajīva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aj%C4%ABva)* (non-soul, *[anātman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An%C4%81tman_(Hinduism))*), the latter consisting of five categories: inert non-sentient atomic matter (*pudgalāstikāya*), space (*[ākāśa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%80k%C4%81%C5%9Ba)*), time (*[kāla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C4%81la)*), principle of motion (*dharmāstikāya*), and principle of rest (*adharmāstikāya*).[\[51\]](#page-14-21)[\[52\]](#page-14-22) The use of the term *dharmāstikāya* to mean motion and to refer to an ontological sub-category is peculiar to Jainism, and not found in the [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics) of Buddhism and various schools of Hinduism.[\[52\]](#page-14-22)

# **Hinduism**

*Dharma* is an organising principle in [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism) that applies to human beings in solitude, in their interaction with human beings and nature, as well as between inanimate objects, to all of [cosmos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmos) and its parts.[\[21\]](#page-13-4) It refers to the order and customs which make life and universe possible, and includes behaviours, rituals, rules that govern society, and ethics.[\[1\]](#page-11-6)\[note 1] [Hindu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindus) *dharma* includes the religious duties, moral rights and duties of each individual, as well as behaviours that enable social order, right conduct, and those that are virtuous.[\[53\]](#page-15-0) *Dharma*, according to Van Buitenen,[\[54\]](#page-15-1) is that which all existing beings must accept and respect to sustain harmony and order in the world. It is neither the act nor the result, but the natural laws that guide the act and create the result to prevent chaos in the world. It is innate characteristic, that makes the being what it is. It is, claims Van Buitenen, the pursuit and execution of one's nature and true calling, thus playing one's role in cosmic concert. In Hinduism, it is the *dharma* of the bee to make honey, of cow to give milk, of sun to radiate sunshine, of river to flow. [\[54\]](#page-15-1) In terms of humanity, *dharma* is the need for, the effect of and essence of service and interconnectedness of all life.[\[21\]](#page-13-4)[\[37\]](#page-14-5) This includes duties, rights, [laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law), conduct, [virtues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtue) and "right way of living".[\[55\]](#page-15-2)

In its true essence, *dharma* means for a Hindu to "expand the mind". Furthermore, it represents the direct connection between the individual and the societal phenomena that bind the society together. In the way societal phenomena affect the conscience of the individual, similarly may the actions of an individual alter the course of the society, for better or for worse. This has been subtly echoed by the credo धर्मो धा रयति प्रजा : meaning *dharma* is that which holds and provides support to the social construct.

In Hinduism, *dharma* generally includes various aspects:

- [Sanātana Dharma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San%C4%81tana_Dharma), the eternal and unchanging principals of *dharma*. [\[56\]](#page-15-3)
- Varṇ āśramā dharma, one's duty at specific [stages of life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashrama_(stage)) or inherent duties.[\[57\]](#page-15-4)
- [Svadharma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Svadharma), one's own individual or personal duty. [\[58\]](#page-15-5)[\[59\]](#page-15-6)
- Āpad dharma, *dharma* prescribed at the time of adversities.[\[59\]](#page-15-6)
- Sadharana dharma, moral duties irrespective of the [stages of life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashrama_(stage)). [\[60\]](#page-15-7)\[note 3]
- [Yuga dharma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuga_dharma), *dharma* which is valid for a [yuga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuga), an epoch or age as established by Hindu tradition and thus may change at the conclusion of its time.[\[16\]](#page-12-9)[\[62\]](#page-15-8)

### **In Vedas and Upanishads**

The history section of this article discusses the development of *dharma* concept in [Vedas.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas) This development continued in the [Upanishads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upanishads) and later ancient scripts of Hinduism. In Upanishads, the concept of *dharma* continues as universal principle of law, order, harmony, and truth. It acts as the regulatory moral principle of the Universe. It is explained as law of righteousness and equated to *[satya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satya)* [\(Sanskrit:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_language) सत्यं, truth),[\[19\]](#page-13-2)[\[63\]](#page-15-9) in hymn 1.4.14 of [Brhadaranyaka Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brihadaranyaka_Upanishad), as follows:

Nothing is higher than dharma. The weak overcomes the stronger by dharma, as over a king. Truly that dharma is the Truth (*Satya*); Therefore, when a man speaks the Truth, they say, "He speaks the Dharma"; and if he speaks Dharma, they say, "He speaks the Truth!" For both are one.

—[Brihadaranyaka Upanishad,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brihadaranyaka_Upanishad) 1.4.xiv[\[19\]](#page-13-2)[\[63\]](#page-15-9)

#### **Dharma and Mimamsa**

*[Mimamsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%ABm%C4%81%E1%B9%83s%C4%81)*, developed through commentaries on its foundational texts, particularly the *[Mimamsa Sutras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purva_Mimamsa_Sutras)* attributed to [Jaimini,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaimini) emphasizes "the desire to know dharma" as the central concern, defining dharma as what connects a person with the highest good, always yet to be realized. While some schools associate dharma with post-mortem existence, *Mimamsakas* focus on the continual renewal and realization of a ritual world through adherence to Vedic injunctions. They assert that the ultimate good is essentially inaccessible to perception and can only be understood through language, reflecting confidence in Vedic injunctions and the reality of language as a means of knowing.[\[64\]](#page-15-10)

*Mimamsa* addresses the delayed results of actions (like wealth or heaven) through the concept of apurva or adrsta, an unseen force that preserves the connection between actions and their outcomes. This ensures that Vedic sacrifices, though their results are delayed, are effective and reliable in guiding toward dharma.[\[65\]](#page-15-11)

### **In the Epics**

The Hindu religion and philosophy, claims [Daniel Ingalls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_H._H._Ingalls,_Sr.), places major emphasis on individual practical morality. In the Sanskrit epics, this concern is omnipresent.[\[66\]](#page-15-12) In Hindu Epics, the good, morally upright, law-abiding king is referred to as "dharmaraja".[\[67\]](#page-15-13)

*Dharma* is at the centre of all major events in the life of Dasharatha, [Rama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rama), [Sita,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sita) and [Lakshman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakshmana) in Ramayana. In the Ramayana, Dasharatha upholds his dharma by honoring a promise to Kaikeyi, resulting in his beloved son Rama's exile, even though it brings him immense personal suffering.[\[68\]](#page-15-14)

In the [Mahabharata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahabharata)*, dharma* is central, and it is presented through symbolism and metaphors. Near the end of the epic, Yama referred to as *dharma* in the text, is portrayed as taking the form of a [dog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dog) to test the compassion of [Yudhishthira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yudhishthira), who is told he may not enter paradise with such an animal. Yudhishthira refuses to abandon his companion, for which he is then praised by *dharma*. [\[69\]](#page-15-15) The value and appeal of the Mahabharata, according to Ingalls, is not as much in its complex and rushed presentation of metaphysics in the 12th book.[\[68\]](#page-15-14) Indian metaphysics, he argues, is more eloquently presented in other Sanskrit scriptures. Instead, the appeal of Mahabharata, like [Ramayana,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramayana) lies in its presentation of a series of moral problems and life situations, where there are usually three answers:[\[68\]](#page-15-14) one answer is of [Bhima,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhima) which represents brute force, an individual angle representing materialism, egoism, and self; the second answer is of [Yudhishthira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yudhishthira), which appeals to piety, [deities,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_deities) social virtue, and tradition; the third answer is of introspective [Arjuna,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arjuna) which falls between the two extremes, and who, claims Ingalls, symbolically reveals the finest moral qualities of man. The Epics of Hinduism are a symbolic treatise about life, virtues, customs, morals, ethics, law, and other aspects of *dharma*. [\[70\]](#page-16-0) There is extensive discussion of *dharma* at the individual level in the Epics of Hinduism; for example, on free will versus destiny, when and why human beings believe in either, the strong and prosperous naturally uphold free will, while those facing grief or frustration naturally lean towards destiny. [\[71\]](#page-16-1) The Epics of Hinduism illustrate various aspects of *dharma* with metaphors.[\[72\]](#page-16-2)

### **According to 4th-century Vatsyayana**

According to [Klaus Klostermaier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klaus_Klostermaier), 4th-century CE Hindu scholar [Vātsyāyana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C4%81tsy%C4%81yana) explained *dharma* by contrasting it with adharma.[\[73\]](#page-16-3) Vātsyāyana suggested that *dharma* is not merely in one's actions, but also in words one speaks or writes, and in thought. According to Vātsyāyana:[\[73\]](#page-16-3)[\[74\]](#page-16-4)

- 1. Adharma of body: hinsa (violence), steya (steal, theft), pratisiddha maithuna (sexual indulgence with someone other than one's partner)
- 2. Dharma of body: dana (charity), paritrana (succor of the distressed) and paricarana (rendering service to others)

- 3. Adharma from words one speaks or writes: mithya (falsehood), parusa (caustic talk), sucana (calumny) and asambaddha (absurd talk)
- 4. Dharma from words one speaks or writes: satya (truth and facts), hitavacana (talking with good intention), priyavacana (gentle, kind talk), svadhyaya (self-study)
- 5. Adharma of mind: paradroha (ill will to anyone), paradravyabhipsa (covetousness), nastikya (denial of the existence of morals and religiosity)
- 6. Dharma of mind: daya (compassion), asprha (disinterestedness), and sraddha (faith in others)

### **According to Patanjali Yoga**

In the *Yoga Sutras* [of Patanjali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoga_Sutras_of_Patanjali) the *dharma* is real; in the Vedanta it is unreal.[\[75\]](#page-16-5)

*Dharma* is part of [yoga,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoga) suggests [Patanjali;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patanjali) the elements of Hindu dharma are the attributes, qualities and aspects of yoga.[\[75\]](#page-16-5) Patanjali explained *dharma* in two categories: *[yamas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamas)* (restraints) and *[niyamas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niyama)* (observances).[\[73\]](#page-16-3)

The five yamas, according to Patanjali, are: abstain from injury to all living creatures, abstain from falsehood (satya), abstain from unauthorised appropriation of things-of-value from another (acastrapurvaka), abstain from coveting or sexually cheating on your partner, and abstain from expecting or accepting gifts from others.[\[76\]](#page-16-6) The five yama apply in action, speech and mind. In explaining yama, Patanjali clarifies that certain professions and situations may require qualification in conduct. For example, a fisherman must injure a fish, but he must attempt to do this with least trauma to fish and the fisherman must try to injure no other creature as he fishes.[\[77\]](#page-16-7)

The five niyamas (observances) are cleanliness by eating pure food and removing impure thoughts (such as arrogance or jealousy or pride), contentment in one's means, meditation and silent reflection regardless of circumstances one faces, study and pursuit of historic knowledge, and devotion of all actions to the Supreme Teacher to achieve perfection of concentration.[\[78\]](#page-16-8)

#### **Sources**

*Dharma* is an empirical and experiential inquiry for every man and woman, according to some texts of Hinduism.[\[37\]](#page-14-5)[\[79\]](#page-16-9) For example, [Apastamba Dharmasutra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apastamba) states:

*Dharma* and *Adharma* do not go around saying, "That is us." Neither do gods, nor gandharvas, nor ancestors declare what is *Dharma* and what is *Adharma*.

## —*Apastamba Dharmasutra*[\[80\]](#page-16-10)

In other texts, three sources and means to discover *dharma* in Hinduism are described. These, according to [Paul Hacker,](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Hacker) are:[\[81\]](#page-16-11) First, learning historical knowledge such as Vedas, Upanishads, the Epics and other [Sanskrit literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_literature) with the help of one's teacher. Second, observing the behaviour and example of good people. The third source applies when neither one's education nor example exemplary conduct is known. In this case, ["atmatusti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmatusti)" is the source of *dharma* in Hinduism, that is the good person reflects and follows what satisfies his heart, his own inner feeling, what he feels driven to.[\[81\]](#page-16-11)

### **Dharma, life stages and social stratification**

Some texts of Hinduism outline *dharma* for society and at the individual level. Of these, the most cited one is *[Manusmriti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manusmriti)*, which describes the four *Varnas*, their rights and duties.[\[82\]](#page-16-12) Most texts of Hinduism, however, discuss *dharma* with no mention of *Varna* [\(caste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caste_system_in_India)).[\[83\]](#page-16-13) Other *dharma* texts and Smritis differ from Manusmriti on the nature and structure of Varnas.[\[82\]](#page-16-12) Yet, other texts question the very existence of varna. [Bhrigu,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhrigu) in the Epics, for example, presents the theory that *dharma* does not require any varnas.[\[84\]](#page-16-14) In practice, medieval India is widely believed to be a socially stratified society, with each social strata inheriting a profession and being endogamous. Varna was not absolute in Hindu dharma; individuals had the right to renounce and leave their Varna, as well as their [asramas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashrama_(stage)) of life, in search of moksa.[\[82\]](#page-16-12)[\[85\]](#page-17-0) While neither Manusmriti nor succeeding Smritis of Hinduism ever use the word varnadharma (that is, the *dharma* of varnas), or varnasramadharma (that is, the *dharma* of varnas and asramas), the scholarly commentary on Manusmriti use these words, and thus associate *dharma* with varna system of India.[\[82\]](#page-16-12)[\[86\]](#page-17-1) In 6th-century India, even Buddhist kings called themselves "protectors of varnasramadharma" – that is, *dharma* of varna and asramas of life.[\[82\]](#page-16-12)[\[87\]](#page-17-2)

At the individual level, some texts of Hinduism outline [four āśramas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashrama_(stage)), or stages of life as individual's *dharma*. These are:[\[88\]](#page-17-3) (1) [brahmacārya,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmacharya) the life of preparation as a student, (2) gṛ[hastha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grihastha), the life of the householder with family and other social roles, (3) [vānprastha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanaprastha) or aranyaka, the life of the forest-dweller, transitioning from worldly occupations to reflection and renunciation, and (4) [sannyāsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanny%C4%81sa), the life of giving away all property, becoming a recluse and devotion to moksa, spiritual matters. Patrick Olivelle suggests that "ashramas represented life choices rather than sequential steps in the life of a single individual" and the vanaprastha stage was added before renunciation over time, thus forming life stages.[\[89\]](#page-17-4)

The four stages of life complete the four human strivings in life, according to Hinduism.[\[90\]](#page-17-5) *Dharma* enables the individual to satisfy the striving for stability and order, a life that is lawful and harmonious, the striving to do the right thing, be good, be virtuous, earn religious merit, be helpful to others, interact successfully with society. The other three strivings are [Artha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artha) – the striving for means of life such as food, shelter, power, security, material wealth, and so forth; [Kama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kama) – the striving for sex, desire, pleasure, love, emotional fulfilment, and so forth; and [Moksa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moksa) – the striving for spiritual meaning, liberation from liferebirth cycle, self-realisation in this life, and so forth. The four stages are neither independent nor exclusionary in Hindu *dharma*. [\[90\]](#page-17-5)

### **Dharma and poverty**

According to Adam Bowles,[\[91\]](#page-17-6) [Shatapatha Brahmana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shatapatha_Brahmana) verse 11.1.6.24 links social prosperity and *dharma* through water. It claims that waters come from rains; when rains are abundant, there is prosperity on the earth, and this prosperity enables people to follow *Dharma* – moral and lawful life. In times of distress, of drought, of poverty, everything suffers, including relations between human beings and the human ability to live according to *dharma*. [\[91\]](#page-17-6)

In Rajadharmaparvan 91.34-8, the relationship between poverty and *dharma* reaches a full circle. A land with less moral and lawful life suffers distress, and as distress rises it causes more immoral and unlawful life, which further increases distress.[\[91\]](#page-17-6)[\[92\]](#page-17-7) Those in power must follow the raja dharma (that is, dharma of rulers), because this enables the society and the individual to follow dharma and achieve prosperity. [\[93\]](#page-17-8)

#### **Dharma and law**

The notion of *dharma* as duty or propriety is found in India's ancient legal and religious texts. Common examples of such use are pitri dharma (meaning a person's duty as a father), putra dharma (a person's duty as a son), raj dharma (a person's duty as a king) and so forth.[\[28\]](#page-13-11) In Hindu philosophy, justice, social harmony, and happiness requires that people live per *dharma*. The [Dharmashastra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharmashastra) is a record of these guidelines and rules.[\[94\]](#page-17-9) The available evidence suggest India once had a large collection of *dharma* related literature (sutras, shastras); four of the sutras survive and these are now referred to as Dharmasutras.[\[80\]](#page-16-10) Along with laws of Manu in Dharmasutras, exist parallel and different compendium of laws, such as the laws of Narada and other ancient scholars.[\[95\]](#page-17-10)[\[96\]](#page-17-11) These different and conflicting law books are neither exclusive, nor do they supersede other sources of *dharma* in Hinduism. These Dharmasutras include instructions on education of the young, their rites of passage, customs, religious rites and rituals, marital rights and obligations, death and ancestral rites, laws and administration of justice, crimes, punishments, rules and types of evidence, duties of a king, as well as morality. [\[80\]](#page-16-10)

# **Buddhism**

Buddhism held the Hindu view of *Dharma* as "cosmic law", as in the working of [Karma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karma). [\[1\]](#page-11-6) The term Dharma ([Pali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pali_language): *dhamma*) later came to refer to the teachings of [the Buddha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Buddha) (*pariyatti*); the practice (*pa*ṭ*ipatti*) of the Buddha's teachings is then comprehended as Dharma.[\[1\]](#page-11-6)[\[97\]](#page-17-12) In [Buddhist philosophy,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_philosophy) *dhamma/dharma* is also the term for ["phenomena"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomena).[\[97\]](#page-17-12)[\[2\]](#page-11-8)

#### **Buddha's teachings**

For practising Buddhists, references to *dharma* (*dhamma* in Pali) particularly as "the dharma", generally means the teachings of the Buddha, commonly known throughout the East as Buddhadharma. It includes especially the discourses on the fundamental principles (such as the [Four Noble Truths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Noble_Truths) and the [Noble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path) [Eightfold Path\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path), as opposed to the parables and to the poems. The Buddha's teachings explain that in order to end suffering, *dharma*, or the right thoughts, understanding, actions and livelihood, should be cultivated.[\[98\]](#page-17-13)

The status of *dharma* is regarded variably by different Buddhist traditions. Some regard it as an ultimate truth, or as the fount of all things which lie beyond the "[three realms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_realms)" (Sanskrit: *tridhatu*) and the "wheel of becoming" (Sanskrit: *[bhavachakra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhavachakra)*). Others, who regard the Buddha as simply an enlightened human being, see the *dharma* as the [essence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essence) of the "84,000 different aspects of the teaching" (Tibetan: *chos-sgo brgyad-khri bzhi strong*) that the Buddha gave to various types of people, based upon their individual propensities and capabilities.

Dharma refers not only to the sayings of the [Buddha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Buddha), but also to the later traditions of interpretation and addition that the various [schools of Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schools_of_Buddhism) have developed to help explain and to expound upon the Buddha's teachings. For others still, they see the *dharma* as referring to the "truth", or the ultimate reality of "the way that things really are" (Tibetan: *Chö*).

The *dharma* is one of the [Three Jewels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Jewels) of Buddhism in which practitioners of Buddhism seek refuge, or that upon which one relies for his or her lasting happiness. The Three Jewels of Buddhism are the [Buddha,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhahood) meaning the mind's perfection of enlightenment, the *[dharma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharma_(Buddhism))*, meaning the teachings and the methods of the Buddha, and the [Sangha,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sangha_(Buddhism)) meaning the community of practitioners who provide one another guidance and support.

#### **Chan Buddhism**

Dharma is employed in [Chan Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chan_Buddhism) in a specific context in relation to transmission of authentic doctrine, understanding and bodhi; recognised in [dharma transmission.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharma_transmission)

### **Theravada Buddhism**

In [Theravada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theravada) Buddhism obtaining ultimate realisation of the dhamma is achieved in three phases; learning, practising and realising.[\[99\]](#page-17-14)

#### In Pali:

- 1. [Pariyatti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pariyatti) the learning of the theory of *dharma* as contained within the suttas of the Pali canon
- 2. Patipatti putting the theory into practice and
- 3. Pativedha when one penetrates the *dharma* or through experience realises the truth of it.[\[99\]](#page-17-14)
