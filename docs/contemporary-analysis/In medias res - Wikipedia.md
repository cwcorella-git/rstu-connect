# *In medias res*

A narrative work beginning *in medias res* [\(Classical Latin:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Latin_language) [ɪ[n](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Latin) [ˈ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Latin)[m](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Latin)ɛdɪ.aː[s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Latin) ˈ[re](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Latin)ːs], lit. "into the middle of things") opens in the chronological middle of the plot, rather than at the beginning (cf. *[ab ovo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ab_ovo)*, *[ab initio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ab_initio)*).[\[1\]](#page-2-0) Often, [exposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exposition_(narrative)) is initially bypassed, instead filled in gradually through dialogue, fl[ashbacks,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flashback_(narrative)) or description of past events. For example, *[Hamlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamlet)* begins after the death of Hamlet's father, which is later discovered to have been a murder. Characters make reference to [King Hamlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Hamlet)'s death without the plot's first establishment of this fact. Since the play is about Hamlet and the revenge more so than the motivation, [Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare) uses *in medias res* to bypass superfluous exposition.

Works that employ *in medias res* often later use flashback and [nonlinear narrative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonlinear_narrative) for exposition to fill in the [backstory.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backstory) In [Homer'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer)s *[Odyssey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odyssey)*, the reader first learns about [Odysseus'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odysseus)s journey when he is held captive on [Ogygia,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ogygia) [Calypso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calypso_(mythology))'s island. The reader then finds out, in Books IX through XII, that the greater part of Odysseus's journey precedes that moment in the narrative. In Homer's *[Iliad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iliad)* there are fewer flashbacks, although it opens in the thick of the [Trojan War.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trojan_War)

## **First use of the phrase**

The Roman lyric poet and satirist [Horace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horace) (65–8 BC) first used the terms *[ab ovo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ab_ovo)* ("from the egg") and *in mediās rēs* ("into the middle of things") in his *[Ars Poetica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ars_Poetica_(Horace))* ("Poetic Arts", c. 13 BC), wherein lines 147–149 describe the ideal [epic poet:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_poetry)[\[2\]](#page-2-1)

Nor does he begin the [Trojan War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trojan_War) *from the egg*, but always he hurries to the action, and snatches the listener *into the middle of things* ...

The word "egg" reference is to the [mythological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mythology) origin of the [Trojan War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trojan_War) in the birth of [Helen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helen_of_Troy) and [Clytemnestra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clytemnestra) from the double egg laid by [Leda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leda_(mythology)) following her seduction by [Zeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeus) in the guise of a [swan.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swan) Compare the *[Iliad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iliad)*, which begins nine years after the start of the Trojan War, rather than at its beginning.

# **Literary history**

With likely origins in [oral tradition,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_tradition) the narrative technique of beginning a story *in medias res* is a stylistic convention of [epic poetry,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_poetry) the exemplars in Western literature being the *Iliad* and the *Odyssey* (both 7th century BC), by [Homer.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer)[\[3\]](#page-2-2) Likewise, the *[Mah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mah%C4%81bh%C4%81rata)ābhārata* (c. 8th century BC – c. 4th century AD) opens *in medias res*.

The [classical-era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classics) poet [Virgil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgil) (Publius Vergilius Maro, 70–19 BC) continued this literary narrative technique in the *[Aeneid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeneid)*, which is part of the Roman literary tradition of imitating [Homer.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer)[\[3\]](#page-2-2) Later works starting *in medias res* include the story ["The Three Apples"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Three_Apples) from the *[One Thousand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Thousand_and_One_Nights) [and One Nights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Thousand_and_One_Nights)* (c. 9th century),[\[4\]](#page-2-3) the Italian *[Divine Comedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_Comedy)* (1320) by [Dante Alighieri,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dante_Alighieri)[\[5\]](#page-2-4)[\[6\]](#page-2-5) the German *[Nibelungenlied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nibelungenlied)* (12th century), the Spanish *[Cantar de Mio Cid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantar_de_Mio_Cid)* (c. 14th century),[\[7\]](#page-2-6) the Portuguese *[The Lusiads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lusiads)* (1572) by [Luís de Camões](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lu%C3%ADs_de_Cam%C3%B5es)[,\[8\]](#page-2-7) *[Jerusalem Delivered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem_Delivered)* (1581) by [Torquato Tasso,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torquato_Tasso) *[Paradise Lost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise_Lost)* (1667) by [John Milton,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Milton)[\[9\]](#page-2-8) and generally in [Modernist literature.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernist_literature)

Modern novelists using *in medias res* with flashbacks include [William Faulkner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Faulkner) and [Toni Morrison.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toni_Morrison)

[Edgar Allan Poe'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_Allan_Poe)s ["The Tell-Tale Heart"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tell-Tale_Heart) is written *in medias res*. [\[10\]](#page-3-0)

Some biographers start with a turning point in the subject's life. For example, [David Mc Cullough'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Mc Cullough)s *[Truman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truman_(book))* begins with his World War I experience.

### **Cinematic history**

It is typical for fi[lm noir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_noir) to begin *in medias res*; for example, a private detective will enter the plot already in progress[.\[11\]](#page-3-1) *[Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crossfire_(film))fire* (1947) opens with the murder of Joseph Samuels. As the police investigate the crime, the story behind the murder is told via flashbacks.[\[12\]](#page-3-2) *[Dead Reckoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_Reckoning_(1947_film))* (1947) opens with [Humphrey Bogart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humphrey_Bogart) as Rip Murdock on the run and attempting to hide in a Catholic church. Inside, the backstory is told in flashback as Murdock explains his situation to a priest.[\[12\]](#page-3-2)

The technique has been used across genres, including dramas such as *[Through a Glass Darkly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Through_a_Glass_Darkly_(film))* (1961),[\[13\]](#page-3-3) *[8½](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/8%C2%BD)* (1963),[\[13\]](#page-3-3) *[Raging Bull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raging_Bull)* (1980), and *[City of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_God_(2002_film))* (2002);[\[14\]](#page-3-4) crime thrillers such as *[No Way Out](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_Way_Out_(1987_film))* (1987), *[Grievous Bodily Harm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grievous_Bodily_Harm)* (1988),[\[15\]](#page-3-5) *[The Usual Suspects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Usual_Suspects)* (1995),[\[16\]](#page-3-6) and *[Kill Bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kill_Bill) [Volume 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kill_Bill)* (2004);[\[17\]](#page-3-7) horror films such as *[Firestarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firestarter_(1984_film))* (1984);[\[18\]](#page-3-8) action films such as many in the [James Bond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Bond_(film_series)) franchise;[\[16\]](#page-3-6)[\[19\]](#page-3-9) and comedies such as *[Dr. Strangelove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dr._Strangelove)* (1964)[.\[13\]](#page-3-3) Some have argued that *[Star Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Wars)* takes advantage of this technique because its first-released film, *[A New Hope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Wars_(film))*, is the fourth episode of a nine-part epic[.\[20\]](#page-3-10)

[Superhero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superhero_films) films with a satirical edge such as *[Deadpool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deadpool_(film))* (2016) and *[Birds of Prey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birds_of_Prey_(2020_film))* (2020) have utilized *in medias res* to frame their stories[.\[21\]](#page-3-11)

[Animated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animated_films) films such as *[Grave of the Fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grave_of_the_Fireflies)flies* (1988), *[The Emperor's New Groove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Emperor%27s_New_Groove)* (2000), *[Hoodwinked!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoodwinked!)* (2005), *[Happily N'Ever After](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Happily_N%27Ever_After)* (2006), *[Megamind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megamind)* (2010), and *[The Mitchells vs. the Machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mitchells_vs._the_Machines)* (2021) have opening scenes *in medias res*, with a brief but significant scene that foreshadows the events that occurred earlier. This scene is then seen again afterwards (although in a different way than how it was shown at the beginning).

Many [war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_films) films, such as *[The Thin Red Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Thin_Red_Line_(1998_film))* (1998), also begin *in medias res*, with the protagonists already actively in combat and no prior domestic scenes leading up to the film's events[.\[22\]](#page-3-12)
