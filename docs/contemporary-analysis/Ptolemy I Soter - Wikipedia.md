# **Ptolemy I Soter**

**Ptolemy I Soter** [\(/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English)[ˈ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English)[t](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English)[ɒ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English)[l](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English)ə[mi/;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English) [Greek:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) Πτολεμαῖος Σωτήρ, *Ptolemaîos Sōt*ḗ*r*, "Ptolemy [the](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soter) [Savior"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soter); c. 367 BC – January 282 BC) was a [Macedonian Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Macedonians)[\[2\]](#page-8-0) general, historian, and [successor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diadochi) of [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great) who went on to found the [Ptolemaic Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_Kingdom) centered on [Egypt.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt) Ptolemy was *[basileus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basileus)* and [pharaoh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharaoh) of [Ptolemaic Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_Egypt) from 305/304 BC to his death in 282 BC[,\[note 1\]](#page-7-0)[\[3\]](#page-8-1) and [his descendants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_dynasty) continued to rule Egypt until 30 BC. During their rule, Egypt became a thriving bastion of [Hellenistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic) civilization and [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria) a great seat of [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_culture) [culture.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_culture)

Ptolemy I was the son of [Arsinoe of Macedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arsinoe_of_Macedon) by either her husband [Lagus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagus) or [Philip II of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_II_of_Macedon) [Macedon,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_II_of_Macedon) the father of Alexander. However, the latter is unlikely and may be a myth fabricated to glorify the [Ptolemaic Dynasty.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_Dynasty)[\[4\]](#page-8-2) Ptolemy was one of Alexander's most trusted companions and military officers. After the [death of Alexander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_of_Alexander) in 323 BC, Ptolemy retrieved his body as it was en route to be buried in Macedon, placing it in [Memphis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memphis,_Egypt) instead, where it was later moved to Alexandria in a new [tomb.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomb_of_Alexander_the_Great) Afterwards he joined a coalition against [Perdiccas,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perdiccas) the royal regent over [Philip III of Macedon.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_III_of_Macedon) The latter invaded Egypt but was assassinated by his own officers in 320 BC, allowing Ptolemy I to consolidate his control over the country. After a [series of wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wars_of_the_Diadochi) [between Alexander's successors,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wars_of_the_Diadochi) Ptolemy gained a claim to [Judea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judea) in southern [Syria,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria_(region)) which was disputed with the Seleucid king [Seleucus I.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucus_I) He also took control of [Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprus) and [Cyrenaica,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrenaica) the latter of which was placed under the control of Ptolemy's stepson [Magas.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magas_of_Cyrene) Ptolemy also commanded the construction of the [Library of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_of_Alexandria) and of the [Lighthouse of Alexandria,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lighthouse_of_Alexandria) one of the [Seven Wonders of the Ancient World.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Wonders_of_the_Ancient_World)

Ptolemy I may have married [Thaïs,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tha%C3%AFs) his mistress during the life of Alexander; he is known to have married the Persian noblewoman [Artakama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artakama) on Alexander's orders. He later married [Eurydice,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurydice_of_Egypt) daughter of the Macedonian regent [Antipater;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antipater) their sons [Ptolemy Keraunos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy_Keraunos) and [Meleager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meleager_(king)) ruled in turn as kings of Macedon. Ptolemy's final marriage was to Eurydice's cousin and lady-in-waiting, [Berenice I.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berenice_I) Upon his death, he was succeeded by his son with Berenice, [Ptolemy II.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy_II)

## **Early life and career**

Ptolemy was born in 367 BC[\[5\]](#page-8-3) in the ancient kingdom of [Macedon.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonia_(ancient_kingdom))[\[2\]](#page-8-0) His mother was [Arsinoe.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arsinoe_of_Macedon) According to [Satyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satyrus_the_Peripatetic) [the Peripatetic,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satyrus_the_Peripatetic) Arsinoe was a descendant of [Alexander I of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_I_of_Macedon) [Macedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_I_of_Macedon) and thus a member of the [Argead dynasty,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argead_dynasty) claiming ultimate descent from [Heracles.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heracles) Ostensibly, Ptolemy's father was [Lagus,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagus) a Macedonian nobleman from [Eordaea,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eordaea) but many ancient sources claim that he was actually an illegitimate son of [Philip II of Macedon.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_II_of_Macedon) If true, this would have made Ptolemy the half-brother of [Alexander the Great.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great) It is probable that this is a later myth fabricated to glorify the Ptolemaic dynasty.[\[6\]](#page-8-4) However, through his mother Ptolemy may have been a great-grandson of [Amyntas I of Macedon,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amyntas_I_of_Macedon) making him a member of the Argead royal house and a distant relative of Alexander, who was a great-great-grandson of Amyntas.[\[7\]](#page-8-5)[\[8\]](#page-8-6)

## ![](_page_1_Picture_4.jpeg)

Ptolemy served with Alexander from his first campaigns, and was among the seven *[somatophylakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somatophylakes)* (bodyguards) of Alexander. He played a principal part in the later campaigns in [Afghanistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afghanistan) and [India.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India)[\[9\]](#page-8-7) He participated in the [Battle of Issus,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Issus) commanding troops on the left wing under the authority of Parmenion. Later he accompanied Alexander during his journey to the [Oracle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oracle) in the [Siwa Oasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siwa_Oasis) where he was proclaimed a son of [Zeus.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeus)[\[10\]](#page-8-8) Ptolemy had his first independent command during the campaign against the rebel [Bessus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessus) whom his own guards captured and handed over to Ptolemy, who then handed him over to Alexander for execution.[\[11\]](#page-8-9)

# **Successor of Alexander**

When Alexander died in 323 BC, Ptolemy is said to have instigated the settlement of the empire made at [Babylon.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylon) Through the [Partition of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_of_Babylon) [Babylon,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_of_Babylon) he was appointed [satrap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satrap) of [Egypt,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt) under the nominal kings [Philip III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_III_of_Macedon) and the infant [Alexander IV;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_IV_of_Macedon) the former satrap, the Greek [Cleomenes,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleomenes_of_Naucratis) stayed on as his deputy. Ptolemy quickly moved, without authorization, to subjugate [Cyrenaica.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrenaica)[\[9\]](#page-8-7)

By custom, kings in Macedonia asserted their right to the throne by burying their predecessor. Probably because he wanted to pre-empt [Perdiccas,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perdiccas) the imperial regent, from staking his claim in this way, Ptolemy took great pains in acquiring the body of Alexander the Great. [On his deathbed,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_of_Alexander_the_Great) Alexander wished to be buried at the Temple of [Zeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeus_Ammon) [Ammon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeus_Ammon) in the [Siwa Oasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siwa_Oasis) of [ancient Libya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Libya) instead of the royal tombs of [Aigai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegae_(Macedonia)) in Macedon[.\[12\]](#page-8-10) However, his successors including Perdiccas attempted to bury his body in Macedon instead. In late 322 or early 321 BC, Alexander's body was in Syria, on its way to Macedon, when it was captured by Ptolemy I. ## **Rivalry and wars**

In 321 BC, Perdiccas attempted to invade Egypt, only to fall at the hands of his own men[.\[15\]](#page-8-13) Ptolemy's decision to defend the [Nile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile) against Perdiccas ended in fiasco for Perdiccas, with the loss of 2,000 men. This failure was a fatal blow to Perdiccas' reputation, and he was murdered in his tent by two of his subordinates. Ptolemy immediately crossed the Nile, to provide supplies to what had the day before been an enemy army. Ptolemy was offered the regency in place of Perdiccas, but he declined.[\[16\]](#page-8-14) Ptolemy was consistent in his policy of securing a power base, while never succumbing to the temptation of risking all to succeed Alexander[.\[17\]](#page-8-15)

In the long wars that followed between the different [Diadochi,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diadochi) Ptolemy's first goal was to hold Egypt securely, and his second was to secure control in the outlying areas: Cyrenaica and [Cyprus,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprus) as well as [Syria,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coele-Syria) including the province of [Judea.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judea) His first occupation of Syria was in 318, and he established at the same time a protectorate over the petty kings of Cyprus. When [Antigonus I,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antigonus_I) master of Asia in 315, showed expansionist ambitions, Ptolemy joined the coalition against him, and on the outbreak of war, evacuated Syria. In Cyprus, he fought the partisans of Antigonus, and re-conquered the island (313). A revolt in [Cyrene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrene,_Libya) was crushed the same year[.\[9\]](#page-8-7)

In 312, Ptolemy and [Seleucus,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucus_I_Nicator) the fugitive satrap of Babylonia, both invaded Syria, and defeated [Demetrius I,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demetrius_I_of_Macedon) the son of Antigonus, in the [Battle of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Gaza_(312_BC)) [Gaza.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Gaza_(312_BC)) Again he occupied Syria, and again—after only a few months, when Demetrius had won a battle over his general, and Antigonus entered [Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria) in force—he evacuated it. In 311, a peace was concluded between the combatants. Soon after this, the surviving 13-year-old king, Alexander

IV, was murdered in Macedonia on the orders of Cassander, leaving the satrap of Egypt absolutely his own master[.\[9\]](#page-8-7)

The peace did not last long, early in 310 he was informed that his ally [Nicocles of Paphos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicocles_of_Paphos) was planning to defect to Antigonus; he sent some agents, who together with his brother [Menelaus,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menelaus_(son_of_Lagus)) who was still on Cyprus with an army, dealt with the situation, they surrounded Nicocles palace and forced him to commit suicide[.\[18\]](#page-8-16) In 309 Ptolemy personally commanded a fleet which detached the coastal towns of [Phaselis,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phaselis) [Xanthos,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xanthos) [Kaunos,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaunos) [Iasos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iasos) and [Myndus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myndus) in [Lycia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycia) and [Caria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caria) from Antigonus, then crossed into Greece, where he took possession of [Corinth,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corinth) [Sicyon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicyon) and [Megara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megara) (308 BC). In 306, a great fleet under Demetrius attacked Cyprus, and Ptolemy's brother [Menelaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menelaus_(son_of_Lagus)) was defeated and captured in another decisive [Battle of Salamis.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Salamis_(306_BC)) Ptolemy's complete loss of Cyprus followed.[\[9\]](#page-8-7)

## ![](_page_3_Figure_4.jpeg)

The satraps Antigonus and Demetrius now each assumed the title of king; Ptolemy, as well as [Cassander,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassander) [Lysimachus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lysimachus) and [Seleucus I Nicator,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucus_I_Nicator) responded by doing the same. In the winter of 306 BC, Antigonus tried to follow up his victory in Cyprus by invading Egypt; but Ptolemy was strongest there, and successfully held the frontier against him. Ptolemy led no further overseas expeditions against Antigonus[.\[19\]](#page-8-17) However, he did send great assistance to [Rhodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhodes) when it was [besieged by Demetrius \(305/304\).](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Rhodes_(305%E2%80%93304_BC)) The Rhodians granted divine honors to Ptolemy as a result of the lifting of the siege[.\[20\]](#page-9-0)

When the coalition against Antigonus was renewed in 302, Ptolemy joined it, and invaded Syria a third time, while Antigonus was engaged with Lysimachus in [Asia Minor.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_Minor) On hearing a report that Antigonus had won a decisive victory there, he once again evacuated Syria. But when the news came that Antigonus had been defeated and slain by Lysimachus and Seleucus at the [Battle of Ipsus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Ipsus) in 301, he occupied Syria a fourth time.[\[19\]](#page-8-17)

The other members of the coalition had assigned all Syria to Seleucus, after what they regarded as Ptolemy's desertion, and for the next hundred years, the question of the ownership of southern Syria (i.e., Judea) produced recurring warfare between the [Seleucid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucid_dynasty) and Ptolemaic dynasties. Henceforth, Ptolemy seems to have involved himself as little as possible in the rivalries between [Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_Minor) and [Greece;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greece) he lost what he held in Greece, but reconquered Cyprus in 295/294. [Cyrenaica,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrene,_Libya) after a series of rebellions, was finally subjugated in about 300 and placed under his stepson [Magas.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magas_of_Cyrene)[\[19\]](#page-8-17)

## **Marriages, children, and succession**

While Alexander was alive, Ptolemy had three children with his mistress [Thaïs,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tha%C3%AFs) who may also have been his wife: Lagus; Leontiscus; and Eirene, who was given in marriage to Eunostos of Soloi in Cyprus. During the [Susa weddings,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susa_weddings) Ptolemy married Persian noblewoman [Artakama,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artakama) as ordered by Alexander the Great.[\[21\]](#page-9-1) Around [322 BC,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/322_BC) he married [Eurydice,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurydice_of_Egypt) daughter of [Antipater,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antipater) regent of Macedonia. They had five children before she was repudiated: three sons– [Ptolemy Ceraunus,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy_Ceraunus) king of [Macedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedon) from [281 BC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/281_BC) to [279 BC;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/279_BC) his brother and successor [Meleager,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meleager_(king)) who ruled for two months in 279 BC; and a 'rebel in Cyprus' who was put to death by his half-brother [Ptolemy II–](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy_II)as well as the daughters Ptolemais, who married [Demetrius I of Macedon,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demetrius_I_of_Macedon) and [Lysandra,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lysandra) first married to [Alexander V of Macedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_V_of_Macedon) and after to Lysimachus' son [Agathocles.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agathocles_(son_of_Lysimachus)) [\[21\]](#page-9-1)[\[22\]](#page-9-2)[\[23\]](#page-9-3)[\[24\]](#page-9-4)[\[25\]](#page-9-5)[\[26\]](#page-9-6) Ptolemy married once more to [Berenice,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berenice_I_of_Egypt) Eurydice's cousin, who had come to Egypt as Eurydice's lady-in-waiting with the children from her first marriage to [Philip.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_(husband_of_Berenice_I_of_Egypt)) Their children were [Arsinoe II,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arsinoe_II) [Philotera,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philotera) and Ptolemy II. Their eldest child Arsinoe married Lysimachus, then her half-brother Ptolemy Keraunos, and finally her full brother Ptolemy II.[\[22\]](#page-9-2)[\[27\]](#page-9-7)

# **Economic reforms**

The Ptolemaic economy consisted of two ancient economic systems, linked by a bureaucratic hierarchy, but also by increased market activity, which was stimulated by the minting of coins and the formation of a state-controlled fiscal policy. The main sources of income were based on the cultivation of grain and the proceeds from the circulation of money, from its own trade, as well as tax revenues from the movement of goods. Another element of economic development was the ability to transport in the Mediterranean region, where the port of Alexandria played a dominant role.[\[30\]](#page-9-10)[\[31\]](#page-9-11)

Ptolemy directly owned almost half of Egypt's agricultural land, and Egyptian exports of grain, linen, and other goods helped finance the expansion of the empire. The population is estimated to have been about 3 million, and anyone who produced cloth, papyrus, or beer also had to comply with strict government controls and pay taxes on their sales. One advantage of all these processes was that the government could pay for improvements to the system and strengthen the economy. This included irrigation projects that expanded agricultural land. In practice, Egypt was divided into administrative provinces called nomes. At the nome level, officials dealt with municipal and village authorities to resolve economic issues such as land administration, taxation, and currency circulation. The Macedonians and Greeks who owned the land and traded its proceeds did not work the land themselves the tenant farmers were mostly local Egyptians.[\[32\]](#page-9-12)

The evolving fiscal policy was based on reforms in the circulation of monetary units—coins—minted from precious metals (gold, silver, copper) as early as the 5th–4th centuries BC (for example, under Pharaoh [Nectanebo II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nectanebo_II) of the 30th Dynasty). At the time of [Alexander's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great) conquest of Egypt, the

Egyptian economy was in the early stages of monetisation. The main coin was the Athenian silver [tetradrachm,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetradrachm) called the *[owl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Owl)* which served as international currency in Egypt and the Levant in the 5th and 4th centuries BC. The *owls* circulating in Egypt included coins from the Athenian mint, as well as imitations minted locally, in the Nile Delta and around [Memphis,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memphis,_Egypt) but also in more distant areas such as the island of [Elephantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elephantine) and the oasis of [Kharga.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharga_Oasis)[\[33\]](#page-9-13)[\[34\]](#page-9-14)

To cope with the limited supply of silver, Ptolemy Soter reduced the silver and gold drachmas to 80% of their original weight by 305 BC. The standard of Ptolemaic currency as a whole was then frequently changed, especially when later rulers devalued the currency by reducing its precious metal content to finance their wars or cope with economic hardship. Around 299 BC, Ptolemy I Soter replaced the older gold stater of [Alexander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great) with his own image on the obverse, with coins whose weight was adjusted to the standard 14.25 g[.\[35\]](#page-10-0)

This not only distinguished Egypt from other territories, but also, perhaps more importantly, allowed Ptolemy to expand his currency supply to keep up with the growth of military and judicial spending. Aspects of these later reforms included the creation of fiscal institutions that increased state revenues, and most notably a closed monetary zone with mandatory exchange of other coins at the borders. Around 294 BC a commission was tasked with exchanging gold for silver. The history of his monetary reforms shows him to have been an unusually energetic and capable ruler, as a result of which, together with his military successes was recognised as the legitimate pharaoh of Egypt[.\[36\]](#page-10-1)

### **Historian**

Ptolemy himself wrote an eyewitness history of Alexander's campaigns (now lost).[\[37\]](#page-10-2) In the second century AD,

Ptolemy's history was used by [Arrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrian) of Nicomedia as one of his two main primary sources (alongside the history of [Aristobulus of Cassandreia\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristobulus_of_Cassandreia) for his own extant *[Anabasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Anabasis_of_Alexander)* of Alexander, and hence large parts of Ptolemy's history can be assumed to survive in paraphrase or précis in Arrian's work[.\[38\]](#page-10-3) Arrian cites Ptolemy by name on only a few occasions, but it is likely that large stretches of Arrian's *Anabasis* reflect Ptolemy's version of events. Arrian once names Ptolemy as the author "whom I chiefly follow"[,\[39\]](#page-10-4) and in his Preface writes that Ptolemy seemed to him to be a particularly trustworthy source, "not only because he was present with Alexander on campaign, but also because he was himself a king, and hence lying would be more dishonourable for him than for anyone else"[.\[40\]](#page-10-5)

## Ptolemy Soter gold pentadrachm

Ptolemy's lost history was long considered an objective work, distinguished by its straightforward honesty and sobriety,[\[19\]](#page-8-17) but more recent work has called this assessment into question. R. M. Errington argued that Ptolemy's history was characterised by persistent bias and self-aggrandisement, and by systematic blackening of the reputation of [Perdiccas,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perdiccas) one of Ptolemy's chief dynastic rivals after Alexander's death[.\[41\]](#page-10-6) For example, Arrian's account of the fall of [Thebes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thebes,_Greece) in 335 BC (*Anabasis* [1.8.1–1.8.8,](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Anabasis_of_Alexander/Book_I/Chapter_VIII) a rare section of narrative explicitly attributed to Ptolemy by Arrian) shows several significant variations from the parallel account preserved in [Diodorus Siculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diodorus_Siculus) [\(17.11–12 \(https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Diodorus\\_Siculus/17A\\*.html#8.3\)\)](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Diodorus_Siculus/17A*.html#8.3), most notably in attributing a distinctly unheroic role in proceedings to Perdiccas. More recently, J. Roisman has argued that the case for Ptolemy's blackening of Perdiccas and others has been much exaggerated[.\[42\]](#page-10-7)

## **Euclid**

Ptolemy personally sponsored the great [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mathematics) [Euclid.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid) He found Euclid's seminal work, the *[Elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid%27s_Elements)*, too difficult to study, so he asked if there were an easier way to master it. According to [Proclus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proclus), Euclid famously quipped: "Sire, there is no [Royal Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Road) to [geometry.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry)"[\[43\]](#page-10-8)
