## **Albert Ellis**

## **Psychotherapist who preached a rational, behavioural approach**

## **Published: 30 July 2007**

**Albert Ellis, psychologist and psychotherapist: born Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania 27 September 1913; three times married; died New York 24 July 2007.**

Albert Ellis was one of the best known and most influential psychotherapists in the post-Freudian era. He came to be known for an approach he called rational emotive behaviour therapy (REBT). He thought that people "can improve their lives by taking control of self-defeating thoughts, feelings, and behaviours." He put forth his ideas in his 1957 book How to Live with a Neurotic, and in 1959 established the Institute for Rational Living in New York (now the Albert Ellis Institute).

In July 1982 a survey of clinical psychologists by the American Psychological Association ranked him as the second most influential psychotherapist, behind Carl Rogers, but ahead of Sigmund Freud, at number three, and Carl Jung. A review that year of psychotherapy journals found him to be the most frequently cited author of works published after 1957.

Like the Greek Stoic philosopher Epictetus, whom he liked to quote, he believed that in order to change your life, you have to change your thinking. Like Marcus Aurelius, the student of Epictetus, Ellis thought that our suffering is due not so much to the events in our lives, but to the way we interpret them. According to Ellis much human unhappiness is based upon irrational assumptions: people create their own problems in life and can think their way out of them only by approaching life rationally.

He observed that people's personal philosophies and beliefs often drove them to distraction. Some examples he gave of what he called "crooked thinking" are the demand for perfection in oneself or others, the need to be appreciated, and the fear of not finding anyone to love. He devised practices to uncover and undo these habits of mind.

In one exercise he encouraged patients to imagine situations that normally provoke extreme fear, panic or rage. He asked the patients to hold the imaginary situations in their minds and to change their feelings to acceptance. He said that the exercise practiced daily for a month can help people to change their most deep-seated feelings about situations.

He preached "USA, Unconditional Self-Acceptance," which meant, "You always accept you no matter what you do." He also taught "Unconditional Other Acceptance: Nobody is evil, even if they do evil things" and "Unconditional Life Acceptance: You always accept things, no matter how they are". He was an energetic promoter of his ideas, writing over 75 books and many articles. The Albert Ellis Institute he founded trained professionals, ran workshops and produced tapes. Among his books were sex manuals, but most dealt with his rational emotive behaviour therapy.

In a book entitled How to Stubbornly Refuse to Make Yourself Miserable About Anything - Yes, Anything! (1988) he wrote that most emotional disturbance stems from arrogant thinking . . . And when you demand that you must not have failings, you can also demand that you must not be neurotic . . . Neurosis still comes mainly from you . . . And you can choose to stop your nonsense and to stubbornly refuse to make yourself neurotic about virtually anything.

In How to Keep People From Pushing Your Buttons (1995) he said that a "type of screwball thinking" is "absolutist thinking".

Some of us walk around all day long getting on our own cases: "I've got to do this. I've got to do that. I should have said this to that person. I need to be more that. I ought to be more organized. I should be more attractive, intelligent, witty, popular and personable. I ought to be more assertive. I need to be less aggressive. I've got to speak up more. I really need to keep my mouth shut." . . . Some of us "should on ourselves" all day long!

"The trouble with most therapy," he said in a 2004 article in The New York Times, "is that it helps you to feel better, but you don't get better. You have to back it up with action, action, action." His conviction was that it is always possible to change, and a therapist's job is to nudge the person, gently or otherwise, in the right direction. That view defined his career and helped to usher in an emphasis on quick results.

At about the same time that Ellis was developing rational emotive behaviour therapy, an American psychiatrist, Aaron Beck, developed similar ideas and methods. What is now called cognitive behaviour therapy - CBT - owes its origins in a large way to both men. Research has shown that CBT is effective in treating depression, relieving anxiety and diminishing other kinds of distress. Unlike Beck, who subjected his methods and techniques to careful scientific testing, Ellis's views evolved in great part from personal experience.

He alleged that he had had undergone a major change in his own life. At age 19 he had seen himself as painfully shy. That summer he set himself a task. Hanging around a park bench in the New York Botanical Garden in the Bronx, he decided that every time a woman sat down on the bench alone, he would talk to her. Within a month he had approached 130 women. "Thirty walked away immediately. I talked with the other hundred, for the first time in my life, no matter how anxious I was. Nobody vomited and ran away. Nobody called the cops." He said he completely got over his shyness by "thinking differently, feeling differently and, in particular, acting differently".

In his own words, he "got to be one of the best picker-uppers of women in the United States, and finally started making it with them, a lot." He became a sexologist, writing books entitled Sex Without Guilt and Science of Love. "I just kept going and going and going". He admitted that the sexologist Alfred Kinsey was much better known. "But he wasn't read." said Ellis. 'They were reading my books.'

## He once wrote:

From the age of sixteen onward (in 1929), I read many books by Freud and his followers, but I could see that Sigmund was especially obsessed with the sexual "origins" of disturbance, especially with the ubiquitousness of the Oedipus complex. I could also see that he was an over-generaliser and a dogmatist, and therefore a poor scientist. But psychoanalytic details about sex helped to loosen me up; I came to consider practically all forms of non-coercive sex permissible. In fact, at the age of fifteen, I had my first and only homosexual episode - with my thirteen-year-old brother no less!

He was confrontational in style, urging people who were suffering to stop complaining and to "deal with it". He used to tell people not to "awfulise", meaning not to make something upsetting worse than it was. "Neurosis," he was fond of saying, "is just a high-class word for whining."

Ellis lived on the top floor of a six-storey townhouse in Manhattan's Upper East Side, which since 1964 has been the home of the Albert Ellis Institute. For decades he held forth at Friday night meetings of 50 to 100 colleagues and guests in what had once been the dining room of the house. He filled these evenings with insights and salty humour. From among the audience he would choose two volunteers for a half-hour each of "stand-up therapy", and invited ongoing comments from the audience during the sessions.

Ellis had lids for many pots, but was uninterested in the sorts of disorders that psychiatrists call psychosis - though, according to his autobiography, his mother had bipolar tendencies.

In 2003 he nearly died from an infection that led to the removal of his large intestine. He was then severely deaf and had to wear headphones, and guests had to shout into a microphone, In December 2006 about a dozen students went to see him at a nursing home. They were studying to be school counsellors. According to a journalist, despite Ellis's age and infirmity, he still "returned the students' volleys tartly and profanely with the suave assurance they expected." One student talked about growing up with a depressed father who made her feel guilty. "Only you can make you feel guilty," he nearly shouted at her.

Albert Ellis was born in 1913 to Jewish parents in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, and moved at the age of four to the Bronx, New York. He was the eldest of three children. The father was a businessman, often away from home on business trips. Ellis said in his autobiography that his mother was self-absorbed and moody. He recounted that his mother was usually sleeping when he left for school and was often not at home when he returned. Instead of feeling bitter, he took on the responsibility of caring for his siblings. He bought an alarm clock with his own money, and woke and dressed his younger brother and sister.

At age five Albert was hospitalised with nephritis, a kidney ailment. Later he was in hospital with tonsillitis. He reported that he had eight hospitalisations between the ages of five and seven, one of these lasting nearly a year. His parents provided little or no emotional support for him during these years.

He attended City College of New York, where his major subject was business administration, and got his bachelor's degree in 1934. He worked at various odd jobs, including assistant to the president of a novelty gift company. At the same time he was writing novels and plays, but none were published, so he gave up writing fiction. In 1942 he entered the clinical psychology course at Columbia

University in New York, where he got a master's degree in 1943 and a PhD in 1947.

Ellis underwent psychoanalytic training at the Karen Horney Institute in New York. As a practicing psychoanalyst from 1947 to 1953, he grew increasingly doubtful about the efficacy of that form of psychotherapy, concerned that talk would not help his clients if they did not take action against their habitual thoughts, feelings, and behaviours. He finally dropped psychoanalytic methods after spending - or "wasting" - as he put it - these years.

By late 1953, he had stopped calling himself a psychoanalyst and was developing his action-oriented rational emotive behaviour therapy. He was also doing sex and marriage therapy. His focus was not on examining memories of childhood, but on confronting the irrational thoughts that he believed led to self-destructive feelings and behaviour.

Ellis was a board member of the Albert Ellis Institute for over 40 years. In early 2004, with Albert in declining health, the board began to negotiate with him about his future role at the institute. Some members argued that paying for his expensive medical care would hobble the institute's finances. Some said they were uncomfortable with his confrontational style and eccentricities, and saw him as a liability.

Ellis had his defenders, who argued that the institute had fallen into the hands of psychologists who were trying to move it away from the revolutionary therapy techniques he had pioneered in the 1960s and 1970s. The two sides could not come to an agreement and in July 2005, institute officials cancelled the Friday evening sessions that had been a staple for many years and removed him as a trustee. They also fired Debbie Joffe, his longstanding assistant, and later his wife.

He sought to be reinstated and asked for damages to be paid to him. Among other things, he claimed age discrimination. In January 2006 a judge ruled that his dismissal was improper and he was returned to the board by court order.

He was married twice early in life. Then in 1964, in his early 50s, he started his longest relationship, with Janet Wolfe, an intern who rose to be Executive Director of the Albert Ellis Institute. She lived with him in an open relationship for 37 years, until she moved out in 2002. He had no children by these relationships or by any of his very many love affairs.
