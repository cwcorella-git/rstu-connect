# [Lumad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_Page)

The Lumad are a group of [Austronesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austronesian_peoples) [indigenous peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples) in the southern [Philippines.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippines) It is a [Cebuano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cebuano_language) term meaning "native" or "indigenous". The term is short for Katawhang Lumad (Literally: "indigenous people"), the [autonym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonym_(semantics)) officially adopted by the delegates of the Lumad Mindanao Peoples Federation (LMPF) founding assembly on 26 June 1986 at the Guadalupe Formation Center, Balindog, [Kidapawan,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kidapawan) [Cotabato,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotabato) Philippines[.\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-:0-1) Usage of the term was accepted in Philippine jurisprudence when [President](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_Philippines) [Corazon Aquino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corazon_Aquino) signed into law [Republic Act 6734,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_Region_in_Muslim_Mindanao) where the word was used in Art. XIII sec. 8(2) to distinguish Lumad ethnic communities from the islands of [Mindanao.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mindanao)[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-2)

Mindanao is home to a substantial part of the country's indigenous population, around 15% of the Philippines' total population of over 100 million.[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-National_Statistics_Office_2008,_p._119-3)

# History

The name Lumad grew out of the political awakening among tribes during the [martial law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martial_law_in_the_Philippines) regime of [President](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_Philippines) [Ferdinand Marcos.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_Marcos) It was advocated and propagated by the members and affiliates of Lumad-Mindanao, a coalition of all-Lumad local and regional organizations that formalized themselves as such in June 1986 but started in 1983 as a multi-sectoral organization. Lumad-Mindanao's main objective was to achieve selfdetermination for their member-tribes or, put more concretely, self-governance within their ancestral domain in accordance with their culture and customary laws. No other Lumad organization had the express goal in the past[.\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-:0-1)

Representatives from 15 tribes agreed in June 1986 to adopt the name; there were no delegates from the three major groups of the [T'boli,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%27boli) the Teduray. The choice of a [Cebuano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cebuano_language) word was a bit ironic but they deemed it appropriate as the Lumad tribes do not have any other [common language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lingua_franca) except Cebuano. This marked the first time that these tribes had agreed to a common name for themselves, distinct from that of other Mindanao native groups: the [Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim) [Moro peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moro_people) of southwestern Mindanao; and the sea-faring [Visayans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visayans) of coastal areas in northern and eastern Mindanao [\(Butuanon,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butuanon_people) [Surigaonon,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surigaonon_people) and [Kagay-anon,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cagayan_de_Oro) collectively known as the "Dumagat" or "Sea People" by the Lumad). All of which, in turn are distinct from the (mostly Visayan) migrant majority of modern Mindanao.

# Ethnic groups

The Lumad include groups such as the Erumanen ne Menuvu', Matidsalug Manobo, Agusanon Manobo, Dulangan Manobo, Dabaw Manobo, Ata Manobo, B'laan, Kaulo, Banwaon, Bukidnon, Teduray, Lambangian, Higaunon, Dibabawon, Mangguwangan, Mansaka, Mandaya, K'lagan, [Subanen,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subanon_people) [Tasaday,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tasaday) [Tboli,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tboli_people) Mamanuwa, Tagakaolo, Talaandig, Tagabawa, Ubu', Tinenanen, Kuwemanen, K'lata and Diyangan. Considered as "vulnerable groups", they live in [hinterlands,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinterland) forests, lowlands, and coastal areas[.\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-8)

The term lumad excludes the [Butuanons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butuanon_people) and [Surigaonons,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surigaonon_people) even though these two groups are also native to Mindanao. This is due to their [Visayan ethnicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visayans) and lack of close affinity with the Lumad. The [Moros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moro_people) like the [Maguindanaon,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maguindanao_people) [Maranao,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maranao_people) [Taus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taus%C5%ABg_people)ūg, [Sama-Bajau,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sama-Bajau) [Yakan,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakan_people) etc. are also excluded, despite being also native to Mindanao and despite some groups being closely related ethnolinguistically to the Lumad. This is because unlike the Lumad, the Moros converted to Islam during the 14th to 15th centuries. This can be confusing since the word lumad literally means "native" in [Bisayan languages.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bisayan_languages)

#### Bagobo

The Bagobo are one of the largest subgroups of the Manobo peoples. They comprise three subgroups: the Tagabawa, the Klata (or Guiangan), and the Ovu (also spelled Uvu or Ubo) peoples. The Bagobo were formerly nomadic and farmed through kaingin ["slash-and-burn"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slash-and-burn) methods. Their territory extends from [Davao Gulf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davao_Gulf) to [Mount Apo.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Apo) They are traditionally ruled by chieftains (matanum), a council of elders (magani), and [mabalian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filipino_shamans) or female shamans. The supreme spirit in their indigenous [anito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anito) religions is Eugpamolak Manobo or Manama.[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-Mangune-9)[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-de_Jong-10)[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-Carillo-11)

# Blaan

The [Blaan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blaan_people) is an indigenous group that is concentrated in [Davao del Sur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davao_del_Sur) and [South Cotabato.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Cotabato) They practice indigenous rituals while adapting to the way of life of modern Filipinos.[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-12)

#### Bukidnon

The Bukidnon are one of the seven tribes in the [Bukidnon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bukidnon) [plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plateau) of [Mindanao.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mindanao) Bukidnon means 'that of the mountains or highlands' (i.e., 'people of the mountains or highlands'), despite the fact that most Bukidnon tribes settle in the lowlands.

The name Bukidnon itself used to describe the entire [province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provinces_of_the_Philippines) in a different context (it means 'mountainous lands' in this case) or could also be the [collective name of the permanent residents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demonym) in the province regardless of ethnicity.[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-13)

The Bukidnon people believe in one god, Magbabaya (Ruler of All), though there are several minor gods and goddesses that they worship as well. Religious rites are presided by a baylan whose ordination is voluntary and may come from any sex. The Bukidnons have rich musical and oral tradition[s\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-14) which are celebrated annually in [Malaybalay city's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaybalay) [Kaamulan Festival,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaamulan) with other tribes in Bukidnon (the Manobo tribes, the Higaonon, Matigsalug, Talaandig, Umayamnom, and the Tigwahanon)[.\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-15)

The Bukidnon Lumad is distinct from and should not be confused with the [Visayan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visayans) [Suludnon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suludnon) people of [Panay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panay) and a few indigenous peoples scattered in the Visayas area who are also alternatively referred to as "Bukidnon" (also meaning "highland people").

#### Higaonon

The Higaonon are mainly located in the entire province of [Misamis Oriental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misamis_Oriental) as well as northern parts of [Bukidnon,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bukidnon) western parts of [Agusan del Norte,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agusan_del_Norte) western parts of [Agusan del Sur,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agusan_del_Sur) [Camiguin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camiguin) (used to be Kamiguing), Rogongon in [Iligan City,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iligan_City) and eastern parts of [Lanao del Norte.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lanao_del_Norte) The Higaonons have a rather traditional way of living. Farming is their most important economic activity.

The word Higaonon is derived from the word "higa" in the Higaonon dialect, which means coastal plains, and "gaon" meaning ascend to the mountains. Taken together, Higaonon, means the people of the coastal plains that ascended to the mountains. Higaonons were formerly coastal people of the provinces, as mentioned, who resisted the Spanish occupation and later avoided contact with southward migrants from Luzon and Visayas during the late Spanish colonial period and since American colonial and postwar eras seeking better economics opportunities awaiting them in Higaonon homelands. Driven to the hills and mountains, these people continued to exist and fought for the preservation of their people, heritage, and culture.

#### Kalagan

The Kalagan, also spelled K'lagan or (by the Spanish) Caragan, are a subgroup of the Mandaya-Mansaka people who speak the [Kalagan language.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalagan_language) They comprise three subgroups which are usually treated as different tribes: the Tagakaulo, the Kagan, and the Kallao people of [Samal.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samal_Island) They are native to areas within [Davao del Sur,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davao_del_Sur) [Compostela Valley,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compostela_Valley) [Davao del Norte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davao_del_Norte) (including Samal Island), [Davao Oriental,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davao_Oriental) and [North Cotabato;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Cotabato) between the territories of the [Blaan people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blaan_people) and the coastline. The [Caraga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caraga) region is named after them. Their name means "spirited people" or "brave people", from [kalag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anito), ("spirit" or "soul"). They were historically composed of small warring groups. Their population, as of 1994, is 87,270.[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-16)[\[17\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-17)[\[18\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-18)

#### Kamigin

A subgroup of the [Manobo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#Manobo) from the island of [Camiguin.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camiguin) They speak the [Kamigin language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamigin_language) and are closely related to the Manobo groups from [Surigao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surigao_del_Norte) [del Norte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surigao_del_Norte)[.\[19\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-19)

### Mamanwa

The Mamanwa are a [Negrito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negrito) tribe often grouped together with the Lumad. They come from [Leyte,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leyte) [Agusan del Norte,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agusan_del_Norte) and [Surigao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surigao_(province)) provinces in Mindanao; primarily in [Kitcharao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitcharao,_Agusan_del_Norte) and [Santiago,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santiago,_Agusan_del_Norte) Agusan del Norte,[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-20) though they are lesser in number and more scattered and nomadic than the Manobos and Mandaya tribes who also inhabit the region. Like all Negritos, the Mamanwas are phenotypically distinct from the lowlanders and the upland Manobos, exhibiting curly hair and much darker skin tones.

They are traditionally [hunter-gatherers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunter-gatherers)[\[21\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-Omoto_K_1989-21) and consume a wide variety of wild plants, herbs, insects, and animals from [tropical rainforests.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_rainforest) The Mamanwa are categorized as having the "negrito" [phenotype:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenotype) dark skin, kinky hair, and short stature.[\[21\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-Omoto_K_1989-21)[\[22\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-22) The origins of this phenotype (found in the [Agta,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agta) [Ati,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ati_people) and [Aeta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeta) tribes in the Philippines) are a continued topic of debate, with recent evidence suggesting that the phenotype convergently evolved in several areas of southeast Asia[.\[23\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-23)

However, recent genomic evidence suggests that the Mamanwa were one of the first populations to leave Africa along with peoples in New Guinea and Australia, and that they diverged from a common origin about 36,000 years ago.[\[24\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-24)

Currently, Mamanwa populations live in sedentary settlements ("barangays") that are close to agricultural peoples and market centers. As a result, a substantial proportion of their diet includes starch-dense domesticated foods.[\[25\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-ucsc.academia.edu-25) The extent to which agricultural products are bought or exchanged varies in each Mamanwa settlement with some individuals continuing to farm and produce their own domesticated foods while others rely on purchasing food from market centers. The Mamanwa have been exposed to many of the modernities mainstream agricultural populations possess and use, such as cell phones, televisions, radio, and processed foods[.\[25\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-ucsc.academia.edu-25)

The political system of the Mamanwa is informally democratic and age-structured. Elders are respected and expected to maintain peace and order within the tribe. The chieftain, called a Tambayon, usually takes on the duties of counseling tribal members, speaking at gatherings, and arbitrating disagreements. The chieftain may be a man or a woman, which is characteristic of other gender-egalitarian hunter-gatherer societies.[\[26\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-26) They believe in a collection of spirits, governed by the [supreme deity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creator_deity) Magbabaya, although it appears that their

Mamanwa (also spelled Mamanoa) means 'first forest dwellers', from the words man (first) and banwa (forest).[\[27\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-27) They speak the [Mamanwa language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamanwa_language) (or Minamanwa).[\[28\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-28) They are genetically related to the [Denisovans.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denisovans)[\[29\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-reichdenadse-29)

## Mandaya

"Mandaya" derives from "man" meaning "first", and "daya" meaning "upstream" or "upper portion of a river", and therefore means "the first people upstream". It refers to a number of groups found along the mountain ranges of Davao Oriental, as well as to their customs, language, and beliefs. The Mandaya are also found in Compostela and New Bataan in [Compostela Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compostela_Valley) (formerly a part of Davao del Norte Province).

### Manobo

Manobo is the [hispanicized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispanicized) spelling of the [endonym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endonym) Manuvu (also spelled Menuvu or Minuvu). Its etymology is unclear; in its current form, it means "person" or "people." It is believed that it is derived from the rootword tuvu, which means "to grow"/"growth" (thus Man[t]uvu would be "[native]-grown" or "aboriginal").[\[30\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-Sevilla-30)

The Manobo are considered the most diverse among the many [indigenous peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Philippines) [of the Philippines,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Philippines) with the largest number of subgroups within its family of languages[.\[31\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-NCCAprofile-31) The Philippines' National Commission on Culture and the Arts has been able to develop a tentative classification of Manobo subgroups, but notes that "the various subgroupings are not sufficiently defined" as of the time the classification was developed[.\[31\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-NCCAprofile-31) The classification divides the Manobo into several major groups: (1) The Ata subgroup: Dugbatang, Talaingod, and Tagauanum; (2) The Bagobo subgroup: Attaw (Jangan, Klata, Obo, Giangan, Guiangan), Eto (Ata), Kailawan (Kaylawan), Langilan, Manuvu/Obo, Matigsalug, (Matigsaug, Matig Salug), Tagaluro, and Tigdapaya; (3) The Higaonon subgroup: Agusan, Lanao, and Misamis; (4) North Cotabato: Ilianen, Livunganen, and Pulenyan; (5) South Cotabato: Cotabato (with subgroup Tasaday and Blit), Sarangani, Tagabawa; (6) Western Bukidnon: Kiriyeteka, Ilentungen, and Pulangiyen; (7) Agusan del Sur; (8) Banwaon; and (9) Bukidnon and others.[\[31\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-NCCAprofile-31)

The total current Manobo population is not known, although they occupy core areas from [Sarangani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarangani,_Davao_Occidental) island into the Mindanao mainland in the regions of [Agusan,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agusan_province) Davao, Bukidnon, Surigao, Misamis, and [Cotabato.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotabato) A study by the [NCCP-PACT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Council_of_Churches_in_the_Philippines) put their population in 1988 at around 250,000.

The geographical distribution of the subgroups is so great that some of the local groups have been noted to "assumed the character of distinctiveness as a separate ethnic grouping," as in the case of the Bagobo or the Higaonon[.\[31\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-NCCAprofile-31) Part of what makes the classification more difficult is that a dialectical subgroup's membership within a supergroup can shift depending on specific points of view regarding linguistics.[\[32\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-iccaregistry-32)[\[33\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-Felix-33)

The Manobo possess [Denisovan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denisovan) admixture, much like the Mamanwa.[\[29\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-reichdenadse-29) Manobos also hold [Austroasiatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austroasiatic_languages) ancestry[.\[34\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-Larena-34)

### Mansaka

The term "Mansaka" derives from "man" with literal meaning "first" and "saka" meaning "to ascend", and means "the first people to ascend mountains/upstream". The term most likely describes the origin of these people who are found today in [Davao del Norte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davao_del_Norte) and [Davao de Oro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davao_de_Oro) and some parts of Davao Oriental, specifically in the Batoto River, the Manat Valley, Caragan, [Maragusan,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maragusan,_Compostela_Valley) the Hijo River Valley, and the seacoasts of Kingking, Maco, Kwambog, Hijo, Tagum, Libuganon, Tuganay, Ising, and Panabo.[\[35\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-35)

#### Matigsalug

Bukidnon groups are found in the Tigwa-Salug Valley in San Fernando in Bukidnon province, Philippines. Their name means "people along the Salug River (now called the [Davao River\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davao_River)." ### Subanon

The Subanons are the first settlers of the Zamboanga peninsula. The family is patriarchal while the village is led by a chief called a Timuay. He acts as the village judge and is concerned with all communal matters.

History has better words to speak for Misamis Occidental. Its principal city was originally populated by the Subanon, a cultural group that once roamed the seas in great number; the province was an easy prey to the marauding sea pirates of Lanao whose habit was to stage lightning forays along the coastal areas in search of slaves. As the Subanon retreated deeper and deeper into the interior, the coastal areas became home to inhabitants from Bukidnon who were steadily followed by settlers from nearby Cebu and Bohol.

### Tasaday

The Tasaday is a group of about two dozen people living within the deep and mountainous rainforests of Mindanao, who attracted wide media attention in 1971 when they were first "discovered" by western scientists who reported that they were living at a "stone age" level of technology and had been completely isolated from the rest of Philippine society. They later attracted attention in the 1980s when it was reported that their discovery had in fact been an elaborate [hoax,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoax) and doubt was raised both about their status as isolated from other societies and even about the reality of their existence as a separate ethnic group. The question of whether Tasaday studies published in the seventies are accurate is still being discussed.[\[44\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-44)[\[45\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-45)

#### Teduray

The [Teduray/Tiruray people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teduray_people) live in the municipalities of [Datu Blah T. Sinsuat,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Datu_Blah_T._Sinsuat,_Maguindanao) [Upi,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upi,_Maguindanao) and [South Upi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Upi,_Maguindanao) in southwestern [Maguindanao Province;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maguindanao_Province) and in [Lebak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebak,_Sultan_Kudarat) municipality, northwestern [Sultan Kudarat Province.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultan_Kudarat_Province) They speak the [Tiruray language,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiruray_language) which is related to [Bagobo,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giangan_language) [B'laan,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blaan_language) and [T'boli.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tboli_language) Coastal Tirurays are mostly farmers, hunters, fishermen, and basket weavers; those living in the mountains engage in dry field agriculture, supplemented by hunting and the gathering of forest products. Tirurays are famous for their craftsmanship in weaving baskets with two-toned geometric designs. While many have adopted the cultures of neighboring [Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslims) and [Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christians) people, a high percentage of their population still believe and practice their indigenous customs and rituals.[\[46\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-46)

### Tboli

The [Tboli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tboli_people) are one of the indigenous peoples of South Mindanao. From the body of ethnographic and linguistic literature on Mindanao, they are variously known as Tboli, Teboli, Tau Bilil, Tau Bulul or Tagabilil. They selfidentify as T'boli. Their whereabouts and identity are to some extent confused in the literature; some publications present the Teboli and the Tagabilil as distinct peoples; some locate the Tbolis to the vicinity of the Buluan Lake in the Cotabato Basin or in Agusan del Norte. The T'boli, then, reside on the mountain slopes on either side of the upper Allah Valley and the coastal area of Maitum, Maasim, and Kiamba. In former times, the T'boli also inhabited the upper Allah Valley floor.

# Musical heritage

Most of the Mindanao Lumad groups have a musical heritage consisting of various types of [agung ensembles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agung#In_agung_ensembles) – ensembles composed of large hanging, suspended or held, bossed/knobbed [gongs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gong) which act as a drone without any accompanying melodic instrument.[\[50\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-Philip-50)

Lumad groups also have traditional stringed instruments, such as the [hegalong,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kutiyapi) and various forms of wind and percussive instruments. Traditional vocal music include love songs, lullabies, funeral songs, narrative songs, and songs about nature. Music may be used to accompany dances in rituals and celebrations.[\[51\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-51)

# Social issues

Lumad peoples confront a variety of social issues. Identical with other Indigenous peoples (IPs) across the globe, the IPs in the Philippines represent the country's poorest sector who endure disproportionate access to healthcare, education, and human rights[.\[52\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-52) There are claims that Lumad social issues arise from ethnic grievances that develop into an issue of economic greed.[\[53\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-53) They face loss of ancestral lands due to [land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_grabbing) [grabbing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_grabbing) or militarization[,\[54\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-:1-54) economic and social exclusion[,\[55\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-55) and threats to their traditional culture and identity. Lumad groups contend with displacement, [extrajudicial killings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extrajudicial_killings_and_forced_disappearances_in_the_Philippines)[,\[56\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-:2-56) harassment of Lumad rights defenders[,\[57\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-:3-57) and forced closure of Lumad schools[.\[58\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-58)

### Ancestral land rights

Lumads face loss of ancestral lands due to land grabbing or militarization.[\[54\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-:1-54)[\[59\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-59) Some communities have been forced out of their lands for resisting encroachment by mining, logging, and energy companies.[\[60\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-60)

At the beginning of the 20th century, the Lumad controlled an area that now covers 17 of Mindanao's 24 provinces, but by the 1980 census, they constituted less than 6% of the population of Mindanao and [Sulu.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulu_Province) Significant migration to Mindanao of [Visayans,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visayan_people) spurred by government-sponsored resettlement programs,

## ![](_page_9_Picture_9.jpeg)

Norma Capuyan, vice chair of Apo Sandawa Lumadnong Panaghiusa sa Cotabato (ASLPC) speaking out in a press conference to defend the ancestral domains of the Lumad.

turned the Lumad into minorities. The Bukidnon province population grew from 63,470 in 1948 to 194,368 in 1960 and 414,762 in 1970, with the proportion of indigenous Bukidnons falling from 64% to 33% to 14%.

Lumad have a traditional concept of land ownership based on what their communities consider their ancestral territories. The historian B. R. Rodil notes that 'a territory occupied by a community is a communal private property, and community members have the right of usufruct to any piece of unoccupied land within the communal territory.' Ancestral lands include cultivated land as well as hunting grounds, rivers, forests, uncultivated land, and the mineral resources below the land. River systems indicate the Lumad people's generations of civilization. Water is used as a "hermeneutic" for how Lumads orient themselves in relation to other ethnic groups, the state, modern Filipino culture, and their own cultural customs.[\[61\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-61) Unlike the [Moros,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moro_people) the Lumad groups never formed a revolutionary group to unite them in [armed struggle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armed_struggle) against the Philippine government. When the migrants came, many Lumad groups retreated into the mountains and forests.

For the Lumad, securing their rights to their ancestral domain is as urgent as the Moros' quest for [self-determination.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-determination) However, much of their land has already been registered in the name of multinational corporations, logging companies, and other wealthy Filipinos, many of whom are, relatively speaking, recent settlers to Mindanao. Mai Tuan, a [T'boli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%27boli) leader explains, "Now that there is a peace agreement for the [MNLF,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moro_National_Liberation_Front) we are happy because we are given food assistance like rice... we also feel sad because we no longer have the pots to cook it with. We no longer have control over our ancestral lands."[\[62\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-62)

#### Lumad killings

Lumad communities contend with extrajudicial killing[s\[56\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-:2-56) and [red-tagging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red-tagging_in_the_Philippines)[.\[63\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-63) Defenders of Indigenous land rights, environmentalists, and human rights activists have also been harassed.[\[57\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-:3-57)[\[64\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-64)[\[65\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-65)

The Lumad are people from various ethnic groups in [Mindanao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mindanao) island. Residing in their ancestral lands[,\[66\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-66) they are often evicted and displaced because of the [Moro people'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moro_people)s claim on the same territory[.\[67\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-67) The Lumad have lost parts of their ancestral land because of a failure to understand the modern land tenure system[.\[68\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-fianza2015-68) Some NGOs have established schools that teach Indigenous communities how to protect their rights, property, and culture.[\[69\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-69) However, the Lumad communities are located in mountains that are distant from urban areas. These areas are also the sites of [armed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moro_conflict) [con](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moro_conflict)flict between the [New People's Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_People%27s_Army) (NPA) and the [Armed Forces of the Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armed_Forces_of_the_Philippines) (AFP). Caught in the conflict, the Lumad people's education, property, and security are endangered by the increasing amount of [violent confrontations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymmetric_warfare) by the armed parties.[\[68\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-fianza2015-68) In [Surigao del Sur,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surigao_del_Sur) a [barangay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barangay) was evacuated to shelter sites in [Tandag City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tandag_City) because of increasing military and NPA activity.[\[70\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-70) There are claims that Lumads account for about 70 percent of the fighting force of the [New People's Army,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_People%27s_Army) with the communists collecting [revolutionary tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutionary_tax) of P1.2 billion per year in [Davao Region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davao_Region) alone according to NCIP chief and former army colonel[.\[71\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-MB-Lumad-71)

Human rights watchdogs, Indigenous peoples groups, and environmentalists claim that Lumad territories were being militarized by the [Armed Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armed_Forces_of_the_Philippines) [of the Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armed_Forces_of_the_Philippines) and that community leaders and teachers were being detained by the military on suspicion of being rebels.[\[72\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-72) They also say that alternative schools within the communities (aided by NGOs and universities) face closure or demolition, with some buildings converted for military use[.\[73\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-73) They have staged demonstrations to gain the public's attention, calling for a halt to the alleged militarization of Lumad communities[.\[74\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-74) Organizers of the [Lakbayan ng Pambansang Minorya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakbayan_ng_Pambansang_Minorya) support the Lumad by raising awareness on the plight of Indigenous peoples through protest marches, concerts, cultural festivals, and commemoration of Lumad leaders that have been killed.[\[75\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-75)

The Philippines Commission on Human Rights (CHR) has been investigating the 2015 murder of Lumad leaders and a school official by [paramilitary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paramilitary) group Magahat-Bagani[\[76\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-76) (in line with the idea of [CAFGU\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizen_Armed_Force_Geographical_Unit) created by the AFP to hunt for NPA members. The AFP denied the allegation and attributed the killings to tribal conflict,[\[77\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-77) though the AFP has admitted that CAFGU has Lumad recruits within its ranks while asserting that the NPA has also recruited Lumad for the group.[\[78\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-78)[\[79\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-79) CHR postponed the presentation of their initial report to December 2015 to include reports of subsequent killings and displacement.[\[80\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-80)

Indigenous women leaders organized the Sabokahan Unity of Lumad Women.[\[81\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-81) Being located in the mountains, community evacuations have become the highest form of protest for the Lumad. Communities pack up and move en masse to urban areas to set up camp in evacuation centers. Through this, the Lumad people emphasize to the public, "We are here, and we are not going back until our land is free from troops and corporations, so that we can take back our ancestral land.["\[82\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-82) Relocating to urban centers allowed Lumad leaders to broadcast their plight to local and international audiences. Shortly after, Lumad people themselves realized they needed to have an umbrella organization through which advocates from around the world could work together, leading to the creation of the Liyang Network. The organization Liyang Network works alongside Lumad communities to amplify the voices of their environmental defenders and highlight Lumad social issues.[\[83\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-:03-83) Liyang Network organizes forums, webinars, and educational discussions on current sociopolitical issues and their root causes—mainly the needs of Lumad and rural communities[.\[83\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-:03-83)

Under the President Benigno Aquino III administration (2010-2015), a total of 71 Indigenous leaders were killed. Ninety-five cases of attacks against the 87 Indigenous schooling for children were also recorded. More than 40,000 Indigenous peoples—whole communities whose social, political, and economic life had been obstructed—had no choice but to evacuate because their schools were attacked or their leaders had been murdered or incarcerated[.\[84\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-84)

On 8 December 2017, human rights group Karapatan asked the [United Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations) to probe Lumad killings, including the killing of eight [T'boli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%27boli) and [Dulangan Manobo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manobo_people) farmers, allegedly by members of the Philippine Army.[\[85\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-85)[\[86\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-86) A pro-AFP and pro-mining dat[u\[87\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-87) of the Langilan Manobo people in [Davao del Norte,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davao_del_Norte) during an AFP-sponsored press conference, claimed that the NPA were responsible for the killings and that none of the alleged "militarization" was actually happening[.\[88\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-Romero2019-88)[\[89\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-89) Another datu accused protesters in Manila of pretending to be Lumad by wearing Lumad clothing[.\[90\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-Caliwan2018-90) They have also held anti-NPA rallies in Mindanao.[\[91\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-91) The military has attributed to the NPA the assassination of a Lumad leader sympathetic to the government.[\[92\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-92) Some of which are acknowledged by NPA members[.\[93\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-93)[\[94\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-94)

In 2018, President Rodrigo Duterte threatened to shut down or destroy NGO-funded community schools because of suspicions that they radicalize Lumad students into joining the [NPA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_People%27s_Army) communist rebels.[\[95\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-95)[\[96\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-Duterte_and_Lumad_schools-96) This was supported by some Lumad leaders, who also felt that they were being infiltrated by the NPA and their children being exploited.[\[97\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-Colina2018-97)[\[98\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-Gagalac2018-98)[\[99\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-99)

In August 2019, after spending time in refugee camps, Lumad evacuees in Surigao del Sur formally returned to their home after army soldiers left their communities.[\[100\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-100)

In December 2019, the Philippines under Duterte became the deadliest for farmers and Indigenous peoples.[\[101\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-101)

# Lumad schools

Many Lumad youths live in indigent, hard-to-reach communities[.\[102\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-:02-102) In the 1980s, the Tribal Filipino Program of Surigao del Sur (TRIFPSS) began a functional literacy program for Indigenous children in these communities[.\[103\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-:12-103) TRIFPSS established 10 schools in 10 Indigenous people's communities in three municipalities of Surigao del Sur in 1997[.\[103\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-:12-103) The Alternative Learning Center for Agriculture Livelihood Development (Alcadev), formed in 2004[\[102\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-:02-102) with support from TRIFPSS[,\[103\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-:12-103) established community schools for Lumad communities using lessons tailored to suit Indigenous culture and tradition[.\[104\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-:22-104)

The [Department of Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Education_(Philippines)) (Dep Ed) recognized community schools and the Alcadev system through the Indigenous framework of education now observed by alternative tribal schools nationwide.[\[104\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-:22-104)[\[105\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-105)[\[106\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-106) The policy framework was signed in 2012 by then-Secretary [Armin Luistro.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armin_Luistro)[\[107\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-107)

In a Bakwit school in Cebu, 22 students, 2 teachers and 2 tribe elders were arrested without a warrant on 15 February 2021, in what was labeled as a "rescue operation". Philippine media dubbed this raid as "Lumad 26", as they were taken into custody by members of the Philippine National Police's (PNP) Central Visayas office (PRO-7) and charged with illegal detention and kidnapping.[\[108\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-108) Local officials claimed the operation aimed to reunite the children with their parent[s\[109\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-109) while human rights groups condemned the raid as part of a pattern of harassment of Indigenous peoples[.\[110\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-110)[\[111\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-111)

#### Climate change

The Philippines is vulnerable to the effects of [climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change) and was ranked third globally among countries most at-risk to disasters, according to a 2012 report.[\[112\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-112) Climate change is threatening [food security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effects_of_climate_change_on_agriculture) among Lumads whose farmlands are affected by stronger typhoons and more intense droughts[.\[113\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-:4-113) Climate change is also giving rise to various [health issues,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effects_of_climate_change_on_human_health) with Lumad communities reporting health problems brought about by rising temperatures and diseases that spread with changing rainfall patterns, such as dengue[.\[113\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-:4-113)

In 2019, Lumad youth and urban poor children joined the [global climate strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School_strike_for_climate) to demand protection for environmental activists, protest destructive mining operations within ancestral lands, and promote [climate justice.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_justice)[\[114\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-114)[\[115\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumad#cite_note-115)
