While many analysts have commented on the representation of 1968 campus events and antiwar demonstrations, less attention has been paid to the global signiicance of the dramatic struggles in industrial Detroit during the period. he meanings of events in the city were intensely fought over. According to longtime community organizers in the League who had been involved with the Congress of Racial Equality (CORE) and the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC), the Detroit Rebellion of July 1967—the largest urban uprising in US history before the Los Angeles revolt of 1992—was essentially a working-class rebellion. he revolt was met with deadly force: state oicials gave the order to "shoot to kill" aggrieved and insurgent poor people who were labeled as "looters" and "arsonists" by National Guard troops, federal soldiers, and police oicers. he violence let over forty people dead.<sup>4</sup> Yet, as Martin Glaberman persuasively argued, the Detroit rebellion exposed the vulnerability of auto corporations to the pressure of working-class struggle at the point of production. he RUMs and the League presented a critique of the racial regime that has buttressed US capitalism. hese organic intellectuals articulated a counterhegemonic ideology that meshed Detroit's Black working-class expressive culture with socialist visions of class struggle. he years between 1967 and 1973 saw the highest number of strikes in the postwar period since 1946. During the 1950s and 1960s, many community organizers and civil rights activists had begun to theorize contemporary events as a Second Reconstruction. ### Urban Insurgency in Detroit he Detroit Rebellion began when city residents witnessed police harassment at a homecoming party for two Black soldiers returning from the US war in Vietnam. People had been hanging out at an ater-hours bar run by the United Community and Civil League (UCCL). On July 22, 1967, the Detroit Police Department (DPD) raided a series of so-called "blind pigs" (ater-hours clubs). he ith and inal raid targeted the club on Twelth Street that hosted the homecoming party.<sup>11</sup> While harassment of ater-hours drinking establishments was a persistent practice in Detroit, the police usually just forced people to disperse and sometimes arrested a few. Only a few blocks away from the raided club, twenty-seven-year-old army veteran Danny homas had been murdered recently by a gang of white youth. Community members were still reeling from that killing. It did not take long for the aggrieved people of Detroit's slums to resist the situation "on their own terms."<sup>12</sup>

While many photos of the revolt showed "systematic and integrated looting" among Black and poor white working-class participants "shopping for free," dominant signiications of the event such as reports in the New York Times described a riot caused by "Negroes in Detroit" that created a rampage of burning, looting, and chaos. A curfew was issued to suppress the race and class insurrection. Soon ater the uprising, US attorney general Ramsey Clark was contacted by Michigan governor George Romney to request federal troops to put down the rebellion.<sup>13</sup> On July 27, president Lyndon Johnson addressed the nation as part of an efort to ensure that "law and order" would be restored. Johnson's words ofered no restraint: "First—let there be no mistake about it—the looting, arson, plunder and pillage which have occurred are not part of a civil-rights protest. . . . hat is crime—and crime must be dealt with forcefully, switly, certainly." he president cited the Insurrection Act of 1807 as the legal basis for repression of the rebellion he labeled as the activity of a criminal mob.<sup>14</sup>

he rhetoric of criminality should compel us to reconsider how state discourses dramatically clashed with the discourses about the rebellion voiced from within aggrieved communities. To do so we need a sustained analysis of the "prose of counter-insurgency," and that is narratives of insurgency that serve the interests of capital and the state.<sup>15</sup> President Johnson's attempt to distinguish between legitimate Civil Rights protest and crime while calling on the Insurrection Act suggests that defenders of law and order criminalized dissent in an efort to contain the challenges to the legitimacy of the state. he importance of the language of law and order for the prose of counterinsurgency would be diicult to overstate. By making state security the central issue, this rhetoric represented state repression as a natural response to disorder on one hand, and denied insurgents recognition as subjects of their own history on the other. he rhetoric of counterinsurgency purported that for capitalist development to occur security forces must quell ideological and political opposition. he state's deployment of force to maintain its monopoly on legitimate violence was central to uneven capitalist development.16 Racial narratives were ofered as justiications for the relations of class power. In response to the Detroit insurgency, the shit in the terms of racialization towards law and order relected a political and economic situation where mass social protest had been equated with "mob rule."<sup>17</sup>

Journalistic narratives oten reproduced the state's counterinsurgent deinitions of the situation. Consider for example Newsweek's take on the events in the earliest hours of July 23: "he trouble burst on Detroit like a irestorm and turned the nation's ith largest city into a theater of war. Whole streets lay ravaged by looters, whole blocks immolated by lames. "If there is one point that has been proved repeatedly over four summers of ghetto riots," Time magazine suggests, "it is that when the police abandon the street, the crowd takes it over, and the crowd can switly become a mob. Detroit's dance halls and nightclubs were targeted in journalistic representations of Black working-class cultural spaces as particular sites of criminality. he Time and Newsweek narratives deined the events not as motivated by the structural racism and class exploitation endemic to racial capitalism, but as a problem of the behavior of Black Power activists and poor inner-city residents. he logic of such representations was that Black proletarian bodies needed discipline by the state that was not otherwise being provided by the sheer "force of circumstance." his discourse implied that the police raid of the club was as much about the "immorality" of the Black worker as it was about serving alcohol ater hours.<sup>21</sup>

he social base of the insurrection was young autoworkers and the wageless. hus, I follow the practice advocated by labor historian E. he uprising quickened the step of the working class at the point of production, sparking labor–management clashes on assembly lines and in workshops throughout the city. he ensuing self-activity and collective struggles of Black workers produced many victories. He argued that the Commune represented a revolutionary event because it abolished night work for bakers and, as Frederick Engels has noted, "ordered the closing of the pawnshops, on the ground that they were a private exploitation of labour, and were in contradiction with the right of the workers to their instruments of labour and to credit." While the uprising in Detroit was one of hundreds of urban rebellions across the nation, it occurred in the context of a deeply politicized and concentrated Black working-class community in the city's ghetto and factories. Socialist, feminist, and antiracist activists at the time were theorizing about cycles of urban revolt in North America as a point of reference for class struggles on both sides of the Atlantic.<sup>27</sup> According to Selma James, he Black movement . . . in the United States challenged and continues to challenge the most powerful capitalist State in the world. he most powerful at home and abroad. Her insights are crucial to understanding the content of this insurgent challenge to what she calls "constituted authority." In the wake of the urban uprisings in Detroit and other cities, Dr. Martin Luther King followed the lead of the Black working class in challenging racism, poverty, and militarism. In his speeches following the uprisings, he linked the racist and imperialist US war in Vietnam to the political and economic repression of Black and poor people in domestic spaces. As masses in the streets pressed political demands on the state for an end to police brutality, access to the social wage (housing, education, welfare), and human rights, King sought to articulate the struggle for political power and the redistribution of the social wealth.<sup>29</sup> He explained: "he dispossessed of this nation—the poor, both white and Negro—live in a cruelly unjust society. hey must organize a revolution against that injustice, not against the lives of the persons who are their fellow citizens, but against the structures through which the society is refusing to . . . lit the load of poverty."30 King visited Detroit in February of 1968 to gain support for the Poor People's Campaign, a mass mobilization designed to confront the issues of racism and poverty. Soon ater he united with the forces of the Black labor movement, he was assassinated on April 4, 1968, in Memphis, one year to the day ater he gave his famous speech at Riverside Church in New York City calling for intensiied resistance to the imperialist war in Vietnam. "As ires raged and guns sounded and screams illed the night in scores of black neighborhoods," Harding observed, "King's death seemed to be sparking the confrontation that would open the next levels of urban insurrection and revolution."<sup>32</sup> Perhaps like no year since 1848, 1968 was as a year of global revolution and counterrevolution. he tumultuous events of 1968 include the Tet ofensive in Vietnam, Martin Luther King's assassination in Memphis, Tennessee, the accumulating wave of urban revolts ater King's murder, the student and worker mobilizations in Paris in May, student occupation of buildings at US universities and colleges (including the takeover of North Hall on the University of California, Santa Barbara's campus by Black students as described in this issue), the mobilizations in Mexico City among students and workers, and the uprisings in Kingston, Jamaica, ater Walter Rodney was prevented from reentering the country. hese struggles were not unrelated. he spontaneous rebellions of the poor asserted the emergence of an aggrieved and insurgent people with speciic class aims and interests that challenged racism and class rule.<sup>33</sup>

In May of 1968, less than a month ater King's tragic death, over four thousand autoworkers initiated wildcat strikes at the Dodge Main plant in Detroit.

he repression of the Detroit rebellion shaped urban counterinsurgency policy for decades. he United States expanded its counterintelligence program (COINTELPRO) against Black radical organizations represented as "Black Hate" groups on August 25, 1967. his program was designed to control and prevent the actual or potential expansion of antiracist and class militancy in the wake of urban uprisings in ghettoes. hese dramatic events were central to the process by which class anxieties came to be legible or represented in the racialized terms of security or law and order. Political ideologues promoted the idea that the state should be restricted to the enforcement of order. hus disorder was attributed to the insurgent demands for dignity, autonomy, and the redistribution of the social wealth by Black freedom and radical labor struggles.<sup>53</sup>Against these demands, as Ruth Wilson Gilmore demonstrates, a racialized domestic-security discourse responded by legitimating the strategy of militarized policing and mass imprisonment as geographical solutions to crisis. Law-and-order narratives presented the repression of surplus labor as a natural response to "disorder" and denied insurgent workers recognition as subjects of their own history.<sup>55</sup>

Yet they survive as important sources of evidence about the roots of the contemporary conjuncture between race- and class-based struggles. hey underscore the potential to facilitate alliances among students, organic intellectuals, and workers and to articulate counterhegemonic practices through cultural productions. hey hold in abeyance discourses and practices needed to challenge the hegemonic prose of counterinsurgency that has for decades purported to legitimate the practices of the neoliberal security apparatus.

Counterinsurgent discourses function to hide social memories of alternatives to this regime by representing society as consensus and obfuscating the drama of social conlict.<sup>58</sup> he knowledge of social contradiction expressed in the cultural productions of labor and social movements serves as an important counterdiscourse to representations of insurgencies that have come to inhabit neoliberal racial and security regimes—the modalities of political, economic, and cultural relationships between racism, uneven capitalist development, and state power. Drawing on a counterinsurgent security doctrine to justify its deployment of technologies of social control, this regime features the criminalization of dissent, elimination of the social wage, and dramatic expansion of militarized policing and mass imprisonment. Seizing hold of social memories articulated in the poetry of labor and social movements from Detroit to Turin and beyond, the League attempted to subvert the prose of counterinsurgency. heir cultural productions suggest that collective memory can function as a powerful force shaping contemporary social relations. As C. L. R. James argued, scholars "should make it their business" that the revolts of that period "are properly recorded, analyzed, and made available to the general public at home and abroad." hese social memories can be tapped today in the context of the current carceral crisis, and used to challenge the terms of order. An understanding of radical traditions forged in the past can ire the political imagination, sparking possibilities of alternative futures.<sup>59</sup>
