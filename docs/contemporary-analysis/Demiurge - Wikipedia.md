# **Demiurge**

In the [Platonic,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonism) [Neopythagorean,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neopythagorean) [Middle Platonic,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Platonism) and [Neoplatonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonism) schools of [philosophy,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy) the **Demiurge** [\(/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English)[ˈ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English)[d](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English)[ɛ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English)[mi.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English)[ɜː](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English)rd[ʒ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English)[/\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English) is an [artisan-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artisan)like figure responsible for fashioning and maintaining the physical [universe.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe) Various sects of [Gnostics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnosticism) adopted the term *demiurge*.

Although a fashioner, the demiurge is not necessarily the same as the [creator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creator_deity) figure in the [monotheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism) sense, because the demiurge itself and the material from which the demiurge fashions the universe are both considered consequences of something else. Depending on the system, they may be considered either uncreated and eternal or the product of some other entity. Some of these systems are monotheistic, while others are [henotheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henotheism) or [polytheistic.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytheism)

The word *demiurge* is an English word derived from *demiurgus*, a [Latinised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Latinised_names) form of the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) δημιουργός (*dēmiurgós*) . It was originally a common noun meaning "craftsman" or "artisan", but gradually came to mean "producer", and eventually "creator." The philosophical usage and the proper noun derive from [Plato's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timaeus_(dialogue)) *[Timaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timaeus_(dialogue))*, written c. 360 BC, where the demiurge is presented as the creator of the universe. The demiurge is also described as a creator in the Platonic (c. 310–90 BC) and Middle Platonic (c. 90 BC–AD 300) philosophical traditions. In the various branches of the Neoplatonic school (third century onwards), the demiurge is the fashioner of the real, perceptible world after the model of the [Ideas,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_Forms) but (in most Neoplatonic systems) is still not itself ["the One"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Form_of_the_Good).

Within the vast spectrum of Gnostic traditions, views of the Demiurge range dramatically. It is generally understood and agreed upon to be a lesser divinity who governs the material universe. However, the nature of its rule over the material realm differs from sect to sect. [Sethian Gnosticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sethianism) portrays the Demiurge as an oppressive, ignorant ruler, intentionally binding souls in an inherently corrupt material realm. In contrast, [Valentinian Gnosticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valentinianism) sees the Demiurge as a well-meaning but limited figure whose rule reflects ignorance rather than malice.

## **Plato and the** *Timaeus*

[Plato,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato) as the speaker Timaeus, refers to the Demiurge frequently in the [Socratic dialogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socratic_dialogue) *[Timaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timaeus_(dialogue))* (28a [ff](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/ff.#Abbreviation).), c. 360 BC. The main character refers to the Demiurge as the entity who "fashioned and shaped" the material world. Timaeus describes the Demiurge as unreservedly [benevolent,](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/benevolence) and so it desires a world as good as possible. The result of his work is a universe as a living god with lesser gods, such as the stars, planets, and gods of traditional religion, inside it. Plato argues that the cosmos needed a Demiurge because the cosmos needed a cause that makes [Becoming resemble Being.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analogy_of_the_divided_line)[\[1\]](#page-8-0) *Timaeus* is a philosophical reconciliation of [Hesiod'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hesiod)s cosmology in his *[Theogony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theogony)*, [syncretically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syncretism) reconciling Hesiod to [Homer,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer)[\[2\]](#page-8-1)[\[3\]](#page-8-2)[\[4\]](#page-8-3) though other scholars have argued that Plato's theology 'invokes a broad cultural horizon without committing to any specific poetic or religious tradition'.[\[5\]](#page-8-4) Moreover, Plato believed that the Demiurge created other, so-called "lower" gods who, in turn, created humanity[.\[6\]](#page-8-5) Some scholars have argued that the lower gods are gods of traditional mythology, such as Zeus and Hera.[\[7\]](#page-8-6)

# **Gnosticism**

[Gnosticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnosticism) presents a distinction between the highest, unknowable God or [Supreme Being](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeon_(Gnosticism)) and the demiurgic "creator" of the material, identified in some traditions with [Yahweh,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yahweh) the God of the [Hebrew Bible.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible) Several systems of Gnostic thought present the Demiurge as antagonistic to the will of the [Supreme Being,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God) with his creation initially having the malevolent intention of entrapping aspects of the divine *in* materiality. In other systems, the Demiurge is instead portrayed as "merely" incompetent or foolish: his creation is an unconscious attempt to replicate the divine world (the [pleroma\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleroma) based on faint recollections, and thus ends up fundamentally flawed. Thus, in such systems, the Demiurge is a proposed solution to the [problem of evil:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Problem_of_evil) while the divine beings are omniscient and omnibenevolent, the Demiurge who rules over our own physical world is not[.\[8\]](#page-8-7)

### **Angels**

[Psalm 82](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psalm_82) begins, "God stands in the assembly of [El](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_(deity)) [\[LXX:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Septuagint) assembly of gods], in the midst of the gods he renders judgment",[\[9\]](#page-8-8) indicating a plurality of gods, although it does not indicate that these gods were coactors in creation. [Philo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philo) had inferred from the expression "Let us make man" of the [Book of Genesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Genesis) that God had used other beings as assistants in the creation of man, and he explains in this way why man is capable of vice as well as virtue, ascribing the origin of the latter to God, of the former to his helpers in the work of creation.[\[10\]](#page-8-9)

The earliest Gnostic sects ascribe the work of creation to angels, some of them using the same passage in Genesis.[\[11\]](#page-8-10) So [Irenaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irenaeus) tell[s\[12\]](#page-8-11) of the system of [Simon Magus,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Magus)[\[13\]](#page-8-12) of the system of [Menander,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menander_(gnostic))[\[14\]](#page-8-13) of the system of [Saturninus,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturninus_of_Antioch) in which the number of these angels is reckoned as seven, an[d\[15\]](#page-8-14) of the system of [Carpocrates.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpocrates) In [Basilides'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilides)s system, he reports[,\[16\]](#page-9-0) the world was made by the angels who occupy the lowest heaven; but special mention is made of their chief, who is said to have been the [God of the Jews,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_in_Judaism) to have led that people out of the land of [Egypt,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt) and to have given them their law. The prophecies are ascribed not to the chief but to the other world-making angels.

The Latin translation, confirmed by [Hippolytus of Rome,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippolytus_of_Rome)[\[17\]](#page-9-1) makes [Irenaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irenaeus) state that according to [Cerinthus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerinthus) (who shows [Ebionite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebionites) influence), creation was made by a power quite separate from the Supreme God and ignorant of him. [Theodoret,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodoret)[\[18\]](#page-9-2) who here copies Irenaeus, turns this into the plural number "powers", and so [Epiphanius of Salamis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epiphanius_of_Salamis)[\[19\]](#page-9-3) represents Cerinthus as agreeing with Carpocrates in the doctrine that the world was made by angels.

### **Yaldabaoth**

In the [Archontic,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archontics) [Sethian,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sethianism) and [Ophite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ophites) systems, which have many affinities with the [doctrine of Valentinus,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valentinianism) the making of the world is ascribed to a company of seven [archons,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archon_(Gnosticism)) whose names are given, but still more prominent is their chief, "Yaldabaoth" (also known as "Yaltabaoth" or "Ialdabaoth").

In the *[Apocryphon of John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apocryphon_of_John)* c. AD 120–180, the demiurge declares that he has made the world by himself:

Now the [archon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archon) ["ruler"] who is weak has three names. The first name is Yaltabaoth, the second is Saklas ["fool"], and the third is [Samael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samael) ["blind god"]. And he is impious in his arrogance which is in him. For he said, 'I am God and there is no other God beside me,' for he is ignorant of his strength, the place from which he had come[.\[20\]](#page-9-4)

He is demiurge and maker of man, but as a ray of light from above enters the body of man and gives him a soul, Yaldabaoth is filled with envy; he tries to limit man's knowledge by forbidding him the fruit of knowledge in paradise. At the consummation of all things, all light will return to the [Pleroma.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleroma) But Yaldabaoth, the demiurge, with the material world, will be cast into the lower depths.[\[21\]](#page-9-5)

Yaldabaoth is frequently called "the Lion-faced", *leontoeides*, and is said to have the body of a serpent. The demiurge is als[o\[22\]](#page-9-6) described as having a fiery nature, applying the words of Moses to him: "the Lord our God is a burning and consuming fire". Hippolytus claims that Simon used a similar description.[\[23\]](#page-9-7)

#### **Names**

The etymology of the name *Yaldabaoth* has been subject to many speculative theories. Until 1974, etymologies deriving from the unattested [Aramaic:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramaic) אתוהב , romanized: *bāhūthā*, supposedly meaning ["chaos"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaos_(cosmogony)), represented the majority view. Following an analysis by the Jewish historian of religion [Gershom Scholem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gershom_Scholem) published in 1974,[\[25\]](#page-9-9) this etymology no longer enjoyed any notable support. His analysis showed the unattested Aramaic term to have been fabulated and attested only in a single corrupted text from 1859, with its claimed translation having been transposed from the reading of an earlier etymology, whose explanation seemingly equated ["darkness"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darkness) and "chaos" when translating an unattested supposed plural form of [Hebrew:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language) והוב , [romanized:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Hebrew) *bōhu*. [\[25\]](#page-9-9)[\[26\]](#page-9-10)

["Samael"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samael) literally means "Blind God" or "God of the Blind" in Hebrew ( לאמס( . This being is considered not only blind, or ignorant of its own origins, but may, in addition, be evil; its name is also found in [Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaism) as the [Angel of Death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personifications_of_death#Death_(angels)_in_religion) and in [Christian demonology.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_demonology) This link to Judeo-Christian tradition leads to a further comparison with [Satan.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satan) Another alternative title for the demiurge is "Saklas", Aramaic for "fool". In the *[Apocryphon of John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apocryphon_of_John)*, Yaldabaoth is also known as both Sakla and Samael[.\[27\]](#page-9-11)

The angelic name ["Ariel"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariel_(angel)) (Hebrew: 'the lion of God')[\[28\]](#page-9-12) has also been used to refer to the Demiurge and is called his "perfect" name[;\[29\]](#page-9-13) in some Gnostic lore, Ariel has been called an ancient or original name for Ialdabaoth[.\[30\]](#page-9-14) The name has also been inscribed on amulets as "Ariel Ialdabaoth"[,\[31\]](#page-9-15)[\[32\]](#page-9-16) and the figure of the archon inscribed with "Aariel".[\[33\]](#page-9-17)

#### **Marcion**

According to [Marcion,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcion_of_Sinope) the title God was given to the Demiurge, who was to be sharply distinguished from the higher Good God. The former was *díkaios*, severely just, the latter *agathós*, or loving-kind; the former was the "god of this world",[\[34\]](#page-9-18) the God of the [Old Testament,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Testament) the latter the true God of the [New Testament.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Testament) Christ, in reality, is the Son of the Good God. The true believer in Christ entered into God's kingdom; the unbeliever remained forever the slave of the Demiurge[.\[21\]](#page-9-5)

#### **Valentinus**

It is in the [system of Valentinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valentinianism) that the name *Dēmiurgos* is used, which occurs nowhere in Irenaeus except in connection with the Valentinian system. When it is employed by other Gnostics either it is not used in a technical sense, or its use has been borrowed from Valentinus. But it is only the name that can be said to be specially Valentinian; the personage intended by it corresponds more or less closely with the Yaldabaoth of the Ophites, the [great Archon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraxas) of Basilides, the Elohim of [Justinus,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justin_(gnostic)) etc.

The Valentinian theory elaborates that from Achamoth (*he kátō sophía* or lower wisdom) three kinds of substance take their origin, the spiritual (*pneumatikoí*), the animal (*psychikoí*) and the material (*hylikoí*). The Demiurge belongs to the second kind, as he was the offspring of a union of Achamoth with matter.[\[21\]](#page-9-5)[\[35\]](#page-9-19) And as Achamoth herself was only the daughter of *[Sophía](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophia_(Gnosticism))* the last of the thirty Aeons, the Demiurge was distant by many emanations from the Propatôr, or Supreme God[.\[21\]](#page-9-5)

In creating this world out of Chaos the Demiurge was unconsciously influenced for good; and the universe, to the surprise even of its Maker, became almost perfect. The Demiurge regretted even its slight imperfection, and as he thought himself the Supreme God, he attempted to remedy this by sending a Messiah. To this Messiah, however, was actually united with Jesus the Saviour, Who redeemed men. These are either *hylikoí* or *pneumatikoí*. [\[21\]](#page-9-5)

The first, or material men, will return to the grossness of matter and finally be consumed by fire; the second, or animal men, together with the Demiurge, will enter a middle state, neither Pleroma nor *hyle*; the purely spiritual men will be completely freed from the influence of the Demiurge and together with the Saviour and Achamoth, his spouse, will enter the Pleroma divested of body (*hyle*) and soul (*psyché*)[.\[21\]](#page-9-5)[\[36\]](#page-9-20) In this most common form of Gnosticism the Demiurge had an inferior though not intrinsically evil function in the universe as the head of the animal, or psychic world.[\[21\]](#page-9-5)

#### **The devil**

Opinions on [the devil,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_devil) and his relationship to the Demiurge, vary. The Ophites held that he and his demons constantly oppose and thwart the human race, as it was on their account the devil was cast down into this world[.\[37\]](#page-9-21) According to one variant of the Valentinian system, the Demiurge is also the maker, out of the appropriate substance, of an order of *spiritual* beings, the devil, the prince of this world, and his angels. But the devil, as being a *spirit* of wickedness, is able to recognise the higher spiritual world, of which his maker the Demiurge, who is only animal, has no real knowledge. The devil resides in this lower world, of which he is the prince, the Demiurge in the heavens; his mother Sophia in the middle region, above the heavens and below the Pleroma.[\[38\]](#page-10-0)

The Valentinian [Heracleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heracleon)[\[39\]](#page-10-1) interpreted the devil as the *principle* of evil, that of *hyle* (matter). As he writes in his commentary on John 4:21,

The mountain represents the Devil, or his world, since the Devil was one part of the whole of matter, but the world is the total mountain of evil, a deserted dwelling place of beasts, to which all who lived before the law and all Gentiles render worship. But Jerusalem represents the creation or the Creator whom the Jews worship. ... You then who are spiritual should worship neither the creation nor the Craftsman, but the Father of Truth.

This vilification of the creator was held to be inimical to Christianity by the early fathers of the church. In refuting the beliefs of the gnostics, [Irenaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irenaeus) stated that "Plato is proved to be more religious than these men, for he allowed that the same God was both just and good, having power over all things, and himself executing judgment.["\[40\]](#page-10-2)

# **Platonism**

#### **Middle Platonism**

In [Numenius'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numenius_of_Apamea)s [Neo-Pythagorean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Pythagorean) and [Middle Platonist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Platonism) [cosmogony,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmogony) the Demiurge is second God as the *[nous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nous)* or thought of intelligibles and sensible[s\[41\]](#page-10-3)

### **Plotinus**

The work of [Plotinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plotinus) and other later Platonists in the 3rd century AD to further clarify the Demiurge is known as [Neoplatonism.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonism) To Plotinus, the second [emanation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emanationism) represents an uncreated second cause (see [Pythagoras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagoras)' [Dyad\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyad_(symbol)). Plotinus sought to reconcile Aristotle's *[energeia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energeia)* with Plato's Demiurge[,\[42\]](#page-10-4) which, as Demiurge and mind (*nous*), is a critical component in the [ontological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontological) construct of human consciousness used to explain and clarify [substance theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substance_theory) within [Platonic realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_realism) (also called [idealism\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idealism). In order to reconcile Aristotelian with Platonian philosophy[,\[42\]](#page-10-4) Plotinus metaphorically identified the demiurge (or *nous*) within the [pantheon of the Greek Gods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantheon_(gods)) as [Zeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeus)[.\[43\]](#page-10-5)

#### **Henology**

The first and highest aspect of God is described by Plato as the One (Τὸ Ἕν, 'To Hen'), the source, or the [Monad.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monad_(philosophy))[\[44\]](#page-10-6) This is the God above the Demiurge, and manifests through the actions of the Demiurge. The

Monad [emanated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emanationism) the demiurge or *[Nous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nous)* (consciousness) from its "indeterminate" vitality due to the monad being so abundant that it overflowed back onto itself, causing self-reflection.[\[45\]](#page-10-7) This self-reflection of the indeterminate vitality was referred to by Plotinus as the "Demiurge" or creator. The second principle is organization in its reflection of the [nonsentient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentience) force or *[dynamis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potentiality_and_actuality)*, also called the one or the Monad. The dyad is *energeia* emanated by the one that is then the work, process or activity called *nous*, Demiurge, mind, consciousness that organizes the indeterminate vitality into the experience called the material world, universe, cosmos. [Plotinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plotinus) also elucidates the equation of matter with nothing or non-being in *[The](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Enneads) [Enneads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Enneads)*[\[46\]](#page-10-8) which more correctly is to express the concept of [idealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idealism) or that there is not anything or anywhere outside of the "mind" or *nous* (cf. [pantheism\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantheism).

Plotinus' form of [Platonic idealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_idealism) is to treat the Demiurge, *nous*, as the contemplative faculty (*ergon*) within man which orders the force (*dynamis*) into conscious reality.[\[47\]](#page-10-9) In this, he claimed to reveal Plato's true meaning: a doctrine he learned from Platonic tradition that did not appear outside the academy or in Plato's text. This tradition of creator God as *nous* (the manifestation of consciousness), can be validated in the works of pre-Plotinus philosophers such as [Numenius,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numenius_of_Apamea) as well as a connection between Hebrew and Platonic cosmology (see also [Philo\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philo).[\[48\]](#page-10-10)

The Demiurge of Neoplatonism is the *Nous* (mind of God), and is one of the three ordering principles:

- [Arche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arche) (Gr. 'beginning') the source of all things,
- [Logos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos) (Gr. 'reason/cause') the underlying order that is hidden beneath appearances,
- [Harmonia](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/harmonia) (Gr. 'harmony') numerical [ratios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ratios) in [mathematics.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics)

Before Numenius of Apamea and Plotinus' *[Enneads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enneads)*, no Platonic works ontologically clarified the Demiurge from the [allegory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allegory) in Plato's *Timaeus*. The idea of Demiurge was, however, addressed before Plotinus in the works of Christian writer [Justin Martyr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justin_Martyr) who built his understanding of the Demiurge on the works of Numenius.[\[49\]](#page-10-11)

#### **Plotinus' Against the Gnostics**

Gnosticism attributed falsehood or evil to the concept of the Demiurge or creator, though in some Gnostic traditions the creator is from a fallen, ignorant, or lesser—rather than evil—perspective, such as that of [Valentinius.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valentinius) The Neoplatonic philosopher [Plotinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plotinus) addressed within his works Gnosticism's conception of the Demiurge, which he saw as un[-Hellenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece) and blasphemous to the Demiurge or creator of Plato. Plotinus, along with his teacher [Ammonius Saccas,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonius_Saccas) was the founder of [Neoplatonism.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonism)[\[50\]](#page-10-12) In the ninth [tractate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treatise) of the second of his *Enneads*, Plotinus criticizes his opponents for their appropriation of ideas from Plato:

From Plato come their punishments, their rivers of the underworld and the changing from body to body; as for the plurality they assert in the Intellectual Realm—the Authentic Existent, the Intellectual-Principle, *the Second Creator and the Soul*—all this is taken over from the Timaeus.

—Ennead 2.9.vi; emphasis added from [A. H. Armstrong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._H._Armstrong)'s introduction to Ennead 2.9

Of note here is the remark concerning the second [hypostasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypostasis_(philosophy_and_religion)) or Creator and third hypostasis or [World Soul.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anima_mundi)

Plotinus criticizes his opponents for "all the novelties through which they seek to establish a philosophy of their own" which, he declares, "have been picked up outside of the truth";[\[51\]](#page-11-0) they attempt to conceal rather than admit their indebtedness to ancient philosophy, which they have corrupted by their extraneous and misguided embellishments. Thus their understanding of the Demiurge is similarly flawed in comparison to Plato's original intentions.

Whereas Plato's Demiurge is good wishing good on his creation, Gnosticism contends that the Demiurge is not only the originator of evil but is evil as well. Hence the title of Plotinus' refutation: "Against Those That Affirm the Creator of the Kosmos and the Kosmos Itself to be Evil" (generally quoted as "Against the Gnostics"). Plotinus argues of the disconnect or great barrier that is created between the *nous* or mind's [noumenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noumenon) (see [Heraclitus\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraclitus) and the material world [\(phenomenon\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenon) by believing the material world is evil.

The majority of scholars tend[\[52\]](#page-11-1) to understand Plotinus' opponents as being a Gnostic sect—certainly (specifically [Sethian\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sethianism), several such groups were present in [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria) and elsewhere about the [Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean) during Plotinus' lifetime. Plotinus specifically points to the Gnostic doctrine of Sophia and her emission of the Demiurge.

Though the former understanding certainly enjoys the greatest popularity, the identification of Plotinus' opponents as Gnostic is not without some contention. Christos Evangeliou has contende[d\[53\]](#page-11-2) that Plotinus' opponents might be better described as simply "Christian Gnostics", arguing that several of Plotinus' criticisms are as applicable to orthodox Christian doctrine as well. Also, considering the evidence from the time, Evangeliou thought the definition of the term "Gnostics" was unclear. Of note here is that while Plotinus' student [Porphyry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porphyry_(philosopher)) names [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity) specifically in Porphyry's own works, and Plotinus is to have been a known associate of the Christian [Origen,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origen) none of Plotinus' works mention Christ or Christianity whereas Plotinus specifically addresses his target in the *Enneads* as the Gnostics.

[A. H. Armstrong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._H._Armstrong) identified the so-called "Gnostics" that Plotinus was attacking as Jewish and Pagan, in his introduction to the tract in his translation of the *Enneads*. Armstrong alluding to Gnosticism being a Hellenic philosophical heresy of sorts, which later engaged Christianity and Neoplatonism[.\[54\]](#page-11-3)[\[55\]](#page-12-0)

[John D. Turner,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_D._Turner) professor of religious studies at the University of Nebraska, and famed translator and editor of the [Nag Hammadi library,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nag_Hammadi_library) stated[\[56\]](#page-12-1) that the text Plotinus and his students read was Sethian Gnosticism, which predates Christianity. It appears that Plotinus attempted to clarify how the philosophers of the academy had not arrived at the same conclusions (such as [dystheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dystheism) or [misotheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misotheism) for the creator God as an answer to the [problem of evil\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Problem_of_evil) as the targets of his criticism.

#### **Iamblichus**

Later, the Neoplatonist [Iamblichus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iamblichus_of_Chalcis) changed the role of the "One", effectively altering the role of the Demiurge as second cause or dyad, which was one of the reasons that Iamblichus and his teacher [Porphyry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porphyry_(philosopher)) came into conflict.

The figure of the Demiurge emerges in the theoretic of Iamblichus, which conjoins the transcendent, incommunicable "One," or Source. Here, at the summit of this system, the Source and Demiurge (material realm) coexist via the process of *[henosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henosis)*. [\[57\]](#page-12-2) Iamblichus describes the One as a monad whose first principle or emanation is intellect (*nous*), while among "the many" that follow it there is a second, super-existent

"One" that is the producer of intellect or soul (*psyche*).

The "One" is further separated into spheres of intelligence; the first and superior sphere is objects of thought, while the latter sphere is the domain of thought. Thus, a [triad](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/triad#Noun) is formed of the [intelligible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perception) *nous*, the [intellective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nous) *nous*, and the *[psyche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psyche_(psychology))* in order to reconcile further the various Hellenistic philosophical schools of [Aristotle'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle)s *[actus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actus_et_potentia)* [and](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actus_et_potentia) *[potentia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actus_et_potentia)* (actuality and potentiality) of the [unmoved mover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unmoved_mover) and Plato's Demiurge.

Then within this intellectual triad Iamblichus assigns the third rank to the Demiurge, identifying it with the perfect or Divine *nous* with the intellectual triad being promoted to a *hebdomad* (pure intellect).

In the theoretic of Plotinus, *nous* produces nature through intellectual mediation, thus the intellectualizing gods are followed by a triad of psychic gods.

# **Later representations**

#### **Cathars**

[Catharism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catharism) apparently inherited their idea of Satan as the creator of the evil world from Gnosticism. [Gilles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilles_Quispel) [Quispel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilles_Quispel) writes, "There is a direct link between ancient Gnosticism and Catharism. The Cathars held that the creator of the world, Satanael, had usurped the name of God, but that he had subsequently been unmasked and told that he was not really God."[\[58\]](#page-12-3)

### **Modern influence**

[Emil Cioran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emil_Cioran) also wrote his *Le mauvais démiurge* ("The Evil Demiurge"), published in 1969, influenced by Gnosticism and [Schopenhauerian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schopenhauer) interpretation of Platonic ontology, as well as that of Plotinus.
