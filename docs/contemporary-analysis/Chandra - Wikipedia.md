### **Etymology and other names**

### [edit](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Chandra&action=edit§ion=1)

## ![](_page_2_Picture_5.jpeg)

The scriptures compare the Moon to a white goose in the blue lake of sky[.\[2\]](#page-11-2)

The word "Chandra" literally means "bright, shining or glittering" and is used for the ["Moon"](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon) in [Sanskrit](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit) and other [Indo-Aryan languages.](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Aryan_languages) [\[6\]](#page-11-5)[\[7\]](#page-11-6) It is also the name of various other figures in Hindu mythology, including an asura and a [Suryavamsha](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_dynasty) king[.\[8\]](#page-11-7) It is also a common Indian name and surname. Both male and female name variations exist in many South Asian languages that originate from Sanskrit.

Some of the synonyms of Chandra include Soma (distill), Indu (bright drop), Atrisuta (son of Atri), Shashin or Shachin (marked by hare), Taradhipa (lord of stars) and Nishakara (the night maker), Nakshatrapati (lord of the [Nakshatra\)](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nakshatra), Oshadhipati

(lord of herbs), Uduraj or Udupati (water lord), Kumudanatha (lord of lotuses) and Udupa (boat)[.\[2\]](#page-11-2)[\[5\]](#page-11-0)

Soma is one of the most common other names used for the deity; but the earliest use of the word to refer to the Moon is a subject of scholarly debate. Some scholars state that the word Soma is occasionally used for the Moon in the Vedas, while other scholars suggest that such usage emerged only in the post-Vedic literature[.\[9\]](#page-11-8)

In the [Vedas,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas) the word Soma is primarily used for an [intoxicating and energizing/healing plant drink](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soma_(drink)) and the deity representing it[.\[10\]](#page-11-9)[\[11\]](#page-12-0) In post-Vedic [Hindu mythology,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_mythology) Soma is used for Chandra, who is associated with the moon and the plant[.\[9\]](#page-11-8)[\[12\]](#page-12-1)[\[13\]](#page-12-2) The Hindu texts state that the Moon is lit and nourished by the Sun, and that it is Moon where the divine nectar of immortality resides[.\[5\]](#page-11-0) In Puranas, Soma is sometimes also used to refer to [Vishnu,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishnu) [Shiva](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shiva) (as Somanatha), [Yama](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yama) and [Kubera.](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kubera) [\[14\]](#page-12-3) In some Indian texts, Soma is the name of an [apsara;](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apsara) alternatively it is the name of any medicinal concoction, or rice-water gruel, or heaven and sky, as well as the name of certain places of pilgrimage[.\[14\]](#page-12-3)

Inspired by his interest in Indian mysticism, [Aldous Huxley](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aldous_Huxley) took the name for the drug used by the state in his novel [Brave New](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brave_New_World) [World](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brave_New_World) to control the population after the Vedic ritual drink Soma.

## Bengal[.\[15\]](#page-12-4)

The origin of Soma is traced back to the Hindu [Vedic texts,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedic_texts) where he is the personification of [a drink made from a plant](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soma_(drink)) [with the same name.](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soma_(drink)) Scholars state that the plant had an important role in Vedic civilization and thus, the deity was one of the most important gods of the pantheon. In these Vedic texts, Soma is praised as the lord of plants and forests; the king of rivers and earth; and the father of the gods. The entire [Mandala 9](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandala_9) of the [Rigveda](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigveda) is dedicated to Soma, both the plant and the deity[.\[16\]](#page-12-5) The identification of Soma as a lunar deity in the Vedic texts is a controversial topic among scholars.[\[9\]](#page-11-8) According to William J. Wilkins, "In later years the name Soma was [...] given to the moon. How and why this change took place is not known; but in the later of the Vedic hymns there is some evidence of the transition[.\[note 1\]](#page-10-0)[\[17\]](#page-12-6)

In post Vedic texts like the [Ramayana](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramayana), the [Mahabharata](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahabharata) and the [Puranas](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puranas), Soma is mentioned as a lunar deity and has many epithets including Chandra[.\[18\]](#page-12-7)[\[19\]](#page-12-8) According to most of these texts, Chandra, along with his brothers [Dattatreya](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dattatreya) and [Durvasa,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durvasa) were the sons of the sage [Atri](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atri) and his wife [Anasuya.](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anasuya) The [Devi Bhagavata Purana](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devi_Bhagvata_Purana) states Chandra to be the [avatar](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avatar) of the creator god [Brahma.](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahma) [\[8\]](#page-11-7) Some texts contain varying accounts regarding Chandra's birth. According to one text, he is the son of [Dharma;](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharma) while another mention Prabhakar as his father[.\[19\]](#page-12-8) Many legends about Chandra are told in the scriptures.

## Chandra, British Museum, 13th century, Konark

One of the most prominent narratives involving Chandra centers on his illicit relationship with [Tara,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tara_(Hindu_goddess)) the wife of [Brihaspati,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brihaspati) the preceptor of the [Devas.](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deva_(Hinduism)) According to the story, Tara came to Chandra's abode during her wanderings, and they fell in love with each other. Despite multiple attempts by Brihaspati to reclaim his wife, Tara remained with Chandra. Brihaspati's disciples and even Brihaspati himself confronted Chandra, who argued that Tara had come of her own will and would leave when she desired. The situation escalated when Brihaspati sought the intervention of the Devas. [Indra,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indra) the king of the Devas, threatened war to compel Chandra to return Tara. However, the Devas were divided in their opinions, and the [Asuras,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asura) led by their preceptor [Shukra,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shukra) sided with Chandra. A fierce conflict ensued between the Devas and Asuras, resulting in cosmic turmoil. Eventually, [Brahma,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahma) the creator, intervened to restore order. He admonished Chandra and Shukra, leading to the cessation of the battle. Chandra reluctantly returned Tara to Brihaspati. However, Tara was already pregnant, which gave rise to a dispute over the child's paternity. Brahma once again intervened, questioning Tara directly. She revealed that Chandra was the father. Consequently, Brahma ordered Brihaspati to hand over the child to Chandra. The child born from this union was named [Budha,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budha) who later became an important deity associated with intellect and wisdom.[\[8\]](#page-11-7)[\[9\]](#page-11-8) Chandra is traditionally married to the 27 daughters of the sage [Daksha,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daksha) who personify the 27 [Nakshatras](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nakshatra) (lunar mansions). These daughters are named [Ashvini,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashvini) [Bharani,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bharani) [Krittika,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krittika) [Rohini,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rohini_(nakshatra)) [Mrigashira,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mrigash%C4%ABrsha) [Ardra,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ardra_Nakshatra) [Punarvasu,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punarvasu) [Pushya,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pushya)

[Ashlesha,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashlesha) [Magha,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magha_(nakshatra)) Purvaphalguni, [Uttaraphalguni,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uttara_Phalgun%C4%AB) [Hasta,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasta_(nakshatra)) [Chitra,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chitra_(nakshatra)) [Swati,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swati_(star)) [Vishakha,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishakha) [Anuradha,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anuradha_(nakshatra)) [Jyeshtha,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jyeshtha_(nakshatra)) [Mula,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mula_(nakshatra)) [Purvashadha,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Purvashadha&action=edit&redlink=1) [Uttara Ashadha,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uttara_Ashadha) [Shravana,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shravana) [Dhanishta,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhanishta) [Shatabhisha,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shatabhisha) [Purvabhadra,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C5%ABrva_Bh%C4%81drapad%C4%81) [Uttarabhadra,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uttarabhadra) and [Revati.](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revati_(nakshatra)) Chandra's movement through these Nakshatras reflects the lunar cycle and influences various astrological and ritual practices. Despite being married to all 27, Chandra showed special affection for Rohini, causing jealousy among his other wives. The preferential treatment of Rohini led the other wives to complain to their father, Daksha. Despite repeated warnings from Daksha, Chandra continued to favor Rohini. In anger, Daksha cursed Chandra to suffer from [Kshayaroga](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuberculosis) (consumption or tuberculosis). This affliction impacted not just Chandra but also affected the medicinal plants and other living beings dependent on lunar influence. The [Devas](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deva_(Hinduism)) intervened, and after much pleading, Daksha modified his curse, allowing Chandra to be free from the disease for half of every month by bathing in the [Sarasvati Tirtha.](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarasvati_River) This mythological explanation accounts for the waxing and waning of the moon, with Chandra's periodic immersion in the sacred waters symbolizing his gradual recovery and decline ([Mahabharata](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahabharata), Shalya Parva, Chapter 35)[.\[8\]](#page-11-7)[\[20\]](#page-12-9)

In the mythological narrative of the [Samudra Manthan](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samudra_Manthan) (Churning of the Ocean), the Devas and [Asuras](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asura) sought the [Amrita](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amrita) (nectar of immortality). A demon named [Rahu](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rahu) disguised himself as a Brahmin and attempted to drink the nectar. [Surya](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surya) (the Sun) and Chandra recognized the deception and alerted [Vishnu,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishnu) who severed Rahu's head with his [Sudarshana Chakra.](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudarshana_Chakra) However, because Rahu had consumed the nectar, his head and body became immortal, forming the entities Rahu and [Ketu.](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ketu_(mythology)) Rahu and Ketu, bearing enmity toward Surya and Chandra for revealing their deception, periodically "swallow" the moon and the sun, leading to solar and lunar eclipses. This myth provides a symbolic representation of eclipses as moments of cosmic tension and conflict. During the reign of Emperor [Prithu,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prithu) [Bhumidevi](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhumidevi) (Earth) was transformed into a cow to provide nourishment to all beings. When the sages milked Bhumidevi, Chandra served as the calf, an act which pleased [Brahma.](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahma) In recognition, Brahma crowned Chandra as the king of stars and medicines. This role signifies Chandra's influence over medicinal plants and the healing arts, linking his cycles to the growth and potency of herbs.[\[8\]](#page-11-7)

Chandra is one of the Ashta-vasus, a group of eight elemental deities. He had four sons[—Varchas,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varchas) Shishira, Prana, and Ramana—with his wife [Manohara.](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manohara) Notably, his son Varchas was later reborn as [Abhimanyu,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abhimanyu) the heroic son of [Arjuna](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arjuna) in the Mahabharata. [\[8\]](#page-11-7)

According to another legend, [Ganesha](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ganesha) was returning home on his mount Krauncha (a shrew) late on a full moon night after a mighty feast given by [Kubera.](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kubera) On the journey back, a snake crossed their path and frightened by it, his mount ran away dislodging Ganesha in the process. An overstuffed Ganesha fell to the ground on his stomach, vomiting out all the [Modaks](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modak) he had eaten. On observing this, Chandra laughed at Ganesha. Ganesha lost his temper and broke off one of his tusks and flung it straight at the Moon, hurting him, and cursed him so that he would never be whole again. Therefore, It is forbidden to behold Chandra on [Ganesh Chaturthi.](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ganesh_Chaturthi) This legend accounts for the Moon's waxing and waning including a big crater on the Moon, a dark spot, visible even from Earth[.\[21\]](#page-12-10)

Chandra holds a crucial place in [Hindu astrology](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_astrology) [\(Jyotisha\)](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jyotisha) and is associated with the mind, emotions, and fertility. His phases are believed to influence human behavior, agriculture, and the timing of rituals. As the luminary of the night, Chandra embodies the reflective and nurturing aspects of the cosmos, balancing the solar energies represented by Surya.[\[8\]](#page-11-7)

Soma's iconography varies in Hindu texts. The most common is one where he is a white-coloured deity, holding a mace in his hand, riding a chariot with three wheels and three or more white horses (up to ten)[.\[5\]](#page-11-0)

Soma as the Moon-deity is also found in [Buddhism,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism) [\[22\]](#page-12-11) and [Jainism.](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jainism) [\[23\]](#page-12-12)

Soma is the root of the word Somavara or [Monday](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monday) in the Hindu calendar[.\[24\]](#page-12-13) The word "Monday" in the Greco-Roman and other Indo-European calendars is also dedicated to the Moon. [\[25\]](#page-13-0) Soma is part of the [Navagraha](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navagraha) in the Hindu zodiac system. The role and importance of the Navagraha developed over time with various influences. Deifying the moon and its astrological significance occurred as early as the [Vedic period](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedic_period) and was recorded in the [Vedas.](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas) The earliest work of astrology recorded in India is the [Vedanga Jyotisha](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedanga_Jyotisha) which began to be compiled in the 14th century BCE. The moon and various [classical planets](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_planet) were referenced in the [Atharvaveda](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atharvaveda) around 1000 BCE.

The Navagraha was furthered by additional contributions from [Western Asia,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Asia) including [Zoroastrian](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrian_calendar) and [Hellenistic](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_astrology) influences. The [Yavanajataka,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yavanajataka) or 'Science of the [Yavanas](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yavana)', was written by the [Indo-Greek](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Greek_Kingdom) named ["Yavanesvara"](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yavanesvara) ("Lord of the Greeks") under the rule of the [Western Kshatrapa](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Kshatrapa) king [Rudrakarman I.](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudrakarman_I) The Navagraha would further develop and culminate in the [Shaka era](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shaka_era) with the [Saka,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saka) or Scythian, people. Additionally the contributions by the Saka people would be the basis of the [Indian national calendar,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_national_calendar) which is also called the Saka calendar.

The [Hindu calendar](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_calendar) is a [lunisolar calendar](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunisolar_calendar) which records both lunar and solar cycles. Like the Navagraha, it was developed with the successive contributions of various works.

Soma was presumed to be a planet in Hindu astronomical texts[.\[26\]](#page-13-1) It is often discussed in various [Sanskrit](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit) astronomical texts, such as the 5th century Aryabhatiya by [Aryabhatta,](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryabhatta) the 6th century Romaka by Latadeva and Panca Siddhantika by Varahamihira, the 7th century Khandakhadyaka by Brahmagupta and the 8th century Sisyadhivrddida by Lalla[.\[27\]](#page-13-2) Other texts such as Surya Siddhanta dated to have been complete sometime between the 5th century and 10th century present their chapters on various planets with deity mythologies[.\[27\]](#page-13-2) However, they show that the Hindu scholars were aware of elliptical orbits, and the texts include sophisticated formulae to calculate its past and future positions[:\[28\]](#page-13-3)
