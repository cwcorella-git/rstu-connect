## ![](_page_1_Picture_2.jpeg)

Killing No Murder, cover page, 18th century reprint of 17th century English pamphlet written to inspire and [make righteous the act of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyrannicide) [assassinating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyrannicide) Oliver Cromwell

A **tyrant** (from [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language) [τύραννος](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%CF%84%CF%8D%CF%81%CE%B1%CE%BD%CE%BD%CE%BF%CF%82#Ancient_Greek) (túrannos) 'absolute ruler'), in the modern [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language) usage of the word, is an [absolute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autocracy) [ruler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autocracy) who is unrestrained by [law,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law) or one who has [usurped](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Usurper) a legitimate ruler's sovereignty. Often portrayed as cruel, tyrants may defend their positions by resorting to [repressive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_repression) means[.\[1\]](#page-16-0) [\[2\]](#page-16-1) The original Greek term meant an absolute sovereign who came to power without [constitutional right,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_right) [\[3\]](#page-16-2) yet the word had a neutral connotation during the [Archaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaic_Greece) and early [Classical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Greece) periods[.\[4\]](#page-16-3) However, Greek philosopher [Plato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato) saw tyrannos as a negative form of government, and on account of the decisive influence of philosophy on politics, deemed tyranny the "fourth and worst disorder of a state.["\[5\]](#page-16-4)

Tyrants lack "the very faculty that is the instrument of judgment"—reason. The tyrannical man is enslaved because the best part of him (reason) is enslaved, and likewise, the tyrannical state is enslaved, because it too lacks reason and order[.\[5\]](#page-16-4)

The philosophers Plato and [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle) defined a tyrant as a person who rules without law, using extreme and cruel methods against both his own people and others.[\[6\]](#page-16-5)[\[7\]](#page-16-6) The

[Encyclopédie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclop%C3%A9die) defined the term as a usurper of sovereign power who makes "his subjects the victims of his passions and unjust desires, which he substitutes for laws"[.\[8\]](#page-17-0) In the late fifth and fourth centuries BC, a new kind of tyrant, one who had the support of the [military,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military) arose – specifically in [Sicily.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicily)

One can apply accusations of tyranny to a variety of types of government:

- to government by one individual (in an [autocracy\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autocracy)
- to government by a minority (in an oligarchy, [tyranny of the](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyranny_of_the_minority) [minority\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyranny_of_the_minority)
- to government by a majority (in a democracy, [tyranny of the](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyranny_of_the_majority) [majority\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyranny_of_the_majority)

The English noun [tyrant](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/tyrant) appears in [Middle English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_English) use, via [Old](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_French) [French,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_French) from the 1290s. The word derives from [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) tyrannus, meaning 'illegitimate ruler', and this in turn from the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) τύραννος túrannos 'monarch, ruler of a [polis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polis)'; túrannos is of [Pre-Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Greek) origin, perhaps from [Lydian.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lydian_language) [\[9\]](#page-17-1)[\[10\]](#page-17-2) The final -t arises in Old French by association with the present participles in -ant. [\[11\]](#page-17-3)

"The word 'tyranny' is used with many meanings, not only by the Greeks but throughout the tradition of the great books."[\[12\]](#page-17-4) The [Oxford English Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_English_Dictionary) offers alternative definitions: a ruler, an [illegitimate ruler,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Usurper) an absolute ruler, or an oppressive, unjust, or cruel ruler[.\[13\]](#page-17-5)[\[14\]](#page-17-6) The term is usually applied to vicious autocrats who rule their subjects by brutal methods.[\[14\]](#page-17-6) Scholar Paul Rahe has asserted that tyrannical rule "in one form or another" has "been the norm throughout human history", and added that "it is not apt to disappear."[\[15\]](#page-17-7)

### **Greco-Roman culture**

### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Tyrant&action=edit§ion=3)

[Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_ancient_Greek_tyrants) and [Sicilian tyrants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tyrants_of_Syracuse) were influential opportunists that came to power by securing the support of different factions of a [deme.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deme) The word tyrannos, possibly pre-Greek, [Pelasgian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelasgian) or eastern in origin[,\[16\]](#page-17-8) then carried no ethical censure; it simply referred to anyone, good or bad, who obtained executive power in a [polis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polis) by unconventional means. Support for the tyrants could come from fellow oligarchs, from the growing middle class or from the peasants who had no land or were in debt to the wealthy landowners.

The Greek tyrants stayed in power by using mercenary soldiers from outside of their respective city-state. To mock tyranny, [Thales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thales) wrote that the strangest thing to see is "an aged tyrant", meaning that tyrants do not have the public support to survive for long[.\[17\]](#page-17-9)

One of the earliest known uses of the word 'tyrant' (in Greek) was by the poet [Archilochus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archilochus) in reference to king [Gyges of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyges_of_Lydia) [Lydia.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyges_of_Lydia) [\[18\]](#page-17-10) Gyges obtained his power by killing King Candaules and marrying his queen[.\[19\]](#page-18-0)

The heyday of the [Archaic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaic_Greece) tyrants came in the early 6th century BC, when [Cleisthenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleisthenes_of_Sicyon) ruled [Sicyon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicyon) in the [Peloponnesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peloponnesus) and [Polycrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polycrates) ruled [Samos.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samos_Island) During this time, revolts overthrew many government[s\[20\]](#page-18-1) in the [Aegean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegean_Sea) world.

[Chilon,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chilon) the ambitious and capable [ephor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephor) of [Sparta,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparta) built a strong alliance amongst neighboring states by making common cause with these groups seeking to oppose unpopular tyrannical rule[.\[21\]](#page-18-2) By intervening against the tyrants of Sicyon, Corinth and Athens, Sparta thus came to assume Hellenic leadership prior to the Persian invasions. Simultaneously [Persia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_Empire) first started making inroads into Greece, and many tyrants sought Persian help against popular forces seeking to remove them[.\[22\]](#page-18-3)

Corinth hosted one of the earliest of Greek tyrants[.\[23\]](#page-18-4) In [Corinth,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Corinth) growing wealth from colonial enterprises, and the wider horizons brought about by the export of wine and oil, together with the new experiences of the Eastern Mediterranean brought back by returning [mercenary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercenaries) [hoplites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoplites) employed overseas created a new environment. Conditions were right for [Cypselus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cypselus) to overthrow the [aristocratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristocracy) power of the dominant but unpopular clan of [Bacchiadae.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacchiadae) [\[24\]](#page-18-5) Clan members were killed, executed, driven out or exiled in 657 BC. Corinth prospered economically under his rule, and Cypselus managed to rule without a [bodyguard.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodyguard) When he then bequeathed his position to his son, [Periander.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periander) Periander was less popular than his father and was reputed to be more brutal. [\[25\]](#page-18-6)

Nevertheless, under Cypselus and Periander, Corinth extended and tightened her control over her colonial enterprises, and exports of Corinthian pottery flourished. However, tyrants seldom succeeded in establishing an untroubled line of succession. Periander threw his pregnant wife downstairs

(killing her), burnt his concubines alive, exiled his son, warred with his father-in-law and attempted to castrate 300 sons of his perceived enemies[.\[26\]](#page-18-7) He retained his position. Periander's successor was less fortunate and was expelled. Afterward, Corinth was ruled by an oligarchy that would eventually be overthrown in a pro-democratic revolution in the 4th century. [\[25\]](#page-18-6)

![](_page_5_Picture_3.jpeg)

A [sculptural pairing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmodius_and_Aristogeiton_(sculpture)) of [Harmodius and Aristogeiton,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmodius_and_Aristogeiton) who became known as the [tyrannicides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyrannicides) after they killed [Hipparchus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hipparchus_(son_of_Peisistratos)) and were the preeminent symbol of [Athenian democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athenian_democracy)

Athens hosted its tyrants late in the Archaic period.[\[27\]](#page-18-8) In [Athens,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens) the inhabitants first gave the title of tyrant to [Peisistratos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peisistratos) (a relative of [Solon,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solon) the Athenian lawgiver) who succeeded in 546 BC, after two failed attempts, to install himself as tyrant. Supported by the prosperity of the peasantry and landowning interests of the plain, which was prospering from the rise of olive oil exports, as well as his clients from [Marathon,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marathon,_Greece) he managed to achieve absolute power[.\[28\]](#page-18-9) Through an ambitious program of public works, which included fostering the state cult of [Athena;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athena) encouraging the creation of festivals;

supporting the [Panathenaic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panathenaic_Games) in which prizes were jars of olive oil; and supporting the [Dionysia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dionysia) (ultimately leading to the development of Athenian drama), Peisistratos managed to maintain his personal popularity[.\[29\]](#page-18-10)[\[30\]](#page-18-11)

He was followed by his sons, and with the subsequent growth of Athenian [democracy,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democracy) the title "tyrant" took on its familiar negative connotations. The murder of Peisistratos' son, the tyrant [Hipparchus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hipparchus_(son_of_Peisistratos)) by [Aristogeiton and Harmodios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmodius_and_Aristogeiton) in Athens in 514 BC, and were referred to since as the ["tyrannicides"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyrannicide) (i.e., of killers of tyrants)[.\[31\]](#page-19-0) In 510 Hippias, the brother of Hipparchus, was expelled by a combination of intrigue, exile and Spartan arms[.\[32\]](#page-19-1)[\[33\]](#page-19-2) The anti-tyrannical attitude became especially prevalent in Athens after 508 BC, when [Cleisthenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleisthenes) reformed the political system so that it resembled [demokratia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demokratia). Hippias (Peisistratos' other son) offered to rule the Greeks on behalf of the Persians and provided military advice to the Persians against the Greeks[.\[34\]](#page-19-3)

The best known Sicilian tyrants appeared long after the Archaic period[.\[35\]](#page-19-4) The tyrannies of Sicily came about due to similar causes, but here the threat of [Carthaginian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Carthage) attack prolonged tyranny, facilitating the rise of military leaders with the people united behind them[.\[36\]](#page-19-5)[\[37\]](#page-19-6) Such examples of Sicilian tyrants are [Gelo,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gelo) [Hiero I,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiero_I) [Dionysius the Elder,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dionysius_the_Elder) [Dionysius the Younger,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dionysius_the_Younger) and [Agathocles of Syracuse.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agathocles_of_Syracuse) [\[37\]](#page-19-6) The dangers threatening the lives of the Sicilian tyrants are highlighted in the moral tale of the [Sword of Damocles.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sword_of_Damocles) [\[38\]](#page-19-7)

Under the [Macedonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedon) [hegemony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hegemony) in the 4th and 3rd century

BC a new generation of tyrants rose in Greece, especially under the rule of king [Antigonus II Gonatas,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antigonus_II_Gonatas) who installed his puppets in many cities of the Peloponnese. Examples were [Cleon of Sicyon,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleon_of_Sicyon) [Aristodemus of Megalopolis,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristodemus_the_Good) [Aristomachus I of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristomachos_the_Elder) [Argos,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristomachos_the_Elder) [Abantidas of Sicyon,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abantidas) [Aristippus of Argos,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristippus_of_Argos) [Lydiadas of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lydiadas_of_Megalopolis) [Megalopolis,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lydiadas_of_Megalopolis) [Aristomachus II of Argos,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristomachos_of_Argos) and [Xenon of Hermione.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenon_(tyrant)) [\[37\]](#page-19-6)[\[36\]](#page-19-5)

Against these rulers, in 280 BC the democratic cities started to join forces in the [Achaean League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaean_League) which was able to expand its influence even into [Corinthia,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corinthia) [Megaris,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megaris) [Argolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argolis) and [Arcadia.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arcadia_(ancient_region)) From 251 BC under the leadership of [Aratus of Sicyon,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aratus_of_Sicyon) the Achaeans liberated many cities, in several cases by convincing the tyrants to step down, and when Aratus died in 213 BC, Hellas had been free of tyrants for more than 15 years.[\[39\]](#page-19-8) The last tyrant on the Greek mainland, [Nabis of Sparta,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabis_of_Sparta) was assassinated in 192 BC[.\[40\]](#page-19-9)

Roman historians like [Suetonius,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suetonius) [Tacitus,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacitus) [Plutarch,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutarch) and [Josephus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephus) often spoke of "tyranny" in opposition to "liberty".[\[41\]](#page-19-10) Tyranny was associated with imperial rule and those rulers who usurped too much authority from the [Roman Senate.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Senate) Those who were advocates of "liberty" tended to be pro-Republic and pro-Senate. For instance, regarding [Julius Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar) and his assassins, Suetonius wrote:

Therefore the plots which had previously been formed separately, often by groups of two or three, were united in a general conspiracy, since even the populace no longer were pleased with present conditions, but both secretly and openly rebelled at his tyranny and cried out for defenders of their

## liberty[.\[42\]](#page-20-0)

Citizens of the empire were circumspect in identifying tyrants. ["Cicero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cicero)'s head and hands [were] cut off and nailed to the rostrum of the Senate to remind everyone of the perils of speaking out against tyranny.["\[43\]](#page-20-1)

Josephus identified tyrants in Biblical history (in Antiquities of the Jews) including [Nimrod,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nimrod) [Moses,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses) the [Maccabees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maccabees) and [Herod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herod_the_Great) [the Great.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herod_the_Great) [\[44\]](#page-20-2)

### **Greek political thought**

### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Tyrant&action=edit§ion=10)

The Greeks defined both usurpers and those inheriting rule from usurpers as tyrants[.\[4\]](#page-16-3) [Polybius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polybius) (c. 150 BC) indicated that eventually, any one-man rule (monarchy/executive) governing form would become corrupted into a tyranny[.\[45\]](#page-20-3)

The Greek philosophers stressed the quality of rule rather than legitimacy or absolutism. "Both Plato and Aristotle speak of the king as a good monarch and the tyrant as a bad one. Both say that monarchy, or rule by a single man, is royal when it is for the welfare of the ruled and tyrannical when it serves only the interest of the ruler. Both make lawlessness – either a violation of existing laws or government by personal fiat without settled laws – a mark of tyranny.["\[12\]](#page-17-4)

Tyranny is considered an important subject, one of the "Great Ideas" of Western thought. The classics contain many references to tyranny and its causes, effects, methods, practitioners, alternatives. They consider tyranny from historical, religious, ethical, political and fictional perspectives. "If any point in political theory is indisputable, it would seem to be that tyranny is the worst corruption of government – a vicious misuse of power and a violent abuse of human beings who are subject to it.["\[12\]](#page-17-4) While this may represent a consensus position among the classics, it is not unanimous – [Thomas Hobbes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes) dissented, claiming no objective distinction, such as being vicious or virtuous, existed among monarchs. "They that are discontented under monarchy, call it tyranny; and they that are displeased with aristocracy, call it oligarchy: so also, they which find themselves grieved under a democracy, call it anarchy...["\[46\]](#page-20-4)

The [first part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inferno_(Dante)) of [Dante Alighieri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dante_Alighieri)'s [The Divine Comedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Divine_Comedy) describes tyrants ("who laid hold on blood and plunder") in the seventh level of Hell, where they are submerged in boiling blood. These include [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great) and [Attila the Hun,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attila) and share the level with highway robbers[.\[47\]](#page-20-5)

[Niccolò Machiavelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niccol%C3%B2_Machiavelli) described tyrannical rule in [The Prince](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Prince), while never actually using the word "tyrant", instead referring to such individuals as "princes". This is regardless of the legitimacy of the ruler's position -- in his [Discourses on Livy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discourses_on_Livy) he even sometimes he calls leaders of republics "princes".[\[48\]](#page-20-6) He also does not share in the traditional view of tyranny, and in his Discourses he sometimes explicitly acts as an advisor to tyrants[.\[49\]](#page-20-7)[\[50\]](#page-20-8)

The [Ancient Greeks,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece) as well as those who lived in and governed the [Roman Republic,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Republic) became generally quite wary of many people seeking to implement a popular coup. [Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare)

portrays the struggle of one such anti-tyrannical Roman, [Marcus Junius Brutus,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Junius_Brutus) in his play [Julius Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar_(play)).

In Gibbon's Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, Volume I, Chapter III, Augustus was shown to assume the power of a tyrant while sharing power with the reformed senate. "After a decent resistance, the crafty tyrant submitted to the orders of the senate; and consented to receive the government of the provinces, and the general command of the Roman armies..." Emperors "humbly professed themselves the accountable ministers of the senate, whose supreme decrees they dictated and obeyed." The Roman Empire "may be defined as an absolute monarchy disguised by the forms of a commonwealth.["\[51\]](#page-20-9)

![](_page_10_Picture_4.jpeg)

[François Gérard,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois_G%C3%A9rard) The French people demanding destitution of the Tyrant on [10 August 1792](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/10_August_(French_Revolution))

During the [Age of Enlightenment,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment) Western thinkers applied the word tyranny to the system of governance that had developed around [aristocracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristocracy) and [monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarchy). The English philosopher [John Locke,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Locke) as part of his argument against the ["Divine Right of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_Right_of_Kings) [Kings"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_Right_of_Kings) in his 1689 book [Two Treatises of Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Treatises_of_Government), defined it as such: "Tyranny is the exercise of power beyond right, which nobody can have a right to; and this is making use of the power any one has in his hands, not for the good of those who are under it, but for his own private, separate advantage."[\[52\]](#page-20-10)

Locke's concept of tyranny influenced the writers of subsequent generations who developed the concept of tyranny as counterpoint to ideas of [human rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights) and [democracy.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democracy) American statesman [Thomas Jefferson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Jefferson) described the actions of King [George III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_III) as "tyrannical" in the [United](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Declaration_of_Independence) [States Declaration of Independence.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Declaration_of_Independence)

Enlightenment philosophers seemed to define tyranny by its associated characteristics.

- "The sovereign is called a tyrant who knows no laws but his caprice." [Voltaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltaire) in a Philosophical Dictionary
- "Where Law ends Tyranny begins." Locke in Two Treatises of Government
- "For those who misuse power will be called tyrants, not just men." Medrano in [República Mista](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rep%C3%BAblica_Mista)

[Edward Sexby's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Sexby) 1657 pamphlet, "Killing, No Murder" [\(PDF file\)](https://www.yorku.ca/comninel/courses/3025pdf/Killing_Noe_Murder.pdf) outlined 14 key traits of a tyrant, as the pamphlet was written to inspire the assassination of Oliver Cromwell, and show in what circumstances an assassination might be considered honorable. The full document mulls over and references points on the matter from early pre-Christian history, up into the 17th century when the pamphlet was writ. Of the most prevailing traits of tyranny outlined, ["Killing, No Murder"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Killing_No_Murder) emphasizes:

- 1. Prior military leadership service tyrants are often former captains or generals, which allows them to assume a degree of honor, loyalty, and reputability regarding matters of state
- 2. Fraud over force most tyrants are likely to manipulate their way into supreme power rather than force it militarily

- 3. Defamation and/or disbanding of formerly respectable persons, intellectuals, or institutions, and the discouragement of refined thinking or public involvement in state affairs
- 4. Absence or minimalization of collective input, bargaining, or debate (assemblies, conferences, etc.)
- 5. Amplification of military activity for the purposes of public distraction, raising new levies, or opening future business pathways
- 6. Tit-for-tat symbiosis in domestic relations: e.g. finding religious ideas permissible insofar as they are useful and flattering of the tyrant; finding aristocrats or the nobility laudable & honorable insofar as they are compliant with the will of the tyrant or in service of the tyrant, etc.
- 7. Pretenses toward inspiration from God
- 8. Pretenses toward a love of God and religion
- 9. Grow or maintain public impoverishment as a way of removing the efficacy of the people's will

In Scotland, [Samuel Rutherford's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Rutherford) [Lex Rex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lex_Rex) and [Alexander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Shields) [Shields'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Shields) A Hind Let Loose were influential works of theology written in opposition to tyranny.

In his 1602 [República Mista](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rep%C3%BAblica_Mista), [Tomás Fernández de Medrano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom%C3%A1s_Fern%C3%A1ndez_de_Medrano) defines tyranny as a natural extension of [oligarchy,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristocracy#Differentiation) arising when monarchs believe they possess an [absolute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_monarchy) right to rule as they please, without accountability or opposition. He argues that such rulers, particularly those who oppress or neglect their [subjects,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizenship) seldom govern with [wisdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisdom) or [justice.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justice) [\[53\]](#page-21-0) For Medrano, "It is so deeply ingrained in the hearts of such men

(and this is almost a general truth), to hate and detest tyrants as much as to love and revere just kings and princes."[\[53\]](#page-21-0) A modern tyrant might be defined by proven violation of international criminal law such as [crimes against humanity.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimes_against_humanity) [\[54\]](#page-21-1) [\[55\]](#page-21-2)[\[56\]](#page-21-3)

Various lists of tyrants include:

### **Methods of obtaining and retaining power**

### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Tyrant&action=edit§ion=14)

The path of a tyrant can appear easy and pleasant (for all but the aristocracy). In 1939, [Will Durant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Will_Durant) wrote:

Hence the road to power in Greece commercial cities was simple: to attack the aristocracy, defend the poor, and come to an understanding with the middle classes. Arrived at power, the dictator abolished debts, or confiscated large estates, taxed the rich to finance public works, or otherwise redistributed the over-concentrated wealth; and while attaching the masses to himself through such measures, he secured the support of the business community by promoting trade with state coinage and commercial treaties, and by raising the social prestige of the bourgeoisie. Forced to depend upon popularity instead of hereditary power, the dictatorships for the most part kept out of war, supported religion, maintained order, promoted morality, favored the higher status of women, encouraged the arts, and lavished revenues upon the beautification of their cities. And they did all these things, in many cases, while preserving the forms of popular government, so that even under despotism the people learned the ways of liberty. When the dictatorship [of the tyrant] had served to destroy the aristocracy the people destroyed the dictatorship; and only a few changes were needed to make democracy of freemen a reality as well as a form[.\[67\]](#page-22-5)

In the [Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_(Plato)), [Plato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato) stated: "The people have always some champion whom they set over them and nurse into greatness. [...] This and no other is the root from which a tyrant springs; when he first appears he is a protector".

Tyrants either inherit the position from a previous ruler, rise up the ranks in the military/party or seize power as new men.[\[59\]:](#page-21-6)7

The political methods of obtaining power were occasionally supplemented by deceit or force. Peisistratus of Athens blamed self-inflicted wounds on enemies to justify a bodyguard which he used to seize power. He later appeared with a woman dressed as a goddess to suggest divine sanction of his rule.[\[68\]](#page-22-6) The third time he used mercenaries to seize and retain power. [\[69\]](#page-22-7) Dionysius of Syracuse seized power in a similar fashion, claiming that he had been the object of a plot by enemies in order to obtain an armed force around his person.[\[70\]](#page-22-8)

Lengthy recommendations of methods were made to tyrants by Aristotle (in Politics for example) and [Niccolò Machiavelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niccol%C3%B2_Machiavelli) (in [The Prince](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Prince))[.\[12\]](#page-17-4) These are, in general, force and fraud. They include hiring bodyguards, stirring up wars to keep the people busy and dependent, [purges,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_purge) [assassinations,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assassinations) and unwarranted searches and seizures. Aristotle suggested an alternative means of retaining power – ruling justly[.\[71\]](#page-23-0)

The methods of tyrants to retain power include placating world opinion by staging [rigged elections,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigged_election) using or threatening to use violence, seeking popular support by appeals to [patriotism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriotism), and claiming that conditions have improved[.\[59\]:](#page-21-6)2,7
