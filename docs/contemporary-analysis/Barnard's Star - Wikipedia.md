# [Barnard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_Page)'[s Star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_Page)

Barnard's Star is a small [red dwarf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_dwarf) star in the [constellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constellation) of [Ophiuchus.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ophiuchus) At a distance of 5.96 [light-years](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light-year) (1.83 [pc\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parsec) from Earth, it is the [fourth-nearest-known](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_nearest_stars_and_brown_dwarfs) individual star to the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun) after the three components of the [Alpha Centauri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_Centauri) system, and is the closest star in the [northern celestial hemisphere.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_celestial_hemisphere)[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-15) Its [stellar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_mass) [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_mass) is about 16% of the Sun's, and it has 19% of the Sun's diameter. Despite its proximity, the star has a dim [apparent visual magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apparent_visual_magnitude) of +9.5 and is invisible to the [unaided eye;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unaided_eye) it is much brighter in the [infrared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared) than in visible [light.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light)

The star is named after [E. E. Barnard,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._E._Barnard)[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Kaler-16) an American [astronomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomer) who in 1916 measured its [proper motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_motion) as 10.3 [arcseconds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arcsecond) per year relative to the Sun, the highest known for any star. The star had previously appeared on Harvard University [photographic plates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photographic_plates) in 1888 and 1890[.\[17\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-EEB-17)

Barnard's Star is among the most studied red dwarfs because of its proximity and favorable location for observation near the [celestial equator.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_equator)[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Dawson-7) Historically, research on Barnard's Star has focused on measuring its stellar characteristics, its [astrometry,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrometry) and also refining the limits of possible [extrasolar planets.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extrasolar_planet) Although Barnard's Star is ancient, it still experiences [stellar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flare_star) flare events, one being observed in 1998[.\[18\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Paulson Flare-18)

Barnard's Star has been subject to multiple claims of planets that were later disproven. From the early 1960s to the early 1970s, [Peter van de Kamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_van_de_Kamp) argued that planets orbited Barnard's Star. His specific claims of large [gas giants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_giant) were refuted in the mid-1970s after much debate. In November 2018, a candidate [super-Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super-Earth) planetary companion known as Barnard's Star b was reported to orbit Barnard's Star. It was believed to have a minimum mass of 3.2 [M](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_mass)[E](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_mass) and orbit at 0.4 [AU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_unit)[.\[19\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Ribas-19) However, work presented in July 2021 refuted the existence of this planet[.\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Lubin2021-20)

## Naming

In 2016, the [International Astronomical Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Astronomical_Union) organized a [Working Group on](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Working_Group_on_Star_Names) [Star Names](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Working_Group_on_Star_Names) (WGSN)[\[21\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-WGSN-21) to catalogue and standardize proper names for stars. The WGSN approved the name Barnard's Star for this star on 1 February 2017 and it is now included in the List of IAU-approved Star Names[.\[22\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-IAU-LSN-22)

## Description

Barnard's Star is a red dwarf of the dim [spectral type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_type) M4, and it is too faint to see without a [telescope;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telescope) Its [apparent magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apparent_magnitude) is 9.5.

At 7–12 billion years of age, Barnard's Star is considerably older than the Sun, which is 4.5 billion years old, and it might be among the oldest stars in the [Milky Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milky_Way) galaxy.[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Riedel2005-9) Barnard's Star has lost a great deal of rotational energy, and the periodic slight changes in its brightness indicate that it rotates once in 130 day[s\[23\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Benedict1998-23) (the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun) rotates in 25). Given its age, Barnard's Star was long assumed to be quiescent in terms of stellar activity. In 1998, astronomers observed an intense [stellar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_flare) flare, showing that Barnard's Star is a fl[are star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flare_star)[.\[24\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Flare-24) Barnard's Star has the [variable star designation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variable_star_designation) V2500 Ophiuchi. In 2003, Barnard's Star presented the first detectable change in the [radial velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radial_velocity) of a star caused by its motion. Further variability in the radial velocity of Barnard's Star was attributed to its stellar activity.[\[25\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Kurster-25)

The proper motion of Barnard's Star corresponds to a relative lateral speed of 90 km/s. The 10.3 arcseconds it travels in a year amount to a quarter of a degree in a human lifetime, roughly half the angular diameter of the full Moon[.\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Kaler-16)

The radial velocity of Barnard's Star is −110 km/s, as measured from the [blueshift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blueshift) due to its motion toward the Sun. Combined with its proper motion and distance, this gives a ["space velocity"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_kinematics#Space_velocity) (actual speed relative to the Sun) of 142.6 ± 0.2 km/s. Barnard's Star will make its closest approach to the Sun around 11,800 CE, when it will approach to within about 3.75 light-years.[\[26\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Bobylev-26)

## Search for planets

### Astrometric planetary claims

For a decade from 1963 to about 1973, a substantial number of astronomers accepted a claim by [Peter van de Kamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_van_de_Kamp) that he had detected, by using [astrometry,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methods_of_detecting_exoplanets#Astrometry) a perturbation in the [proper motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_motion) of Barnard's Star consistent with its having one or more planets comparable in mass with [Jupiter.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter) Van de Kamp had been observing the star from 1938, attempting, with colleagues at the [Sproul Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sproul_Observatory) at [Swarthmore College,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swarthmore_College) to find minuscule variations of one [micrometre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micrometre) in its position on [photographic plates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photographic_plate) consistent with [orbital perturbations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perturbation_(astronomy)) that would indicate a planetary companion; this involved as many as ten people averaging their results in looking at plates, to avoid systemic individual errors[.\[32\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Blunder-32) Van de Kamp's initial suggestion was a planet having about 1.6 MJ at a distance of 4.4 AU in a slightly eccentric orbit,[\[33\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-33) and these measurements were apparently refined in a 1969 paper[.\[34\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-34) Later that year, Van de Kamp suggested that there were two planets of 1.1 and 0.8 MJ. [\[35\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-35)

Other astronomers subsequently repeated Van de Kamp's measurements, and two papers in 1973 undermined the claim of a planet or planets. [George Gatewood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_David_Gatewood) and Heinrich Eichhorn, at a different observatory and using newer plate measuring techniques, failed to verify the planetary companion.[\[36\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-36) Another paper published by John L. Hershey four months earlier, also using the Swarthmore observatory, found that changes in the astrometric field of various stars correlated to the timing of adjustments and modifications that had been carried out on the refractor telescope's objective lens;[\[37\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-37) the claimed planet was attributed to an artifact of maintenance and upgrade work. The affair has been discussed as part of a broader scientific review[.\[38\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Bell-38)

Van de Kamp never acknowledged any error and published a further claim of two planets' existence as late as 1982[;\[39\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-39) he died in 1995. Wulff [Heintz,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wulff-Dieter_Heintz) Van de Kamp's successor at Swarthmore and an expert on [double stars,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_star) questioned his findings and began publishing criticisms from 1976 onwards. The two men were reported to have become estranged because of this[.\[40\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Swathmore-40)

#### Barnard's Star b

## ![](_page_4_Picture_3.jpeg)

Artist's impression of the surface of a [super-Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super-Earth) orbiting Barnard's Star[\[41\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-41)

In November 2018, an international team of astronomers announced the detection by [radial velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doppler_spectroscopy) of a candidate [super-Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super-Earth) orbiting in relatively close proximity to Barnard's Star. Led by Ignasi Ribas of Spain their work, conducted over two decades of observation, provided strong evidence of the planet's existence. [\[19\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Ribas-19)[\[42\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-42) However, the existence of the planet was refuted in 2021, because the radial velocity signal was found to originate from a stellar activity cycle,[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Lubin2021-20) and a study in 2022 confirmed this result[.\[43\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Artigau2022-43)

Dubbed Barnard's Star b, the planet was thought to be near the stellar system's [snow line,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frost_line_(astrophysics)) which is an ideal spot for the icy accretion of proto-planetary material. It was thought to orbit at 0.4 AU every 233 days and had a proposed [minimum mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum_mass) of 3.2 [M](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_mass)[E.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_mass) The planet would have most likely been frigid, with an estimated surface temperature of about −170 °C (−274 °F), and lie outside Barnard Star's presumed [habitable zone.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habitable_zone) Direct imaging of the planet and its tell-tale [light signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_signature) would have been possible in the decade after its discovery. Further faint and unaccounted-for perturbations in the system suggested there may be a second planetary companion even farther out.[\[44\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-sciam-44)

#### Refining planetary boundaries

For the more than four decades between van de Kamp's rejected claim and the eventual announcement of a planet candidate, Barnard's Star was carefully studied and the mass and orbital boundaries for possible planets were slowly tightened. [M dwarfs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M_dwarf) such as Barnard's Star are more easily studied than larger stars in this regard because their lower masses render perturbations more obvious.[\[45\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-45)

Null results for planetary companions continued throughout the 1980s and 1990s, including [interferometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interferometric) work with the [Hubble Space Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubble_Space_Telescope) in 1999.[\[46\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Hubble99-46) Gatewood was able to show in 1995 that planets with 10 MJ were impossible around Barnard's Star,[\[38\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Bell-38) in a paper which helped refine the negative certainty regarding planetary objects in general.[\[47\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Gatewood95-47) In 1999, the Hubble work further excluded planetary companions of 0.8 MJ with an orbital period of less than 1,000 days (Jupiter's orbital period is 4,332 days)[,\[46\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Hubble99-46) while Kuerster determined in 2003 that within the [habitable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumstellar_habitable_zone) [zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumstellar_habitable_zone) around Barnard's Star, planets are not possible with an "M sin i" valu[e\[note 1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-48) greater than 7.5 times the mass of the Earth ([M](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_mass)[E\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_mass), or with a mass greater than 3.1 times the mass of Neptune (much lower than van de Kamp's smallest suggested value).[\[25\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Kurster-25)

In 2013, a research paper was published that further refined planet mass boundaries for the star. Using radial velocity measurements, taken over a period of 25 years, from the [Lick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lick_Observatory) and [Keck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._M._Keck_Observatory) Observatories and applying [Monte Carlo analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_analysis) for both circular and eccentric orbits, upper masses for planets out to 1,000-day orbits were determined. Planets above two Earth masses in orbits of less than 10 days were excluded, and planets of more than ten Earth masses out to a two-year orbit were also confidently ruled out. It was also discovered that the habitable zone of the star seemed to be devoid of roughly Earth-mass planets or larger, save for face-on orbits.[\[48\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-no-earth-dreams-49)[\[49\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Choi2013-50)

Even though this research greatly restricted the possible properties of planets around Barnard's Star, it did not rule them out completely as

[terrestrial planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrestrial_planet) were always going to be difficult to detect. [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA)'s [Space Interferometry Mission,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Interferometry_Mission) which was to begin searching for extrasolar Earth-like planets, was reported to have chosen Barnard's Star as an early search target[,\[29\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Sol Station-29) however the mission was shut down in 2010[.\[50\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-SIM-51) [ESA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Space_Agency)'s similar [Darwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darwin_(ESA)) interferometry mission had the same goal, but was stripped of funding in 2007.[\[51\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-52)

The analysis of radial velocities that eventually led to discovery of the candidate super-Earth orbiting Barnard's Star was also used to set more precise upper mass limits for possible planets, up to and within the habitable zone: a maximum of 0.7 [M](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_mass)[E](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_mass) up to the inner edge and 1.2 ME on the outer edge of the optimistic habitable zone, corresponding to orbital periods of up to 10 and 40 days respectively. Therefore, it appears that Barnard's Star indeed does not host Earth-mass planets or larger, in hot and temperate orbits, unlike other M-dwarf stars that commonly have these types of planets in close-in orbits[.\[19\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Ribas-19)

# Stellar flares

#### 1998

In 1998 a [stellar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_flare) flare on Barnard's Star was detected based on changes in the [spectral emissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_emissions) on 17 July during an unrelated search for variations in the proper motion. Four years passed before the flare was fully analyzed, at which point it was suggested that the flare's temperature was 8,000 K, more than twice the normal temperature of the star.[\[52\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Paulson-53) Given the essentially random nature of flares, Diane Paulson, one of the authors of that study, noted that "the star would be fantastic for amateurs to observe".[\[24\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Flare-24)

The flare was surprising because intense stellar activity is not expected in stars of such age. Flares are not completely understood, but are believed to be caused by strong [magnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field) fields, which suppress [plasma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_(physics)) [convection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convection) and lead to sudden outbursts: strong magnetic fields occur in rapidly rotating stars, while old stars tend to rotate slowly. For Barnard's Star to undergo an event of such magnitude is thus presumed to be a rarity.[\[52\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Paulson-53) Research on the star's periodicity, or changes in stellar activity over a given timescale, also suggest it ought to be quiescent; 1998 research showed weak evidence for periodic variation in the star's brightness, noting only one possible starspot over 130 days.[\[23\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Benedict1998-23)

Stellar activity of this sort has created interest in using Barnard's Star as a proxy to understand similar stars. It is hoped that photometric studies of its [X-ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray) and [UV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UV) emissions will shed light on the large population of old M dwarfs in the galaxy. Such research has [astrobiological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrobiological) implications: given that the habitable zones of M dwarfs are close to the star, any planet located therein would be strongly affected by solar flares, [stellar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_wind) [winds,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_wind) and plasma ejection events.[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Riedel2005-9)

#### 2019

In 2019, two additional ultraviolet [stellar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_flare) flares were detected, each with far-ultraviolet energy of 3×1022 joules, together with one X-ray [stellar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_flare) fl[are](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_flare) with energy 1.6×1022 joules. The flare rate observed to date is enough to cause loss of 87 Earth atmospheres per billion years through thermal processes and ≈3 Earth atmospheres per billion years through ion loss processes on Barnard's Star b.[\[53\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-54)

## Environment

Barnard's Star shares much the same neighborhood as the Sun. The neighbors of Barnard's Star are generally of red dwarf size, the smallest and most common star type. Its closest neighbor is currently the red dwarf [Ross 154,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ross_154) at a distance of 1.66 [parsecs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parsec) (5.41 light-years). The Sun and [Alpha Centauri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_Centauri) are, respectively, the next closest systems[.\[29\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Sol Station-29) From Barnard's Star, the Sun would appear on the diametrically opposite side of the sky at coordinates RA=5h 57m 48.5s , Dec=−04° 41′ 36″, in the westernmost part of the constellation [Monoceros.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monoceros_(constellation)) The absolute magnitude of the Sun is 4.83, and at a distance of 1.834 parsecs, it would be a first-magnitude star, as [Pollux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollux_(star)) is from the Earth[.\[note 2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-56)

## Proposed exploration

Stars closest to the [Sun,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun) including Barnard's Star (25 April 2014)[\[54\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-NASA-20140425a-55)

#### Project Daedalus

Barnard's Star was studied as part of [Project Daedalus.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Daedalus) Undertaken between 1973 and 1978, the study suggested that rapid, uncrewed travel to another star system was possible with existing or near-future technology[.\[55\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Daedalus76-57) Barnard's Star was chosen as a target partly because it was believed to have planets.[\[56\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Darling Daedalus-58)

The theoretical model suggested that a nuclear pulse rocket employing [nuclear fusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fusion) (specifically, electron bombardment of [deuterium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deuterium) and [helium-3\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helium-3) and accelerating for four years could achieve a velocity of 12% of the [speed of light.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light) The star could then be reached in 50 years, within a human lifetime[.\[56\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Darling Daedalus-58) Along with detailed investigation of the star and any companions, the [interstellar medium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstellar_medium) would be examined and baseline astrometric readings performed.[\[55\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Daedalus76-57)

The initial Project Daedalus model sparked further theoretical research. In 1980, [Robert Freitas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Freitas) suggested a more ambitious plan: a [self-replicating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-replicating_spacecraft) [spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-replicating_spacecraft) intended to search for and make contact with [extraterrestrial life.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extraterrestrial_life)[\[57\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Repro-59) Built and launched in [Jupiter'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter)s orbit, it would reach Barnard's Star in 47 years under parameters similar to those of the original Project Daedalus. Once at the star, it would begin automated self-replication, constructing a factory, initially to manufacture exploratory probes and eventually to create a copy of the original spacecraft after 1,000 years.[\[57\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard%27s_Star#cite_note-Repro-59)
