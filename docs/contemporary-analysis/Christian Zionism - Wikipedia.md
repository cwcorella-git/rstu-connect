**Christian Zionism** is a political and religious ideology that, in a [Christian context,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_and_Judaism) espouses the return of the [Jewish people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews) to the [Holy Land.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Land) [\[1\]](#page-33-0) Likewise, it holds that the founding of the State of Israel in 1948 was in accordance with [biblical prophecies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible_prophecy) transmitted through the [Old Testament:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Testament) that the reestablishment of [Jewish sovereignty in the Levant—](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_and_Judaism_in_the_Land_of_Israel)the eschatological ["Gathering of Israel"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gathering_of_Israel)—is a prerequisite for the [Second Coming of Jesus Christ.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Coming) [\[1\]](#page-33-0)[\[2\]](#page-33-1)[\[3\]](#page-34-0) The term began to be used in the mid-20th century, in place of **Christian restorationism**, as proponents of the ideology rallied behind [Zionists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zionism) in support of a [Jewish national homeland.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeland_for_the_Jewish_people) [\[1\]](#page-33-0)[\[4\]](#page-34-1)[\[5\]](#page-34-2)

An expectation of Jewish restoration among Christians is rooted in 17th-century English [Puritan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puritan) thought[.\[6\]](#page-35-0)[\[2\]](#page-33-1) Contemporary Israeli historian [Anita Shapira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anita_Shapira) suggests that England's Zionist [Evangelical Protestants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelicalism) "passed this notion on to Jewish circles" around the 1840s[,\[7\]](#page-35-1) while Jewish nationalism in the early 19th century was largely met with hostility from [British Jews.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Jews) [\[8\]](#page-35-2)

Christian pro-Zionist ideals emerged among the [Puritans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puritans) in the 16th and 17th centuries[.\[6\]](#page-35-0) While supporting a mass Jewish return to the [Land of Israel,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_of_Israel) Christian Zionism asserts a parallel idea that the returnees ought to be encouraged to [reject](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversion_of_the_Jews_(future_event)) [Judaism and adopt Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversion_of_the_Jews_(future_event)) as a means of fulfilling [biblical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible_prophecy) [prophecies.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible_prophecy) [\[1\]](#page-33-0)[\[9\]](#page-35-3)[\[10\]](#page-35-4) Polling and academic research have suggested a trend of widespread distrust among Jews towards the motives of Evangelical Protestants, who have been promoting support for the [State of Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_of_Israel) and [evangelizing the](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proselytization_and_counter-proselytization_of_Jews) [Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proselytization_and_counter-proselytization_of_Jews) at the same time[.\[1\]](#page-33-0)[\[11\]](#page-35-5)

# **History before the 20th century**

### **Origins in Calvinistic millennialism**

[Thomas Brightman,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Brightman) an English Puritan, published Shall They Return to Jerusalem Again? in 1615. This was one of the earliest [Restorationist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restoration_Movement) works.

The first wave of Protestant leaders, including [Martin Luther](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Luther) and [John Calvin,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Calvin) did not mention any special [eschatological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_eschatology) views which included a return of the Jews to Palestine (converted to Christianity or otherwise)[.\[12\]](#page-36-0) More generally, Luther had hoped that the Jews would convert to his brand of Christianity once he had broken with the [Catholic Church,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church) but later [he harshly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Jews_and_Their_Lies) [denounced Jews.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Jews_and_Their_Lies) Like the Catholic Church and the [Eastern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church) [Orthodox Church,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church) the [Lutheran Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lutheranism) and the [Reformed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvinism) [Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvinism) saw the Christian Church as being the "spiritual Israel" and considered faithful Christians the exclusive ["people of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People_of_God) [God"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People_of_God)—those with whom God had entered into a covenant through [Jesus Christ—](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus_Christ)assigning no special privileges or role to persons of Jewish descent. The Puritans, once a "fringe" faction, came to power under [Oliver Cromwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Cromwell) during the Commonwealth. Several of his closest advisors held Philo-Semitic millennialist religious views.

During the late Tudor and early Stuart period, these Puritans remained outsiders in England and bitterly opposed the [Laudian-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laudian)dominated Anglican Church (though the Presbyterians, who held very similar views, had established the [Church of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Scotland) [Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Scotland) as the largest ["Kirk"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirk) in Scotland). With the [English Civil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Civil_War) [War,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Civil_War) the Puritans filled the ranks of the [Parliamentarians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roundheads) and the [New Model Army.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Model_Army) Under the leadership of [Oliver Cromwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Cromwell) they were victorious, executed [Charles I of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_I_of_England) and gained complete state power, establishing the [Commonwealth of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_of_England) [England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_of_England) between 1649 and 1660[.\[18\]](#page-36-6) The [Philo-Semitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philo-Semitic) [millennialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millennialist) undercurrent came to have a direct influence on politics. A number of Cromwell's close advisors, such as [John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dury) [Dury,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dury) [John Sadler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Sadler_(Town_Clerk_of_London)) and [Hugh Peter,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Peter) came into contact with [Dutch-based Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_the_Netherlands) such as [Menasseh ben Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menasseh_ben_Israel) and advocated [Jewish resettlement in England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resettlement_of_the_Jews_in_England) (they had been banned from the country since the 13th century). Sadler, Cromwell's secretary, even argued that the British were one of the [Lost Tribes of Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_Tribes_of_Israel) in his pamphlet The Rights of the Kingdom (1649) and thus kindred to the Jews, initiating [British Israelism.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Israelism) Other Puritans such as [Jeremiah Burroughs,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremiah_Burroughs) [Peter Bulkley,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Bulkley) [John Fenwicke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Fenwicke) and [John Cotton,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Cotton_(minister)) [\[19\]](#page-36-7) some of whom lived in the [Massachusetts Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Bay_Colony) [Colony,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Bay_Colony) saw Jewish re-entry to England as a step on the path to their eventual return to Palestine (all tied up within a millennialist eschatology, which would hasten the [Second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Coming) [Coming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Coming) of Jesus Christ and thus the [final judgement\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Final_judgement)[.\[20\]](#page-36-8) Johanna and Ebenezer Cartwright, two Baptists who had spent time in [Amsterdam,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amsterdam) held the same view and issued the original petition to [Thomas Fairfax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Fairfax)'s Council of War in January 1649 for Jewish readmission[:\[21\]](#page-37-0) the petition hoped, "That this Nation of England, with the inhabitants of the Netherlands, shall be the first and the readiest to transport Israel's sons and daughters on their ships to the land promised to their forefathers, Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob for an everlasting inheritance."[\[18\]](#page-36-6) Their de facto toleration in England was informally achieved by 1655 to 1656 and was not rolled back after [the Restoration.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuart_Restoration)

A prominent French-born figure [Isaac La Peyrère,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_La_Peyr%C3%A8re) who was nominally a [Huguenot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huguenot) Calvinist, but came from a Portuguese [New Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Christian) (converted [Sephardic Jewish\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sephardic_Jewish) family was also a significant 17th century progenitor, with influence on both sides of the [English Channel.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Channel) [\[22\]](#page-37-1)[\[18\]](#page-36-6) La Peyrère in his millennialist work Du rappel des juifs (1643) wrote about a Jewish return to Palestine, predicted the [building of the Third Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Temple) and [Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem) playing the most powerful role in world governance: all working towards the Second Coming[.\[18\]](#page-36-6) La Peyrère closely followed the developments of Oliver Cromwell's Dissenter regime and dreamed of overthrowing [Louis XIV of France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_XIV_of_France) and replacing him with the [Prince of Condé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis,_Grand_Cond%C3%A9) (who he worked for as a secretary) as part of a millennialist proto-Zionist messianic project[.\[23\]](#page-37-2) After the publication of La Peyrère's book the

### **Pietism, Evangelicalism, and British foreign policy**

With the rise of the [Hanoverians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Hanover) to power in Britain and the ascent of the Enlightenment, much of the 18th century mainstream elite adopted [Philhellenism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philhellenism) looking back to the culture and philosophies of the [classical world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_world) for inspiration for the [Georgian age,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_architecture) rather than entertaining millennialist fantasies based on the Hebrew Old Testament (though Jews themselves enjoyed significant toleration in the [British Empire\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire). Although marginal at first, a religious underground was slowly growing from the 1730s which would eventually spout a second wave of Protestant Zionism and with it the birth of [Evangelical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelical_Protestantism) [Protestantism.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelical_Protestantism) This was precipitated in Germany by [Philipp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philipp_Spener)

[Spener](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philipp_Spener)'s [Pietism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pietism) a mystical and often millennialist take on Lutheranism, which prophesied the "conversion of the Jews and the fall of the [Papacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papacy) as the prelude of the triumph of the Church." One of Spener's followers, [Nicolaus Zinzendorf,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaus_Zinzendorf) spread this into the [Moravian Church,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moravian_Church) linking the theory to Palestine, changing the Moravian liturgy to include a prayer "to restore the tribe of Judah in its time and bless its first fruits among us."[\[18\]](#page-36-6) [John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wesley) and [Charles Wesley,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Wesley) early leaders in [Methodism;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodism) inspired by the Pietists and Zinzendorf's Moravians; also promoted a Jewish return to Palestine with Charles Wesley even authoring a hymn dedicated to it[.\[18\]](#page-36-6)[\[31\]](#page-38-0) The Baptist, [John Gill,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Gill_(theologian)) who moved in similar circles to the Wesleys, authored works expressing similar views[.\[32\]](#page-38-1) By 1771, the Evangelical minister, [John Eyre,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Eyre_(evangelical_minister)) founder of the [Evangelical Magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelical_Magazine) and among the original members of the [London Missionary Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Missionary_Society) was promoting a more developed version of these views with his Observations upon Prophecies Relating to the Restoration of the Jews. By the end of the 18th century, in the aftermath of the [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution) [Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution) and the [National Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Assembly_(French_Revolution)) decreeing in December 1789 that non-Catholics were eligible for all civil and military positions, the Revolutionary government in France made a [play](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon_and_the_Jews) [for the allegiance of Jews,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon_and_the_Jews) in competition with Britain. During the [Egypt–Syria campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_campaign_in_Egypt_and_Syria) of the [French Revolutionary Wars,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolutionary_Wars) [Bonaparte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon) invited "all the Jews of Asia and Africa to gather under his flag in order to re-establish the ancient Jerusalem."[\[33\]](#page-38-2) Although Bonaparte himself was secular and the idea an early example of pragmatic [Political Zionism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_Zionism) the Jacobin idea itself may have originated from [Thomas Corbet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corbet_family) (1773–1804), an [Anglo-Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Irish_people) Protestant émigrée who, as a member of the liberalrepublican [Society of United Irishmen,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_of_United_Irishmen) was an ally of the [Jacobin-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobin)government, engaged in revolutionary activities against the British and served in the French Army[.\[34\]](#page-38-3)[\[35\]](#page-38-4) In February 1790, he authored a letter to the [French Directory,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Directory) then under the leadership of Napoleon's patron [Paul Barras.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Barras) [\[34\]](#page-38-3) In the letter he stated "I recommend you, Napoleon, to call on the Jewish people to join your conquest in the East, to your mission to conquer the land of Israel" saying, "Their riches do not console them for their hardships. They await with impatience the epoch of their re-establishment as a nation.["\[35\]](#page-38-4) Dr. Milka Levy-Rubin, a curator at the National Library of Israel, has attributed Corbet's motivation to a Protestant Zionism based on premillennialist themes[.\[34\]](#page-38-3)

In New England during the 18th century, [Ezra Stiles,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ezra_Stiles) president of [Yale College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University) was a supporter of Jewish restoration and befriended Rabbi [Raphael Chaim Yitzchak Karigal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raphael_Hayyim_Isaac_Carregal) of [Hebron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebron) in 1773 during his visit[.\[36\]](#page-38-5) [Jonathan Edwards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Edwards_(theologian)) also anticipated a future return of Jews to their homeland[.\[37\]](#page-38-6) In 1808, Asa Mc Farland, a Presbyterian, voiced the opinion of many that the fall of the Ottoman Empire was imminent and would bring about Jewish restoration. One David Austin of [New Haven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Haven,_Connecticut) spent his fortune building docks and inns from which the Jews could embark to the Holy Land. In 1825, [Mordecai Manuel Noah,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mordecai_Manuel_Noah) a Jew who wanted to found a national home for the Jews on [Grand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Island,_New_York) [Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Island,_New_York) in New York as a way station on the way to the Holy Land, won widespread Christian backing for his project. Likewise, restorationist theology was among the inspirations for the first American missionary activity in the Middle East[\[38\]](#page-38-7) and for mapping the Holy Land[.\[39\]](#page-38-8)

Most early-19th-century British Restorationists, like [Charles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Simeon) [Simeon,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Simeon) were [postmillennial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postmillennialism) in eschatology[.\[9\]](#page-35-3) With the rise of [James Frere,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Hatley_Frere) [James Haldane Stewart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Haldane_Stewart) and [Edward Irving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Irving) a major shift in the 1820s towards [premillennialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premillennialism) occurred, with a similar focus on advocacy for the restoration of the Jews to Israel[.\[9\]](#page-35-3)[\[40\]](#page-39-0) As the demise of the Ottoman Empire appeared to be approaching, the advocacy of restorationism increased. At the same time, the visit of [John Nelson Darby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Nelson_Darby) to the United States catalyzed a new movement. Darby was the founder of a theological framework known as [dispensationalism.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dispensationalism) This was expressed at the [Niagara Bible Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niagara_Bible_Conference) in 1878, which issued a 14-point proclamation (relying on Luke 12:35–40, 17:26–30, 18:8 Acts 15:14–17, 2 Thessalonians 2:3–8, 2 Timothy 3:1–5, and Titus 1:11–15), including:

that the Lord Jesus will come in person to introduce the millennial age, when Israel shall be restored to their own land, and the earth shall be full of the knowledge of the Lord; and that this personal and premillennial advent is the blessed hope set before us in the Gospel for which we should be constantly looking[.\[41\]](#page-39-1)

The dispensationalist theology of John Nelson Darby is often claimed to be a significant awakener of American Christian Zionism[.\[42\]](#page-39-2) He first distinguished the hopes of the Jews and that of the church and gentiles in a series of 11 evening lectures in Geneva in 1840. His lectures were immediately published in French (L'Attente Actuelle de l'Eglise), English (1841), German and Dutch (1847) and so his teachings began their global journey. Some dispensationalists, like Arno Gabelein, whilst [philo-semitic,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philo-Semitism) opposed Zionism as a movement born in selfconfidence and unbelief[.\[3\]](#page-34-0) While dispensationalism had considerable influence through the [Scofield Reference Bible,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scofield_Reference_Bible) Christian lobbying for the restoration of the Jews preceded the publication of the Scofield Reference Bible (first published by OUP, 1909) by over a century[,\[9\]](#page-35-3) and many Christian Zionists and Christian Zionist organizations such as the [International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Christian_Embassy_Jerusalem) [Christian Embassy Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Christian_Embassy_Jerusalem) do not subscribe to [dispensationalism.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dispensationalism) Many non-dispensationalist Protestants were also strong advocates of a Jewish return to their homeland, [Charles Spurgeon,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Spurgeon) [\[43\]](#page-39-3) both [Horatius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horatius_Bonar)[\[44\]](#page-39-4) and [Andrew Bonar,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Bonar) [Robert Murray M'Chyene,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Murray_M%27Cheyne) [\[45\]](#page-39-5) and [J. C. Ryle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._C._Ryle)[\[46\]](#page-39-6) were among a number of proponents of both the importance and significance of a Jewish return to Israel. However Spurgeon averred of dispensationalism: "It is a mercy that these absurdities are revealed one at a time, in order that we may be able to endure their stupidity without dying of amazement"[.\[47\]](#page-40-0) In 1864,

# Spurgeon wrote[:\[43\]](#page-39-3)

We look forward, then, for these two things. I am not going to theorize upon which of them will come first — whether they shall be restored first, and converted afterwards — or converted first and then restored. They are to be restored and they are to be converted, too.

The crumbling of the Ottoman Empire threatened the British route to India via the [Suez Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suez_Canal) as well as sundry French, German and American economic interests. In 1831 the Ottomans were driven from the [region of Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria_(region)) (including Palestine) by an expansionist Egypt, in the [First Turko-Egyptian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Turko-Egyptian_War) [War.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Turko-Egyptian_War) Although Britain forced [Muhammad Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Ali_of_Egypt) to withdraw to Egypt, [the Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Levant) was left for a brief time without a government. The ongoing weakness of the Ottoman Empire made some in the west consider the potential of a Jewish state in the Holy Land. A number of important figures within the British government advocated such a plan, including [Charles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Henry_Churchill) [Henry Churchill.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Henry_Churchill) [\[48\]](#page-40-1)[\[49\]](#page-40-2) Again during the lead-up to the [Crimean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_War) [War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_War) (1854), there was an opportunity for political rearrangements in the Near East. In July 1853, [Anthony Ashley-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Ashley-Cooper,_7th_Earl_of_Shaftesbury)[Cooper, 7th Earl of Shaftesbury,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Ashley-Cooper,_7th_Earl_of_Shaftesbury) who was President of the [London Society for Promoting Christianity Amongst the Jews,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church%27s_Ministry_Among_Jewish_People) wrote to [Prime Minister Aberdeen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Hamilton-Gordon,_4th_Earl_of_Aberdeen) urging Jewish restoration as a means of stabilizing the region[.\[9\]](#page-35-3)[\[50\]](#page-40-3)[\[51\]](#page-40-4)

Late-19th-century non-messianic restorationism was largely driven by concern over the fate of the Jews of the Russian Empire, beset by poverty and by deadly, government-inspired pogroms. It was widely accepted that western nations did not wish to receive Jewish immigrants. Restorationism was a way for charitable individuals to assist oppressed Jews without actually accepting them as neighbors and fellow-citizens.[\[52\]](#page-40-5)[\[53\]](#page-40-6) [\[54\]](#page-40-7) In this, Restorationism was not unlike the efforts of the [American Colonization Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Colonization_Society) to send blacks to [Liberia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberia) and the efforts of British abolitionists to create [Sierra Leone.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Leone) [[citation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed) [needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)] [Winston Churchill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winston_Churchill) endorsed Restoration because he recognized that Jews fleeing Russian pogroms required a refuge, and preferred Palestine for sentimental reasons.[\[55\]](#page-40-8)

In 1818, President [John Adams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Adams) wrote, "I really wish the Jews again in Judea an independent nation", and believed that they would gradually become [Unitarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitarianism) Christians[.\[56\]](#page-40-9)

The tycoon [William Eugene Blackstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Eugene_Blackstone) was inspired by the conference to publish the book Jesus is Coming, which took up the restorationist cause. #### **In Great Britain**

## ![](_page_15_Picture_4.jpeg)

By the time of [Mandate Palestine,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandate_Palestine) the British struggled to balance sympathies for Jews and Arabs. Some, such as [Orde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orde_Wingate) [Wingate,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orde_Wingate) fought alongside the [Haganah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haganah) as part of the [Special](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Night_Squads) [Night Squads.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Night_Squads)

Ideas favoring the restoration of the Jews in Palestine or the Land of Israel entered the [British](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire) public discourse in the 1830s, though British Reformationists had written about the restoration of the Jews as early as the 16th century, and the idea had strong support among Puritans.[[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)] Not all such attitudes were favorable towards the Jews; they were shaped in part by a variety of [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant) beliefs[,\[9\]](#page-35-3)[\[60\]](#page-41-3) or by a streak of [philo-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philo-Semitism)[Semitism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philo-Semitism) among the classically educated British elite.[\[61\]](#page-41-4)

At the urging of [Lord Shaftesbury,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Ashley-Cooper,_7th_Earl_of_Shaftesbury) Britain established a consulate in [Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem_in_Christianity) in 1838, the first diplomatic appointment to Palestine[.\[9\]](#page-35-3)

In 1839, the [Church of Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Scotland) sent [Andrew Bonar,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Bonar) [Robert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Murray_M%27Cheyne) [Murray M'Cheyne,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Murray_M%27Cheyne) Alexander Black and [Alexander Keith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Keith_(Free_Church_minister)) on a mission to report on the condition of the Jews in Palestine. Their report was widely published[.\[62\]](#page-41-5) They traveled through France, Greece, and Egypt, and from Egypt, overland to [Gaza.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaza_City) On the way home they visited [Syria,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria) the [Austrian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_Empire) and some of the German states. They sought out Jewish communities and inquired about their readiness to accept Christ, and separately, their preparedness to return to Israel as prophesied in the Bible. [Alexander Keith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Keith_(Free_Church_minister)) recounted the journey in his 1844 book The Land of Israel According to the Covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob. It was also in that book that Keith used the slogan that became popular with other Christian Restorationists, [a land without a people for a people without a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_land_without_a_people_for_a_people_without_a_land)

[land.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_land_without_a_people_for_a_people_without_a_land) In 1844 he revisited Palestine with his son, [George Skene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Skene_Keith_(physician)) [Keith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Skene_Keith_(physician)) (1819–1910), who was the first person to photograph the land[.\[63\]](#page-41-6)

An important, though often neglected, figure in British support of the restoration of the Jews was [William Hechler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Hechler) (1845–1931), an English clergyman of German descent who was Chaplain of the British Embassy in [Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna) and became a close friend of [Theodor Herzl.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_Herzl) [\[64\]](#page-42-0) Hechler was instrumental in aiding Herzl through his diplomatic activities, and may, in that sense, be called the founder of modern Christian Zionism. When it came to mark the twenty-fifth anniversary of Herzl's death, the editors of the English-language memorial volume said that William Hechler was "not only the first, but the most constant and the most indefatigable of Herzl's followers".[\[58\]](#page-41-1)

#### **Balfour Declaration**

On 2 November 1917, UK Home Secretary [Arthur Balfour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Balfour) sent a letter to [Lord Walter Rothschild.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Rothschild,_2nd_Baron_Rothschild) This letter, known as the [Balfour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balfour_Declaration) [Declaration,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balfour_Declaration) famously stated that "His Majesty's Government view with favour the establishment in Palestine of a national home for the Jewish people." As noted by Philip Alexander, "A crucial ingredient in Balfour's Zionism [may have been] his Christian belief or, to put it a little more subtly, his Christian formation. The most persuasive advocate of this thesis is the Canadian historian Donald Lewis in his 2010 monograph, The Origins of Christian Zionism, but it has been espoused by a number of other scholars as well.["\[65\]](#page-42-1)[\[66\]](#page-42-2)

# **Between World War I and the 1948 Palestine War**

#### **In the United States**

In the decades leading up to the [1948 Palestine war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1948_Palestine_war) and [establishment of the State of Israel in 1948,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israeli_Declaration_of_Independence) [\[67\]](#page-42-3) the most prominent and politically active American Christian supporters of Zionism were liberal and mainline Protestants whose support for the movement was often unrelated to their interpretation of the Bible[.\[68\]](#page-43-0) These Christian supporters of Zionism viewed Palestine as a needed safe haven for Jews who were fleeing from intensifying persecution in Europe and they frequently believed that their support of the movement was part of a broader effort at [interfaith rapprochement.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian%E2%80%93Jewish_reconciliation) The Pro-Palestine Federation, a Christian pro-Zionist organization which was founded in 1930, called for the promotion of "goodwill and esteem between Jews and non-Jews" and it also called for the British government to adhere to the terms of its Mandate for Palestine, which pledged support for the establishment of a Jewish national home[.\[69\]](#page-43-1)

Amidst World War II and their growing awareness of [the](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Holocaust) [Holocaust,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Holocaust) American Jewish Zionists helped coordinate the establishment of two non-Jewish Zionist organizations, the American Palestine Committee and the Christian Council on Palestine, which were later merged into the American Christian Palestine Committee (ACPC). The ACPC, which was composed largely of liberal and mainline Protestants, became the leading American Christian lobby in support of the creation of a Jewish state in Palestine[.\[70\]](#page-43-2) After the establishment of Israel in 1948, the ACPC continued its lobbying efforts. For instance, it coordinated opposition to the United Nations' efforts to internationalize the city of Jerusalem, which was divided between Israel and Transjordan in the 1948 War.

During these years, [dispensational premillennialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premillennialism#Dispensational_school) grew in popularity among conservative American Protestants. Many [dispensationalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dispensationalism) viewed the Zionist movement as at least a partial fulfillment of biblical prophecy or they viewed it as a modern fulfillment of God's covenantal promises to the Jewish people. In the 1930s, [Southern Baptist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Baptist_Convention) missionary Jacob Gartenhaus, himself a convert from Judaism, argued that "Zionism is going to win whether anybody likes it or not... To oppose it is to oppose God's plan.["\[71\]](#page-43-3) But for the most part, such beliefs did not translate into political action on behalf of the movement in this era. One slight exception was J. Frank Norris, a [fundamentalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_fundamentalism) Baptist who split time between pulpits in Fort Worth, Texas, and Detroit, Michigan[.\[72\]](#page-44-0) While Norris did not organize lobbying efforts in the way that the ACPC did, he did preach to his followers that it was their Christian duty to support the Zionist cause and wrote President Truman in support of Zionist claims to Palestine in 1947 and 1948. Norris also loosely coordinated with the ACPC, at times publishing their materials in his periodical, The Fundamentalist. [\[73\]](#page-44-1)

# **After the founding of the State of Israel**

### **In the United States**

In the decades since the establishment of Israel, and especially since the 1967 [Six-Day War,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six-Day_War) the most prominent American Christian supporters of Israel have come from the [evangelical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelicalism) wing of [American Protestantism.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism_in_the_United_States) [American evangelicalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_evangelicalism) itself underwent significant changes in the years surrounding Israel's birth, as a "new" evangelicalism led by figures like [Billy Graham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billy_Graham) emerged from [Protestantism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism) and came to cultural prominence. [\[75\]](#page-44-3) It was among these new evangelicals that the contemporary movement that most commonly associated with the term "Christian Zionism" originated[.\[76\]](#page-44-4)

Many new evangelicals adhered to dispensationalism or at least, they adhered to beliefs which were inspired by it—most especially, they adhered to the dispensationalist understanding that Jews remained in a special covenantal relationship with God. Most important to the development of Christian Zionism as a movement, though, was the fact that American evangelical leaders began to build relationships with American and Israeli Jews and they also began to build institutional connections with Jewish organizations and the Israeli government itself. Crucial to the building of these relationships was a motivated coterie of American evangelicals who resided in Israel, most notably, the founder of the American Institute of Holy Land Studies, G. Douglas Young. Through his institute, Young worked to convince American Christians that it was their biblical duty to support the

Jewish people and the Jewish state. He also worked as a gobetween for Jewish organizations and Israeli government agencies which were seeking to build relationships with American evangelicals[.\[77\]](#page-44-5) Such activism provided the basis for the development of Christian Zionism as a movement.

# **Biblical interpretations**

Some Christian Zionists interpret the prophetic texts as describing inevitable future events, and these events primarily involve Israel (taken to mean the descendants of the Biblical patriarch [Jacob\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob) or Judah (taken to mean the remaining faithful adherents of Judaism). These prophecies are seen as requiring the presence of a Jewish state in the [Holy Land,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Land) the central part of the lands promised to the Biblical patriarch Abraham in the [Covenant of the pieces.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covenant_of_the_pieces) This requirement is sometimes interpreted as being fulfilled by the contemporary state of Israel[.\[110\]](#page-48-9)

### **Other doctrines**

Christian schools of doctrine which consider other teachings to counterbalance these doctrines, or which interpret them in terms of distinct eschatological theories, are less conducive to Christian Zionism. Among the many texts which address this subject in counterbalance are the words of Jesus, as for example in [Matthew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_Matthew) [21:43,](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Bible_(King_James)/Matthew#21:43) "the kingdom of God will be taken away from you and given to a nation producing the fruits of it".

In Defending Christian Zionism, [David Pawson,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Pawson) a Christian Zionist in the [United Kingdom,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom) puts forward the case that the return of the Jews to the Holy Land is a fulfilment of scriptural prophecy, and that Christians should support the existence of the Jewish State (although not unconditionally its actions) on theological grounds. He also argues that prophecies spoken about Israel relate specifically to Israel (not to the church, as in "replacement theology"). However, he criticises Dispensationalism, which he says is a largely American movement holding similar views. Pawson was spurred to write this book by the work of [Stephen Sizer,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Sizer) an evangelical Christian who rejects Christian Zionism.
