## RCV banned state-wide

**Ranked-choice voting** (**RCV**) can refer to one of several [ranked voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranked_voting) methods used in some cities and states in the United States. The term is not strictly defined, but most often refers to [instant-runoff voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instant-runoff_voting) (IRV) or [single transferable vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_transferable_vote) (STV), the main difference being whether only one winner or multiple winners are elected. At the federal and state level, [instant runoff voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instant_runoff_voting) is used for congressional and presidential elections in [Maine;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maine) state, congressional, and presidential general elections in [Alaska;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaska) and [special](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_election) congressional elections in [Hawaii.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaii) Starting in 2025, it will also be used for all elections in the [District of Columbia.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/District_of_Columbia)

As of February 2024, RCV is used for local elections in 45 US cities including Salt Lake City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_Lake_City) and [Seattle.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seattle) [\[2\] It has also been used by some state political parties in party-run primaries and nominating conventions.\[3\]\[4\]\[5\] As a contingency in the case of a runoff election,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runoff_election) ranked ballots are used by overseas voters in six states[.\[2\]

Since 2020, voters in seven states have rejected [ballot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_initiative) [initiatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_initiative) that would have implemented, or allowed legislatures to implement, ranked choice voting. Ranked choice voting has also been banned in eleven states.

Most elections in the United States use the [first past the post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_past_the_post) system, often with [primary elections.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_elections) Other systems that have been used entailed [ranked votes.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranked_voting) IRV, STV and [Contingent vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contingent_vote) (AKA supplementary voting) use secondary rankings on ranked votes as contingency votes; [Nanson's method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanson%27s_method) and [Bucklin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bucklin_voting) [voting,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bucklin_voting) which have also been used, consider secondary rankings as pertinent alongside first preferences..

Between 1912 and 1930, supplementary voting,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supplementary_voting_system) typically with only two rankings and only two rounds of voting, were implemented. They were later repealed[.\[6\]

Proportional representation by means of the single-

transferable vote (PR-STV) was used in about 24 U. S. cities prior to 1940. PR-STV was first used in North America in Ashtabula, Ohio, in 1915. PR-STV was also used for the election of the nine-member city council of Cincinnati, Ohio,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cincinnati,_Ohio) from 1924 to 1957, and in [Cleveland, Ohio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleveland,_Ohio) and [Sacramento, California.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacramento,_California) [New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City) adopted STV in 1936 as a method for breaking the corrupt [political machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_machine) of [Tammany Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tammany_Hall) dominating the city and used it for five elections from 1937 to 1945.[\[7\] [Cambridge, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge,_Massachusetts) started using proportional representation by means of the [single transferable vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_transferable_vote) in 1941 for its city council and school committee elections, and have contined to use it until the present.

Starting in the early 2000s, instant-runoff voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instant-runoff_voting) was adopted in the [San Francisco Bay Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_Bay_Area) and throughout [Minnesota.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minnesota) [\[8\] The city of Minneapolis, Minnesota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minneapolis) adopted the usage of PR-STV for its Board of Estimate and Taxation and a combination of instant-runoff voting for district elections and PR-STV for atlarge elections of its [Park and Recreation Board.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minneapolis_Park_and_Recreation_Board) [\[9\]

Instant-runoff voting was also adopted in the cities of Aspen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspen,_Colorado) and [Burlington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burlington,_Vermont) during the early 2000s. They were repealed within a decade of their adoption[.\[10\]\[11\] Burlington re-adopted instant-runoff voting in 2021.

Both Portland, Maine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portland,_Maine) and [Portland, Oregon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portland,_Oregon) adopted STV around 2022. The Oregon city's implementation only applied to its new city council system, whose members are to be elected in three-seat wards, whereas Portland, Maine's implementation applies to all of the city's multi-seat bodies where elected in multi-winner contests. (However in the 2025 election no members are to be elected in multi-member contests.[\)\[12\] [[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)]

The proposed Fair Representation Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fair_Representation_Act_(United_States)) would require multimember districts for elections to the [US House of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_House_of_Representatives) [Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_House_of_Representatives) which would then be elected by STV. States with only one representative would instead have elections by [instant runoff voting.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instant-runoff_voting) [\[13\]\[14\]

## **Maine, 2018–present**

[\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ranked-choice_voting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=3)

### **State and congressional elections**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ranked-choice_voting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=4)

In 2018, Maine began using [instant runoff voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instant-runoff_voting) for primary and general elections for the U. S. Senate and House, and for primary elections for governor and the state legislature. Maine was the first state to use instant runoff voting for all these elections.

In 2016, Maine voters approved Maine Question 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maine_Question_5,_2016) with 52% of the vote, approving instant runoff voting for primary and general elections for governor, U. S. Senate, U. S. House and the state legislature, starting in 2018[.\[15\] However, in May 2017, the Maine Supreme Judicial Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maine_Supreme_Judicial_Court) stated that instant runoff voting can be used only for federal offices and primary elections for state offices because the [state constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Maine) specifies that a plurality suffices to win general elections for state offices[.\[16\]

In October 2017, the state legislature voted to delay implementation of Question 5 until 2021, at which time the entire Question would be considered repealed unless a constitutional amendment had been passed permitting instant runoff voting for general elections for state offices.\[17\] Maine voters then collected enough signatures to put a Question on the June 2018 ballot to veto the October 2017 law. The people's veto, Question 1,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maine_Question_1,_June_2018) passed in the June 2018 election. This election also initiated the use of instant runoff voting for state and federal primaries because the presence of Question 1 on the ballot suspended the October 2017 law[.\[18\]

In the 2018 United States House of Representatives elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_United_States_House_of_Representatives_elections_in_Maine) [in Maine,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_United_States_House_of_Representatives_elections_in_Maine) though [Republican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republican_Party_(United_States)) incumbent [Bruce Poliquin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_Poliquin) led by 2,171 votes in the first round of vote tabulation in the [2nd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maine%27s_2nd_congressional_district) [Congressional District,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maine%27s_2nd_congressional_district) he did not have a majority of the votes, initiating the instant runoff tabulation process. Poliquin filed a lawsuit in [federal court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_District_of_Maine) on November 13, seeking an order to halt the second-round tabulation of ballots and declare RCV unconstitutional, but his request for an injunction to halt the counting was denied[.\[19\]\[20\] On November 15, the Maine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maine_Secretary_of_State) [Secretary of State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maine_Secretary_of_State) announced [Democratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Party_(United_States)) candidate [Jared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jared_Golden) [Golden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jared_Golden) as the winner by 3,509 votes, after votes for [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_(politician)) candidates Tiffany Bond and Will Hoar were eliminated and ballots with these votes had their second- or third-choice votes counted[.\[21\]

Poliquin requested a recount of the ballots just before the deadline of November 26.\[22\] On December 14, with almost half of the votes recounted and with the result not being significantly changed, Poliquin ended the recount after incurring \$15,000 in fees.\[23\]\[24\] Poliquin continued his lawsuit\[25\] and asked the judge, Lance Walker,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lance_E._Walker) to order a new election be held should he decline to hold instant runoff unconstitutional[.\[26\] Judge Walker ruled against Poliquin on December 13, rejecting all of his arguments.\[27\]

Poliquin appealed to the Court of Appeals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U. S._Court_of_Appeals_for_the_First_Circuit) in [Boston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston) and requested an order to prevent Golden from being certified as the winner, but that request was also rejected[.\[24\] On December 24, Poliquin dropped his lawsuit, allowing Golden to take the seat.\[28\]\[29\]\[30\]\[31\]

Instant runoff voting was retained for the 2020 [U. S. Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_United_States_Senate_election_in_Maine) and [U. S. House elections.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_United_States_House_of_Representatives_elections_in_Maine)

#### **Presidential elections**

### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ranked-choice_voting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=5)

On August 26, 2019, the Maine Legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maine_Legislature) passed a bill adopting instant runoff voting for both presidential primaries and the general election[.\[32\]\[33\] On September 6, 2019, Governor Janet Mills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janet_Mills) allowed the bill to become law without her signature, which delayed it from taking effect until after the [2020 presidential primaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_United_States_presidential_election_in_Maine#Primary_elections) in March. It was used in the general election, making Maine the first state to use instant runoff for a presidential general election[.\[34\]

In June 2020, the Maine Republican Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maine_Republican_Party) filed signatures for a [veto referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veto_referendum) to ask voters if they want the law repealed and preclude the use of instant runoff for the 2020 election. [Matthew Dunlap,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_Dunlap) [Maine's secretary of state,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_of_State_of_Maine) rejected a number of signatures that had not been collected by a registered voter as required under [the state constitution,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Maine) resulting in there being insufficient signatures for the veto referendum to qualify for the ballot. A challenge to Dunlap's decision in [Maine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maine_Superior_Court) [Superior Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maine_Superior_Court) was successful for the Maine Republican Party, but the case was appealed to the [Maine Supreme Judicial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maine_Supreme_Judicial_Court) [Court.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maine_Supreme_Judicial_Court) [\[35\]\[36\]\[37\] On September 8, the court issued a stay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stay_of_proceedings) of the Superior Court ruling pending appeal on the merits, causing confusion and uncertainty regarding the 2020 election[.\[38\] Nevertheless, ballots began being printed later that day without the veto referendum and including instant runoff for the presidential election.\[39\]\[40\] The court ruled in favor of the secretary of state on September 22, allowing instant runoff to be used.\[41\] An emergency appeal to the U. S.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U. S._Supreme_Court) [Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U. S._Supreme_Court) claiming a [First Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Amendment) violation was denied by Justice [Stephen Breyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Breyer) (the [circuit justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_the_United_States#Justices_as_circuit_justices) for the [First](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Court_of_Appeals_for_the_First_Circuit) [Circuit\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Court_of_Appeals_for_the_First_Circuit) on October 6[.\[42\]

It was predicted that implementation of instant runoff could potentially delay the projection of the winner(s) of Maine's electoral votes for days after election day,\[43\] and could also complicate interpretation of the national popular vote.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_election) [\[44\]

However, the [2020 United States presidential election in Maine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_United_States_presidential_election_in_Maine) was won statewide and in the [1st congressional district](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maine%27s_1st_congressional_district) by Joe Biden and in the [2nd congressional district](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maine%27s_2nd_congressional_district) by Donald Trump with majorities, so instant runoff vote transfers did not need to be conducted, and did not impact the determination of the winners or the national popular vote tally.

### **Alaska, 2022–present**

### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ranked-choice_voting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=6)

In the 2020 Alaska elections,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Alaska_elections) voters approved [Measure 2,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Alaska_Measure_2) which replaced party primaries with a nonpartisan [jungle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jungle_primary) [primary,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jungle_primary) in which the [top 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Top-four_primary) candidates advance to a general election that uses [instant runoff voting.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instant-runoff_voting) This system is now used for all state, federal, and presidential elections (except presidential primaries, which continue to be partisan).[\[45\] The first election using the system was held on August 16, 2022, and elected Democrat Mary Peltola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Peltola) to Congress over Republicans [Sarah Palin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_Palin)[\[46\] and [Nick Begich.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nick_Begich_III)

In 2024, Alaskans voted on a measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Alaska_Ballot_Measure_2) to repeal the rankedchoice voting system and return to partisan primaries.[\[47\] Following an extended vote count, the measure narrowly failed by a margin of 737 votes, keeping the ranked-choice voting system in place.\[48\]\[49\]

#### **Hawaii, 2023–present**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ranked-choice_voting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=7)

On June 17, 2022, [Hawaii Governor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaii_Governor) [David Ige](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Ige) signed Senate

Bill 2162 into law allowing for the use of instant runoff voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instant-runoff_voting) for the [special elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_elections) of federal and county council offices. [\[50\]\[51\] Special elections in Hawaii have candidates of all parties appear on the same ballot. Before the change to instant runoff voting, the winner was whoever received a plurality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plurality_(voting)) of votes, unique among states[.\[52\] The bill's author, State Senator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaii_State_Senator) [Karl Rhoads,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Rhoads) expressed support for the usage of instant runoff voting in special elections stating: "You often get a long list of people who are running and what it results in if it's just the plurality winner, you can get a winner with 10% of the vote, and that doesn't really truly reflect the will of the people [in] that district"[.\[53\]

### **District of Columbia, 2026–present**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ranked-choice_voting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=8)

In November 2024, the District of Columbia voted for Initiative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initiative_83) [83](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initiative_83) or the Make All Votes Count Act of 2024, which will allow registered independents to participate in [party primaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_election) and implement ranked choice voting in all presidential, federal, and district elections[.\[54\]\[55\]

# **Use in party-run primaries, caucuses, and conventions**

### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ranked-choice_voting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=25)

### **Democratic presidential primaries, 2020**

### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ranked-choice_voting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=26)

Five states used RCV in the 2020 Democratic Party presidential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Democratic_Party_presidential_primaries) [primaries,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Democratic_Party_presidential_primaries) some in response to [COVID-19](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19) making an in-person caucus too risky[.\[145\]\[146\] Alaska, Hawaii, Kansas, and Wyoming used it for all voters and Nevada used it for absentee caucus voters.\[147\] Rather than eliminating candidates until a single winner is chosen, voters' choices were reallocated until all remaining candidates had at least 15%, the threshold to receive delegates to the convention.\[148\] While all candidates but one had dropped out by the time of the four primaries, use of RCV ensured that voters who selected non-competing candidates as their first choice would not have their votes wasted, but rather used toward determining delegate allocation among the remaining candidates.\[149\]\[150\]

# **Stalled implementations**

### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ranked-choice_voting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=36)

In 2021, Austin voters approved a ballot measure 59–41% to adopt ranked-choice voting for city elections, replacing the two-round system.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-round_system) [\[171\] However, it is not clear if the reference to "majority" in state law allows its use.\[172\]\[173\]

The implementation of all ranked choice voting methods in the state of Michigan has yet to actually happen as local governments still require the approval of Michigan's state legislature in order to do so. As a result, those cities that have already passed measures adopting ranked choice voting still continue to use their previous method for elections.[\[174\]\[needs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Dates_and_numbers#Chronological_items) [update](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Dates_and_numbers#Chronological_items)]

Despite passing ranked choice voting in 2018, Amherst has not yet received approval from Massachusetts' state legislature, and therefore were unable to implement the reform.\[93\] As of November 2024, ranked choice voting is banned in eleven states.\[175\]

On February 28, 2022, Tennessee became the first state to ban ranked choice voting state-wide. The sponsor of the bill, then Republican State Senator Brian Kelsey,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Kelsey) said the ban was "a win for protecting election integrity and ensuring voter clarity at the ballot box.["\[176\] Florida quickly followed with a similar ban, when governor Ron De Santis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ron_De Santis) signed senate bill 524 into law on April 25th[.\[177\] In spring of the following year, there was another string of bans in red states,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_states) with Idaho passing a ban on March 23rd[,\[178\] South Dakota on March 21st,\[179\] and Montana on April 26th, 2023.\[180\] Between April and June of 2024, the use of RCV was banned statewide in Kentucky, Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana, and Oklahoma.\[181\] In 2024, Missouri voters approved a ballot initiative that banned ranked choice voting.\[182\]

Between 1912 and 1930, limited forms of ranked-choice voting were implemented and subsequently repealed in [Florida,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida)

Indiana, Maryland, Minnesota, and Wisconsin. [citation needed] In the 1970s, it was implemented in Ann Arbor, Michigan, but quickly repealed after only a single election. [183] More recently, it was adopted and repealed in Pierce County, Washington (2006–2009); [184] Burlington, Vermont (2005–2010); [11] and Aspen, Colorado (2007–2010). [10] It has since been reinstated in Burlington, [185] and Ann Arbor residents voted to reinstate it as well, with that use likely needing approval from Michigan's state legislature. [186]

Aspen, Colorado[73] passed ranked-choice voting in November 2007[187] for the mayoral race and for at-large council races with two winners. In March 2009, the Aspen council adopted a unique variation of RCV for the council races.[188] A block voting tally based on the first and second rank choices was used to determine first round support. Any candidate with initial majority support was elected. If there were not two first-round winners, there was a batch elimination of low-placing candidates to reduce the number of continuing candidates before the instant runoff. In the latter case, separate rounds of ranked-choice counting would be conducted for each council seat, with the winner of the first seat eliminated from the race for the second seat.

Aspen's first elections with RCV and the new city council system were on May 5, 2009. The number of voters was the highest in the history of Aspen elections. [189] Mick Ireland was re-elected as mayor in the fourth round of a four-candidate race. Both city council incumbents were defeated in the two-

seat RCV election in which nine candidates participated. The winners were selected after RCV tallies. 168 spoiled ballots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spoiled_ballot) were recast by voters alerted to errors by their optical scanning machine[.\[190\] The city reported 0% invalid ballots in the mayor's race and 0.9% invalid ballots in the two-seat city council elections.\[191\]

The elections were close, and some Aspen observers argued that a traditional runoff system would have given more time to consider their top choices. There also was debate over how to implement audit procedures.\[192\] In 2009, voters rejected an advisory measure to maintain IRV\[193\] and in 2010, approved a binding amendment to return to a traditional runoff system. \[194\]\[195\]

### **Telluride (2011-2019)**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ranked-choice_voting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=45)

On November 4, 2008, voters in the town of Telluride,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telluride,_Colorado) [Colorado,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telluride,_Colorado) passed an ordinance with 67% of the vote to adopt RCV for the next three mayoral elections, starting in November 201[1\[73\] if three candidates file for the office.\[196\] The system was used for the city's 2011 mayoral election. The incumbent mayor Stu Fraser was re-elected by securing a majority of first choices.\[197\] In the 2015 mayoral election, Sean Murphy handily won an open seat election for mayor after trailing in first choices.\[198\]

Per the initial ballot measure, RCV could have continued after three elections with approval from the Town Council. That action was not taken so in 2023, the city returned to first-past](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-preference_plurality)[the-post voting.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-preference_plurality) [\[199\]

### **Ann Arbor (1974–1976)**

### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ranked-choice_voting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=46)

Ranked-choice voting (then called preferential voting) was adopted for mayoral races in Ann Arbor, Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ann_Arbor,_Michigan) in 1974 after a successful ballot initiative sponsored by the local [Human](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Rights_Party_(United_States)) [Rights Party.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Rights_Party_(United_States)) RCV was used in the 1975 mayoral election. Democratic Party nominee [Albert H. Wheeler,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_H._Wheeler) the city's first African-American mayor, won after trailing the Republican incumbent 49% to 40% in the first round of counting, with remaining votes cast for the Human Rights Party nominee.[\[200\] The ousted incumbent Stephenson alleged in a lawsuit that RCV violated the equal protection clause, but the county circuit court upheld the voting system.\[201\]

In April 1976, 62% of voters voted to repeal RCV.\[183\]

A 2006 law established that ranked-choice voting would be used when judicial vacancies were created between a primary election and sixty days before a general election. The law also established a pilot program for RCV for up to 10 cities in 2007 and up to 10 counties for 2008; to be monitored and reported to the 2007–2008 General Assembly.\[202\] In November 2010, North Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Carolina) had three RCV elections for local-level [superior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Carolina_Superior_Court) [court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Carolina_Superior_Court) judges, each with three candidates, and [a statewide IRV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Carolina_judicial_elections,_2010#Court_of_Appeals_(Wynn_seat)) [election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Carolina_judicial_elections,_2010#Court_of_Appeals_(Wynn_seat)) for a [North Carolina Court of Appeals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Carolina_Court_of_Appeals) seat (with 13 candidates). The Court of Appeals race is believed to be the first time RCV has been used in any statewide general election in the United States[.\[203\]\[204\]

Several municipalities considered participating in the RCV pilot in 2007. Cary, Hendersonville and Kinston voted to participate; Kinston dropped out because there were not enough candidates running to use RCV. Other cities declined to participate in the pilot. No North Carolina counties volunteered to participate in RCV in the 2008 elections held in conjunction with state and federal races.\[205\] In August 2008 the governor signed legislation extending the pilot program for local elections to be held in 2009–2011.\[206\]

There was much debate whether RCV was successful when it was used.\[207\]\[208\]\[209\] This debate continued in the North Carolina legislature when it debated legislation to extend the pilot program.\[210\] Some "verified voting" advocates contended that the RCV tabulation procedures used were not legal.\[211\]\[212\]\[213\] Both advocates and opponents of the provision supported amendments to the pilot program to ensure that the local governing body of any jurisdiction participating in the pilot must approve their participation; the jurisdiction must develop and implement voter education plans; and the University of North Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_North_Carolina) School of Government must approve procedures for conducting RCV elections by January 2009. After these amendments were adopted, the state House of Representatives, by a majority of 65-47, rejected an amendment designed to remove the pilot program from the legislation, and the legislation ultimately won approval by both houses[.\[214\]

In 2009, Hendersonville again used RCV.\[215\] Three candidates ran for mayor in Hendersonville in November 2009; five candidates ran for two seats on the city council using a multiseat version of RCV.\[216\] All seats were filled based on first choices without the need for further counting.\[217\]

In 2011, Hendersonville's city council unanimously voted to use RCV a third time, although ultimately not enough candidates filed for office to trigger the need for the system. \[218\]

The RCV pilot program was repealed by the General Assembly in 2013, meaning special judicial elections with more than two candidates would once again be decided by simple plurality. \[219\]\[220\]

In October 2007, the city of Cary, North Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cary,_North_Carolina) used RCV for municipal election for three council seats and for mayor.[\[221\] The mayor's race (with two candidates) and two of the council seats (with four and three candidates on the ballot) were won with a majority in the first round. The remaining council seat, with three candidates, went to a second round of counting; the plurality winner in the first round went on to win with 50.9% of the final round vote, amounting to 46.4% of first-round ballots cast, with 8.9% of the ballots offering no preference between the top two candidates.\[222\]

### **State ballot initiatives**

### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ranked-choice_voting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=59)

### **Massachusetts (2020)**

### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ranked-choice_voting_in_the_United_States&action=edit§ion=60)

Massachusetts rejected [Ballot Question 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Ranked-Choice_Voting_Initiative) in the 2020 general election, which would have authorized ranked-choice voting for "primary and general elections for all Massachusetts statewide offices, state legislative offices, federal congressional offices, and certain other offices beginning in 2022," but not "for

President of the United States, county commissioner, or regional district school committee members."\[239\]

Arizona Proposition 140 was rejected by voters in 2024.\[240\] It would have implemented a nonpartisan jungle primary,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jungle_primary) with the [Arizona Legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arizona_Legislature) deciding how many candidates advance to the general election. Depending on how many candidates advanced, the Legislature would have been authorized to implement ranked choice voting[.\[241\]

Colorado voters rejected Proposition 131 in 2024, which if passed would have implemented a nonpartisan jungle primary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jungle_primary) where the top four candidates advance to a ranked-choice voting general election[.\[242\]\[243\]

Idaho voters broadly rejected a top-four jungle primary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Top-four_primary) ballot measure in November 2024 by nearly 70% voting "No" (opposed). If it had passed it would have implemented a nonpartisan jungle primary where the top four candidates advance to a [ranked-choice voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranked-choice_voting) general election[.\[244\]\[245\]

In the 2022 Nevada elections,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Nevada_elections) voters narrowly approved [Question 3,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Nevada_elections#Top-Five_Ranked_Choice_Voting_Initiative) which proposed replacing party primaries with a single nonpartisan [jungle primary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jungle_primary) where the [top 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Top-four_primary#Top-five) candidates would advance to a general election that uses ranked-choice voting[.\[246\]\[247\]\[248\]\[249\] Because the proposal would have modified the Nevada constitution, it had to be reapproved by Nevada voters in 2024 before it could take effect. In 2024, voters rejected the initiative by double digits.\[250\] If reapproved, the system would have taken effect for the 2026 election cycle and been used for all state and federal elections in Nevada except President and Vice President.\[251\]\[252\]\[253\]

Two rejected initiatives on the 2024 ballot in Montana, CI-126 and CI-127, may potentially have caused ranked choice voting to be implemented. CI-126 would have created a nonpartisan jungle primary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jungle_primary) where the top four candidates advance to the general election. CI-127 would have mandated that the winning candidate receive 50% of the vote, but the [Montana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montana_Legislature) [Legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montana_Legislature) would have decided whether this is accomplished by ranked choice voting or a [runoff election.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runoff_election) [\[254\]\[255\]

Oregon rejected a legislatively-referred constitutional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oregon_Ballot_Measure_117) [amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oregon_Ballot_Measure_117) in 2024 which, if passed, would have adopted ranked-choice voting for subsequent elections for both federal offices (U. S. president, senator and representative) and state constitutional officers (governor, secretary of state, attorney general, state treasurer, and commissioner of labor and industries), as well as allowed local government bodies to adopt ranked-choice voting unless prohibited by local charter. [\[256\]\[257\]\[258\]

The proposed Fair Representation Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fair_Representation_Act_(United_States)) would amend several laws including the [Help America Vote Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help_America_Vote_Act) and the [Reapportionment Act of 1929](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reapportionment_Act_of_1929) to mandate the conversion of all congressional districts from single- to multi-member districts elected by RCV as well as the creation of state-level nonpartisan [redistricting commissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redistricting_commission) for congressional redistricting[.\[259\]\[260\]\[261\]\[262\] Originally introduced in 2017 during the 115th Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/115th_United_States_Congress) by [Don Beyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Don_Beyer) [\(D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Party_(United_States))[–VA\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia), it was reintroduced by Beyer in 2019, 2021, and 2024[.\[263\]\[264\] For Beyer the goal of the bill is to reduce polarization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_polarization_in_the_United_States) and partisanship by incentivizing elected representatives to appeal to a broader range of voters. According to proponents, ranked choice voting and multi-seat districts would reduce the number of safe-seat districts and encourage more political competition. [\[265\]

The Academy Awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_Awards) used ranked-choice voting, as do many student government elections[.\[266\]

Ranked-choice voting has been advocated for as expanding choice by encouraging more candidates to run.\[267\] Proponents have argued that ranked choice voting can reduce the spoiler effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spoiler_effect) from minor and [third-party candidates.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_party_(U. S._politics)) [\[268\] \[269\] Ranked-choice advocates often emphasize that it can sometimes produce clear majority winners where plurality rules would not.\[270\]

Opponents have argued that ranked choice voting is confusing and causes more ballot errors, and that it could disenfranchise poorer, minority and less educated voters.\[271\]\[272\]\[273\] Survey research has shown that voters are less comfortable with ranked choice voting than the simpler runoff or plurality methods.\[267\] Opponents have also noted that ranked-choice voting does not fully prevent vote splitting.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vote_splitting) [\[274\]\[275\]\[276\]\[277\] In a nonpartisan primary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonpartisan_primary) followed by ranked choice voting, as used in [Alaska,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaska) vote splitting can occur between multiple candidates of the same party[.\[278\]\[279\]\[280\]
