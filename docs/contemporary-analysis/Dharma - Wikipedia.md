Dharma ([;\[7\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-7) [Sanskrit:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_language) [धम](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E0%A4%A7%E0%A4%B0%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%AE#Sanskrit), [romanized:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Sanskrit) Dharma, pronounced [dʱɐ[rm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Sanskrit)ɐ] ( [listen\)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/14/Dharma.ogg); [Pali:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pali_language) Dhamma), although there is [no direct single-word](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Untranslatability) [translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Untranslatability) for dharma in European languages,[\[8\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-lraw-8) is commonly translated as "righteousness", "merit" or "religious and moral duties" governing individual conduct.[\[9\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGrimes1996112-9)[\[10\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-britannica19-10) It is a key concept with multiple meanings in [Indian religions,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_religions) such as [Hinduism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism) [Buddhism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism) [Jainism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jainism) [Sikhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhism) and others.[\[11\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-:0-11)

In [Hinduism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism) dharma is one of the four components of the [Puru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puru%E1%B9%A3%C4%81rtha)ṣārtha, the aims of life, and signifies behaviours that are considered to be in accord with Ṛ[ta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E1%B9%9Ata), the order that makes life and universe possible.[\[12\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-ODWR-Dharma-12)[\[note 1\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-ODWR-Dharma-13) It includes duties, rights, laws, conduct, virtues and "right way of living"[.\[13\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-srdtce-14) It had a transtemporal validity.[\[14\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-15)

In [Buddhism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism) dharma means ["cosmic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_cosmology) law and order"[,\[12\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-ODWR-Dharma-12)[\[15\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-Concise-PED-16) as expressed by the teachings of the [Buddha.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gautama_Buddha) [\[12\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-ODWR-Dharma-12)[\[15\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-Concise-PED-16) In [Buddhist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_philosophy) [philosophy,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_philosophy) [dhamma/dharma](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#Buddhism) is also the term for ["phenomena"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomena). [\[16\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-david-17)[\[note 2\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-DK-18) [Dharma in Jainism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharma_(Jainism)) refers to the teachings of [Tirthankara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tirthankara)

(Jina) [\[12\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-ODWR-Dharma-12) and the body of doctrine pertaining to the purification and moral transformation of human beings. In [Sikhism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhism) dharma means the path of righteousness and proper religious practice and one's own moral duties toward God[.\[17\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-rinehart138-19)

The concept of dharma was already in use in the [historical Vedic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_Vedic_religion) [religion,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_Vedic_religion) and its meaning and conceptual scope have evolved over several millennia.[\[18\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-phph-20) The ancient [Tamil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamil_language) moral text [Tirukku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tirukku%E1%B9%9Fa%E1%B8%B7)ṟaḷ, despite being a collection of aphoristic teachings on dharma (aram), artha (porul), and kama (inpam),[\[19\]:](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-Blackburn2000-21)453[\[20\]:](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-Sanjeevi2006-22)82 is completely and exclusively based on aṟ[am](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aram_(Kural_book)), the Tamil term for dharma[.\[21\]:](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-Velusamy Faraday2017-23)55 As with the other components of the Puruṣārtha, the concept of dharma is pan-Indian. The [antonym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonym) of dharma is [adharma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adharma).

# Etymology[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dharma&action=edit§ion=1)]

## ![](_page_2_Picture_5.jpeg)

The word dharma has roots in the Sanskrit dhr-, which means to hold or to support, and is related to Latin firmus (firm, stable).[\[22\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-Etymological Dictionary-24) From this, it takes the meaning of "what is established or firm", and hence "law". It is derived from an older [Vedic Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedic_Sanskrit) n-stem dharman-, with a literal meaning of "bearer, supporter", in a [religious sense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_Vedic_religion) conceived as an aspect of [Rta.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rta) [\[23\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDay198242%E2%80%9345-25)

In the [Rigveda,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigveda) the word appears as an n-stem, dhárman-, with a range of meanings encompassing "something established or firm" (in the literal sense of prods or poles). Figuratively, it means "sustainer" and "supporter" (of deities). It is semantically similar to the Greek [themis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Themis) ("fixed decree, statute, law")[.\[24\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-26)

In Classical Sanskrit, and in the [Vedic Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedic_Sanskrit) of the [Atharvaveda,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atharvaveda) the stem is thematic: dhárma- [\(Devanagari:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devanagari) धम). In [Prakrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prakrit) and [Pali,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pali) it is rendered dhamma. In some [contemporary Indian languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Aryan_languages#New_Indo-Aryan) and dialects it alternatively occurs as dharm.

In the 3rd century BCE the [Mauryan Emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurya_Empire) [Ashoka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashoka) translated dharma into Greek and Aramaic and he used the Greek word [eusebeia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eusebeia) (εὐσέβεια, piety, spiritual maturity, or godliness) in the [Kandahar Bilingual Rock Inscription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kandahar_Bilingual_Rock_Inscription) and the [Kandahar Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kandahar_Greek_Edicts_of_Ashoka) [Edicts.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kandahar_Greek_Edicts_of_Ashoka) [\[25\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-27) In the [Kandahar Bilingual Rock Inscription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kandahar_Bilingual_Rock_Inscription) he used the [Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramaic_language) word אטישק) qšyṭ'; truth, rectitude).[\[26\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-28)

# Definition[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dharma&action=edit§ion=2)]

Dharma is a concept of central importance in [Indian philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_philosophy) and [religion.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_religions) [\[27\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-29) It has multiple meanings in [Hinduism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism) [Buddhism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism) [Sikhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhism) and [Jainism.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jainism) [\[11\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-:0-11) It is difficult to provide a single concise definition for dharma, as the word has a long and varied history and straddles a complex set of meanings and interpretations.[\[28\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-30) There is no equivalent single-word synonym for dharma in western languages.[\[8\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-lraw-8)

There have been numerous, conflicting attempts to translate ancient Sanskrit literature with the word dharma into German, English and French. The concept, claims Paul Horsch[,\[29\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-paulhorsch-31) has caused exceptional difficulties for modern commentators and translators. For example, while Grassmann's[\[30\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-32) translation of Rig-Veda identifies seven different meanings of dharma, [Karl Friedrich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Friedrich_Geldner) [Geldner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Friedrich_Geldner) in his translation of the Rig-Veda employs 20 different translations for dharma, including meanings such as "law", "order", "duty", "custom", "quality", and "model", among others.[\[29\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-paulhorsch-31) However, the word dharma has become a widely accepted [loanword](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loanword) in English, and is included in all modern unabridged English dictionaries.

The root of the word dharma is "dhr̥", which means "to support, hold, or bear". It is the thing that regulates the course of change by not participating in change, but that principle which remains constant.[\[31\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-stevenrosen-33) Monier-Williams, the widely cited resource for definitions and explanation of Sanskrit words and concepts of Hinduism, offers[\[32\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-34) numerous definitions of the word dharma, such as that which is established or firm, steadfast decree, statute, law, practice, custom, duty, right, justice, virtue, morality, ethics, religion, religious merit, good works, nature, character, quality, property. Yet, each of these definitions is incomplete, while the combination of these translations does not convey the total sense of the word. In common parlance, dharma means "right way of living" and "path of rightness".[\[31\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-stevenrosen-33) Dharma also has connotations of order, and when combined with the word sanatana, it can also be described as eternal truth.[\[33\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-35)

The meaning of the word dharma depends on the context, and its meaning has evolved as ideas of Hinduism have developed through history. In the earliest texts and ancient myths of

Hinduism, dharma meant cosmic law, the rules that created the universe from chaos, as well as rituals; in later [Vedas,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas) [Upanishads,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upanishads) [Puranas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puranas) and the [Epics,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_epic_poetry) the meaning became refined, richer, and more complex, and the word was applied to diverse contexts.[\[18\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-phph-20) In certain contexts, dharma designates human behaviours considered necessary for order of things in the universe, principles that prevent chaos, behaviours and action necessary to all life in nature, society, family as well as at the individual level.[\[12\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-ODWR-Dharma-12)[\[18\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-phph-20) [\[34\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-36)[\[note 1\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-ODWR-Dharma-13) Dharma encompasses ideas such as duty, rights, character, vocation, religion, customs and all behaviour considered appropriate, correct or morally upright.[\[35\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-37) For further context, the word varnasramdharma is often used in its place, defined as dharma specifically related to the stage of life one is in[.\[36\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-38)

The antonym of dharma is [adharma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adharma) (Sanskrit: अधम),[\[37\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-39) meaning that which is "not dharma". As with dharma, the word adharma includes and implies many ideas; in common parlance, adharma means that which is against nature, immoral, unethical, wrong or unlawful.[\[38\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-40)

In Buddhism, dharma incorporates the teachings and doctrines of the founder of Buddhism, [the Buddha.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gautama_Buddha)

# History[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dharma&action=edit§ion=3)]

According to Pandurang Vaman Kane, author of the authoritative book [History of Dharma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Dharma%C5%9B%C4%81stra)śāstra, the word dharma appears at least fifty-six times in the hymns of the [Rigveda,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigveda) as an adjective or noun. According to Paul Horsch,[\[29\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-paulhorsch-31) the word dharma has its origin in Vedic Hinduism. The hymns of the Rig Veda claim [Brahman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahman)

created the universe from chaos, they hold (dhar-) the earth and sun and stars apart, they support (dhar-) the sky away and distinct from earth, and they stabilise (dhar-) the quaking mountains and plains.[\[29\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-paulhorsch-31)[\[39\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-41)

The gods, mainly [Indra,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indra) then deliver and hold order from disorder, harmony from chaos, stability from instability - actions recited in the Veda with the root of word dharma[.\[18\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-phph-20) In hymns composed after the mythological verses, the word dharma takes expanded meaning as a [cosmic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_cosmology) principle and appears in verses independent of gods. It evolves into a concept, claims Paul Horsch[,\[29\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-paulhorsch-31) that has a dynamic functional sense in [Atharvaveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atharvaveda) for example, where it becomes the cosmic law that links cause and effect through a subject. Dharma, in these ancient texts, also takes a ritual meaning. The ritual is connected to the cosmic, and "dharmani" is equated to ceremonial devotion to the principles that gods used to create order from disorder, the world from chaos.[\[40\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-42)

Past the ritual and cosmic sense of dharma that link the current world to mythical universe, the concept extends to an ethical-social sense that links human beings to each other and to other life forms. It is here that dharma as a concept of law emerges in Hinduism[.\[41\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-43)[\[42\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-44)

Dharma and related words are found in the oldest Vedic literature of Hinduism, in later Vedas, Upanishads, Puranas, and the Epics; the word dharma also plays a central role in the literature of other Indian religions founded later, such as Buddhism and Jainism.[\[18\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-phph-20) According to Brereton,[\[43\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-jbrereton-45) Dharman occurs 63 times in [Rig-veda;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigveda) in addition, words related to Dharman also appear in Rig-veda, for example once as dharmakrt, 6 times as satyadharman, and once as dharmavant, 4 times as dharman and twice as dhariman. [[citation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed) [needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)]

[Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-European_studies) parallels for "dharma" are known, but the only Iranian equivalent is Old Persian darmān "remedy", the meaning of which is rather removed from [Indo-Aryan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Aryan_languages) dhárman, suggesting that the word "dharma" did not have a major role in the [Indo-Iranian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Iranians) period, and was principally developed more recently under the Vedic tradition[.\[43\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-jbrereton-45)

However, it is thought that the [Daena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daena) of [Zoroastrianism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism) also meaning the "eternal Law" or "religion", is related to Sanskrit "dharma".[\[44\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-46)

Ideas in parts overlapping to Dharma are found in other ancient cultures: such as Chinese [Tao,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tao) Egyptian [Maat,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maat) Sumerian [Me.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Me_(mythology)) [\[31\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-stevenrosen-33)

## Eusebeia and dharma[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dharma&action=edit§ion=4)]

## ![](_page_7_Picture_7.jpeg)

In the mid-20th century, an inscription of the Indian [Emperor Asoka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Asoka) from the year 258 BC was discovered in Afghanistan, the [Kandahar Bilingual Rock Inscription.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kandahar_Bilingual_Rock_Inscription) This rock inscription contains

[Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) and [Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramaic_language) text. According to [Paul Hacker,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Hacker_(Indologist)) [\[45\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-paulhacker-47) on the rock appears a Greek rendering for the Sanskrit word dharma: the word [eusebeia.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eusebeia) [\[45\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-paulhacker-47)

Scholars of Hellenistic Greece explain eusebeia as a complex concept. Eusebia means not only to venerate gods, but also spiritual maturity, a reverential attitude toward life, and includes the right conduct toward one's parents, siblings and children, the right conduct between husband and wife, and the conduct between biologically unrelated people. This rock inscription, concludes Paul Hacker,[\[45\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-paulhacker-47) suggests dharma in India, about 2300 years ago, was a central concept and meant not only religious ideas, but ideas of right, of good, of one's duty toward the human community.[\[46\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-48)

## Rta, maya and dharma[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dharma&action=edit§ion=5)]

The evolving literature of Hinduism linked dharma to two other important concepts: Ṛta and Māyā. Ṛ[ta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E1%B9%9Ata) in Vedas is the truth and [cosmic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_cosmology) principle which regulates and coordinates the operation of the universe and everything within it.[\[47\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-49)[\[48\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-koller-50) [M](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81y%C4%81)āyā in Rig-veda and later literature means illusion, fraud, deception, magic that misleads and creates disorder,[\[49\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-51) thus is contrary to reality, laws and rules that establish order, predictability and harmony. Paul Horsc[h\[29\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-paulhorsch-31) suggests Ṛta and dharma are parallel concepts, the former being a cosmic principle, the latter being of moral social sphere; while Māyā and dharma are also correlative concepts, the former being that which corrupts law and moral life, the later being that which strengthens law and moral life.[\[48\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-koller-50)[\[50\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-52)

Day proposes dharma is a manifestation of Ṛta, but suggests Ṛta may have been subsumed into a more complex concept of dharma, as the idea developed in ancient India over time in a nonlinear manner.[\[51\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDay198242%E2%80%9344-53) The following verse from the [Rigveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigveda) is an example where rta and dharma are linked:

O Indra, lead us on the path of Rta, on the right path over all evils...

# Hinduism[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dharma&action=edit§ion=6)]

Dharma is an organising principle in Hinduism that applies to human beings in solitude, in their interaction with human beings and nature, as well as between inanimate objects, to all of [cosmos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmos) and its parts.[\[31\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-stevenrosen-33) It refers to the order and customs which make life and universe possible, and includes behaviours, rituals, rules that govern society, and ethics.[\[12\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-ODWR-Dharma-12)[\[note 1\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-ODWR-Dharma-13) Hindu dharma includes the religious duties, moral rights and duties of each individual, as well as behaviours that enable social order, right conduct, and those that are virtuous.[\[52\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-54) Dharma, according to Van Buitenen[,\[53\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-vanbuitenen-55) is that which all existing beings must accept and respect to sustain harmony and order in the world. It is neither the act nor the result, but the natural laws that guide the act and create the result to prevent chaos in the world. It is innate characteristic, that makes the being what it is. It is, claims Van Buitenen, the pursuit and execution of one's nature and true calling, thus playing one's role in cosmic concert. In Hinduism, it is the dharma of the bee to make honey, of cow to give milk, of sun to radiate sunshine, of river to flow.[\[53\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-vanbuitenen-55) In terms of humanity, dharma is the need for, the effect of and essence of service and interconnectedness of all life.[\[31\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-stevenrosen-33)[\[45\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-paulhacker-47)

In its true essence, dharma means for a Hindu to "expand the mind". Furthermore, it represents the direct connection between the individual and the societal phenomena that bind the society together. In the way societal phenomena affect the conscience of the individual, similarly may the actions of an individual alter the course of the society, for better or for worse. This has been subtly echoed by the credo धम धारयत जा: meaning dharma is that which holds and provides support to the social construct.

In Hinduism, dharma generally includes various aspects:

- Sanā[tana Dharma,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San%C4%81tana_Dharma) the eternal and unchanging principals of dharma[.\[54\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-56)
- Varṇ āśramā dharma, one's duty at specific [stages of life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashrama_(stage)) or inherent duties.[\[55\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-FOOTNOTEConlon199450-57)
- Sva dharma, one's own individual or personal duty.[\[56\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFritzman2015326-58)[\[9\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGrimes1996112-9)
- Āpad dharma, dharma prescribed at the time of adversities.[\[9\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGrimes1996112-9)
- Sadharana dharma, moral duties irrespective of the [stages of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashrama_(stage)) [life.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashrama_(stage)) [\[57\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKumar Choudhury20208-59)[\[note 3\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-61)
- [Yuga dharma,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuga_dharma) dharma which is valid for a [yuga,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuga) an epoch or age as established by Hindu tradition and thus may change at the conclusion of its time.[\[10\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-britannica19-10)[\[59\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGrimes1996112-113-62)

#### In Vedas and Upanishads[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dharma&action=edit§ion=7)]

The [history section](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#History) of this article discusses the development of dharma concept in [Vedas.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas) This development continued in the [Upanishads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upanishads) and later ancient scripts of Hinduism. In Upanishads, the concept of dharma continues as universal principle of law, order, harmony, and truth. It acts as the regulatory moral principle of the Universe. It is explained as law of righteousness and equated to [satya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satya) [\(Sanskrit:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit) सय, truth), ं [\[60\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-chjo-63)[\[61\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-paulh-64) in hymn 1.4.14 of [Brhadaranyaka Upanishad,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brihadaranyaka_Upanishad) as follows:

धमः तमामात्परं नाय्अथो अबलीयान्बलीयाँसमाशँसतेधमण यथा राैवम्। यो वैस धमः सयं वैतत्तमासयं वदतमाहुर्धमवदतीत धमवा वदतँसयं वदतीय्एतयेवैतदभय ु ं भवत ।।

Nothing is higher than dharma. The weak overcomes the stronger by dharma, as over a king. Truly that dharma is the Truth (Satya); Therefore, when a man speaks the Truth, they say, "He speaks the Dharma"; and if he speaks Dharma, they say, "He speaks the Truth!" For both are one.

## In the Epics[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dharma&action=edit§ion=8)]

The Hindu religion and philosophy, claims [Daniel Ingalls,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_H._H._Ingalls,_Sr.) [\[62\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-65) places major emphasis on individual practical morality. In the Sanskrit epics, this concern is omnipresent.

In the Second Book of [Ramayana,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramayana) for example, a peasant asks the King to do what dharma morally requires of him, the King agrees and does so even though his compliance with the law of dharma costs him dearly. Similarly, dharma is at the centre of all major events in the life of Rama, Sita, and Lakshman in Ramayana, claims Daniel Ingalls.[\[63\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-danielingalls-66) Each episode of Ramayana presents life situations and ethical questions in symbolic terms. The issue is debated by the characters, finally the right prevails over wrong, the good over evil. For this reason, in Hindu Epics, the good, morally upright, law-abiding king is referred to as "dharmaraja".[\[64\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-67)

In [Mahabharata,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahabharata) the other major Indian epic, similarly, dharma is central, and it is presented with symbolism and metaphors. Near the end of the epic, the god Yama, referred to as dharma in the text, is portrayed as taking the form of a dog to test the compassion of [Yudhishthira,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yudhishthira) who is told he may not enter paradise with such an animal, but refuses to abandon his companion, for which decision he is then praised by dharma.[\[65\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-68) The value and appeal of the Mahabharata is not as much in its complex and rushed presentation of metaphysics in the 12th book, claims Ingalls,[\[63\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-danielingalls-66) because Indian metaphysics is more eloquently presented in other Sanskrit scriptures; the appeal of Mahabharata, like Ramayana, is in its presentation of a series of moral problems and life situations, to which there are usually three answers given, according to Ingalls:[\[63\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-danielingalls-66) one answer is of [Bhima,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhima) which is the answer of brute force, an individual angle representing materialism, egoism, and self; the second answer is of [Yudhishthira,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yudhishthira) which is always an appeal to piety and gods, of social virtue and of tradition; the third answer is of introspective [Arjuna,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arjuna) which falls between the two extremes, and who, claims Ingalls, symbolically reveals the finest moral qualities of man. The Epics of Hinduism are a symbolic treatise about life, virtues, customs, morals, ethics, law, and other aspects of dharma.[\[66\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-69) There is extensive discussion of dharma at the individual level in the Epics of Hinduism, observes [Ingalls;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_H._H._Ingalls,_Sr.) for example, on free will versus destiny, when and why human beings believe in either, ultimately concluding that the strong and prosperous naturally uphold free will, while those facing grief or frustration naturally lean towards destiny.[\[67\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-70) The Epics of Hinduism illustrate various aspects of dharma, they are a means of communicating dharma with metaphors[.\[68\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-71)

## According to 4th-century Vatsyayana[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dharma&action=edit§ion=9)]

According to [Klaus Klostermaier,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klaus_Klostermaier) 4th-century CE Hindu scholar Vātsyā[yana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C4%81tsy%C4%81yana) explained dharma by contrasting it with adharma.[\[69\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-klausk-72) Vātsyāyana suggested that dharma is not merely in one's actions, but also in words one speaks or writes, and in thought. According to Vātsyāyana[:\[69\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-klausk-72)[\[70\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-73)

- 1. Adharma of body: hinsa (violence), steya (steal, theft), pratisiddha maithuna (sexual indulgence with someone other than one's partner)
- 2. Dharma of body: dana (charity), paritrana (succor of the distressed) and paricarana (rendering service to others)
- 3. Adharma from words one speaks or writes: mithya (falsehood), parusa (caustic talk), sucana (calumny) and asambaddha (absurd talk)
- 4. Dharma from words one speaks or writes: satya (truth and facts), hitavacana (talking with good intention), priyavacana (gentle, kind talk), svadhyaya (self-study)
- 5. Adharma of mind: paradroha (ill will to anyone), paradravyabhipsa (covetousness), nastikya (denial of the existence of morals and religiosity)
- 6. Dharma of mind: daya (compassion), asprha (disinterestedness), and sraddha (faith in others)

## According to Patanjali Yoga[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dharma&action=edit§ion=10)]

In the [Yoga Sutras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoga_Sutras_of_Patanjali) [of Patanjali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoga_Sutras_of_Patanjali) the dharma is real; in the Vedanta it is unreal.[\[71\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-The_yoga-system_of_Patanjali-74)

Dharma is part of [yoga,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoga) suggests Patanjali; the elements of Hindu dharma are the attributes, qualities and aspects of yoga.[\[71\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-The_yoga-system_of_Patanjali-74) Patanjali explained dharma in two categories: [yamas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamas) (restraints) and [niyamas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niyama) (observances).[\[69\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-klausk-72)

The five yamas, according to Patanjali, are: abstain from injury to all living creatures, abstain from falsehood (satya), abstain from unauthorised appropriation of things-of-value from another (acastrapurvaka), abstain from coveting or sexually cheating on your partner, and abstain from expecting or accepting gifts from others.[\[72\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-75) The five yama apply in action, speech and mind. In explaining yama, Patanjali clarifies that certain professions and situations may require qualification in conduct. For example, a fisherman must injure a fish, but he must attempt to do this with least trauma to fish and the fisherman must try to injure no other creature as he fishes.[\[73\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-76)

The five niyamas (observances) are cleanliness by eating pure food and removing impure thoughts (such as arrogance or jealousy or pride), contentment in one's means, meditation and silent reflection regardless of circumstances one faces, study and pursuit of historic knowledge, and devotion of all actions to the Supreme Teacher to achieve perfection of concentration[.\[74\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-77)

#### Sources[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dharma&action=edit§ion=11)]

Dharma is an empirical and experiential inquiry for every man and woman, according to some texts of Hinduism.[\[45\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-paulhacker-47)[\[75\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-78) For example, [Apastamba Dharmasutra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apastamba) states:

Dharma and Adharma do not go around saying, "That is us." Neither do gods, nor gandharvas, nor ancestors declare what is Dharma and what is Adharma.

-- Apastamba Dharmasutr[a\[76\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-79)

In other texts, three sources and means to discover dharma in Hinduism are described. These, according to [Paul Hacker,](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Hacker) are:[\[77\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-ph79-80) First, learning historical knowledge such as Vedas, Upanishads, the Epics and other Sanskrit literature with the help of one's teacher. Second, observing the behaviour and example of good people. The third source applies when neither one's education nor example exemplary conduct is known. In this case, ["atmatusti"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmatusti) is the source of dharma in Hinduism, that is the good person reflects and follows what satisfies his heart, his own inner feeling, what he feels driven to.[\[77\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-ph79-80)

## Dharma, life stages and social stratification[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dharma&action=edit§ion=12)]

Some texts of Hinduism outline dharma for society and at the individual level. Of these, the most cited one is [Manusmriti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manusmriti), which describes the four Varnas, their rights and duties[.\[78\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-alfh-81) Most texts of Hinduism, however, discuss dharma with no mention of Varna ([caste\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caste_system_in_India).[\[79\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-82) Other dharma texts and Smritis differ from Manusmriti on the nature and structure of Varnas.[\[78\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-alfh-81) Yet, other texts question the very existence of varna. [Bhrigu,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhrigu) in the Epics, for example, presents the theory that dharma does not require any varnas.[\[80\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-83) In practice, medieval India is widely believed to be a socially stratified society, with each social strata inheriting a profession and being endogamous. Varna was not absolute in Hindu dharma; individuals had the right to renounce and leave their Varna, as well as their [asramas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashrama_(stage)) of life, in search of moksa.[\[78\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-alfh-81)[\[81\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-84) While neither Manusmriti nor succeeding Smritis of Hinduism ever use the word varnadharma (that is, the dharma of varnas), or varnasramadharma (that is, the dharma of varnas and asramas), the scholarly commentary on Manusmriti use these words, and thus associate dharma with varna system of India.[\[78\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-alfh-81)[\[82\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-85) In 6th century India, even Buddhist kings called themselves "protectors of varnasramadharma" - that is, dharma of varna and asramas of life.[\[78\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-alfh-81)[\[83\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-86)

At the individual level, some texts of Hinduism outline [four](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashrama_(stage)) āś[ramas,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashrama_(stage)) or stages of life as individual's dharma. These are:[\[84\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-87) (1) [brahmac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmacharya)ārya, the life of preparation as a student, (2) gṛ[hastha,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grihastha) the life of the householder with family and other social roles, (3) vā[nprastha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanaprastha) or aranyaka, the life of the forest-dweller, transitioning from worldly occupations to reflection and renunciation, and (4) [sanny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanny%C4%81sa)āsa, the life of giving away all property, becoming a recluse and devotion to moksa, spiritual matters.

The four stages of life complete the four human strivings in life, according to Hinduism[.\[85\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-jkkpwg-88) Dharma enables the individual to satisfy the striving for stability and order, a life that is lawful and harmonious, the striving to do the right thing, be good, be virtuous, earn religious merit, be helpful to others, interact successfully with society. The other three strivings are [Artha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artha) - the striving for means of life such as food, shelter, power, security, material wealth, and so forth; [Kama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kama) - the striving for sex, desire, pleasure, love, emotional fulfilment, and so forth; and [Moksa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moksa) - the striving for spiritual meaning, liberation from life-rebirth cycle, self-realisation in this life, and so forth. The four stages are neither independent nor exclusionary in Hindu dharma.[\[85\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-jkkpwg-88)

## Dharma and poverty[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dharma&action=edit§ion=13)]

Dharma being necessary for individual and society, is dependent on poverty and prosperity in a society, according to Hindu dharma scriptures. For example, according to Adam Bowles,[\[86\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-adambowles-89) [Shatapatha Brahmana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shatapatha_Brahmana) 11.1.6.24 links social prosperity and dharma through water. Waters come from rains, it claims; when rains are abundant there is prosperity on the earth, and this prosperity enables people to follow Dharma - moral and lawful life. In times of distress, of drought, of poverty, everything suffers including relations between human beings and the human ability to live according to dharma.[\[86\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-adambowles-89)

In Rajadharmaparvan 91.34-8, the relationship between poverty and dharma reaches a full circle. A land with less moral and lawful life suffers distress, and as distress rises it causes more immoral and unlawful life, which further increases distress[.\[86\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-adambowles-89)[\[87\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-90) Those in power must follow the raja dharma (that is, dharma of rulers), because this enables the society and the individual to follow dharma and achieve prosperity.[\[88\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-91)

#### Dharma and law[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dharma&action=edit§ion=14)]

The notion of dharma as duty or propriety is found in India's ancient legal and religious texts. Common examples of such use are pitri dharma (meaning a person's duty as a father), putra dharma (a person's duty as a son), raj dharma (a person's duty as a king) and so forth.[\[89\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-92) In Hindu philosophy, justice, social harmony, and happiness requires that people live per dharma. The [Dharmashastra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharmashastra) is a record of these guidelines and rules[.\[90\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-93) The available evidence suggest India once had a large collection of dharma related literature (sutras, shastras); four of the sutras survive and these are now referred to as Dharmasutras.[\[91\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-polivelle-94) Along with laws of Manu in Dharmasutras, exist parallel and different compendium of laws, such as the laws of Narada and other ancient scholars.[\[92\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-95)[\[93\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-96) These different and conflicting law books are neither exclusive, nor do they supersede other sources of dharma in Hinduism. These Dharmasutras include instructions on education of the young, their rites of passage, customs, religious rites and rituals, marital rights and obligations, death and ancestral rites, laws and administration of justice, crimes, punishments, rules and types of evidence, duties of a king, as well as morality.[\[91\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-polivelle-94)

## Buddhism[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dharma&action=edit§ion=15)]

In Buddhism dharma means cosmic law and order,[\[12\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-ODWR-Dharma-12)[\[15\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-Concise-PED-16) but is also applied to the teachings of the Buddha.[\[12\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-ODWR-Dharma-12)[\[15\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-Concise-PED-16) In [Buddhist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_philosophy) [philosophy,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_philosophy) dhamma/dharma is also the term for ["phenomena"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomena). [\[15\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-Concise-PED-16)[\[16\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-david-17)

## Buddha's teachings[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dharma&action=edit§ion=16)]

For practising Buddhists, references to "dharma" (dhamma in Pali) particularly as "the dharma", generally means the teachings of the Buddha, commonly known throughout the East as Buddhadharma. It includes especially the discourses on the fundamental principles (such as the [Four Noble Truths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Noble_Truths) and the [Noble Eightfold Path\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path), as opposed to the parables and to the poems. The Buddha's teachings explain that in order to end suffering, dharma, or the right thoughts, understanding, actions and livelihood, should be cultivated.[\[94\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-97)

The status of dharma is regarded variably by different Buddhist traditions. Some regard it as an ultimate truth, or as the fount of all things which lie beyond the ["three realms"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_realms) (Sanskrit: tridhatu) and the "wheel of becoming" (Sanskrit: [bhavachakra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhavachakra)). Others, who regard the Buddha as simply an enlightened human being, see the dharma as the [essence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essence) of the "84,000 different aspects of the teaching" (Tibetan: chos-sgo brgyad-khri bzhi strong) that the Buddha gave to various types of people, based upon their individual propensities and capabilities.

Dharma refers not only to the sayings of the Buddha, but also to the later traditions of interpretation and addition that the various [schools of Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schools_of_Buddhism) have developed to help explain and to expand upon the Buddha's teachings. For others still, they see the dharma as referring to the "truth", or the ultimate reality of "the way that things really are" (Tibetan: Chö).

The dharma is one of the [Three Jewels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Jewels) of Buddhism in which practitioners of Buddhism seek refuge, or that upon which one relies for his or her lasting happiness. The Three Jewels of Buddhism are the [Buddha,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhahood) meaning the mind's perfection of enlightenment, the [dharma,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharma_(Buddhism)) meaning the teachings and the methods of the Buddha, and the [Sangha,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sangha_(Buddhism)) meaning the community of practitioners who provide one another guidance and support.

#### Chan Buddhism[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dharma&action=edit§ion=17)]

Dharma is employed in [Chan Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chan_Buddhism) in a specific context in relation to transmission of authentic doctrine, understanding and bodhi; recognised in [dharma transmission.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharma_transmission)

## Theravada Buddhism[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dharma&action=edit§ion=18)]

In Theravada Buddhism obtaining ultimate realisation of the dhamma is achieved in three phases; learning, practising and realising.[\[95\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-What_is_the_Triple_Gem-98)

## In Pali

- 1. [Pariyatti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pariyatti) the learning of the theory of dharma as contained within the suttas of the Pali canon
- 2. Patipatti putting the theory into practice and
- 3. Pativedha when one penetrates the dharma or through experience realises the truth of it.[\[95\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-What_is_the_Triple_Gem-98)

## Jainism[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dharma&action=edit§ion=19)]

The word dharma in Jainism is found in all its key texts. It has a contextual meaning and refers to a number of ideas. In the broadest sense, it means the teachings of the Jinas,[\[12\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-ODWR-Dharma-12) or teachings of any competing spiritual school,[\[96\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-99) a supreme path[,\[97\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-100) socio-religious duty,[\[98\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-101) and that which is the highest mangala

(holy).[\[99\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-Cort2001p192-102)

The [Tattvartha Sutra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tattvartha_Sutra), a major [Jain text,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_text) mentions daśa dharma (lit. 'ten dharmas') with referring to ten righteous virtues: forbearance, modesty, straightforwardness, purity, truthfulness, self-restraint, austerity, renunciation, non-attachment, and celibacy.[\[100\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJain2011128-103) Acārya Amṛtacandra, author of the Jain text, Puruṣārthasiddhyupāya writes[:\[101\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJain201222-104)

A right believer should constantly meditate on virtues of dharma, like supreme modesty, in order to protect the Self from all contrary dispositions. He should also cover up the shortcomings of others.

## Dharmāstikāya[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Dharma&action=edit§ion=20)]

Further information: [Dravya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dravya)

The term dharmāstikāya [\(Sanskrit:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_language) धमातकाय) also has a specific [ontological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontology) and soteriological meaning in Jainism, as a part of its theory of six dravya (substance or a reality). In the Jain tradition, existence consists of jī[va](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C4%ABva_(Jainism)) (soul, ā[tman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%80tman_(Hinduism))) and ajī[va](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aj%C4%ABva) (non-soul, anā[tman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An%C4%81tman_(Hinduism))), the latter consisting of five categories: inert non-sentient atomic matter (pudgalāstikāya), space (āk[āś](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%80k%C4%81%C5%9Ba)a), time (kā[la](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C4%81la)), principle of motion (dharmāstikāya), and principle of rest (adharmāstikāya).[\[102\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-105)[\[103\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-Dundas2003p93-106) The use of the term dharmāstikāya to mean motion and to refer to an ontological sub-category is peculiar to Jainism, and not found in the [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics) of Buddhism and various schools of Hinduism.[\[103\]](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FDharma#cite_note-Dundas2003p93-106)
