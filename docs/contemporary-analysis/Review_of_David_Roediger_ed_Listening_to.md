*Listening to Revolt: the selected writings of George Rawick* Edited by DAVID ROEDIGER with MARTIN SMITH (Chicago, IL, Charles H. Kerr, 2010), 194 pp. Paper \$14.00.

Radical historian, sociologist and socialist George Rawick concluded his magnum opus *From Sundown to Sunup: the making of the Black community* by observing, 'the pressure of blacks for equality intensifies all social conflicts in the United States'.1 When David Roediger asked him how he came to understand the Black working class as the cutting edge of revolutionary politics and culture, Rawick explained that he developed his methodological approach in the wake of the civil rights revolution: 'I was fundamentally racist because I had not thought about it and was challenged by it. Somehow I was convinced that the problem was me and I had to resolve the contradiction.' As the title to this edited collection of his work suggests, Rawick's efforts to solve the contradiction included learning to listen to the perspectives promoted by aggrieved and insurgent people themselves.

Consisting of an introduction by David Roediger, twelve pieces penned by Rawick and afterwords by Ferruccio Gambino, George Lipsitz and Enoch H. Page, *Listening to Revolt* demonstrates Rawick's contributions to labour studies, African American history and Marxist social theory. As one of the most talented Marxist historians of race and class writing today, and someone whose scholarship has been influenced by Rawick's work, Roediger is the ideal editor. He has gathered and analysed primary sources from the George P. Rawick Papers in the Western Historical Manuscripts Collection at the University of Missouri to provide a critical intellectual and political biography in the introduction. In doing so, he explains the development of Rawick's ideas 'at the intersections of radical labor and Black freedom movement politics in Detroit and London' in the context of his participation in the Detroit-based socialist group Facing Reality with comrades Martin Glaberman and C. L. R. James.

Rawick developed his most influential historical and theoretical analyses of racism and capitalism in *From Sundown to Sunup*, which originally appeared as the first volume of *American Slave: a composite autobiography* (1972–1979),2 a multivolume collection of interviews that had been conducted by the Federal Writers' Project of former slaves during the 1930s. The two chapters republished in *Listening to Revolt* interrogate the role of racism and slavery in the development of capitalism. They provide a materialist and psychoanalytic framework to account for the role of the unconscious in the formation of white racism, as well as its role in maintaining class rule. These chapters also suggest how anti-racist, labour and peace movements have learned from the radical traditions forged in the centurieslong Black freedom struggle.

Like Karl Marx and W. E. B. Du Bois, Rawick argues that the Civil War and the abolition of slavery quickened the step of the American working class in general.3 Brushing against the grain of American exceptionalism, Rawick describes these events as definitive moments in the working-class tradition in North America and argues that the 'entire revolutionary and class struggle tradition of the American working class came to fruition in the struggles of the nineteen-thirties in the general strikes in such cities as Minneapolis, San Francisco, Toledo, and Akron'. In his seminal article on working-class self-activity, reprinted here from *Radical America* (1969), he notes that, by the 1930s, 'the working class struggled and created powerful mass industrial unions of a kind never known anywhere in the world'. He concludes that only the Russian Revolution (1917) represented a larger victory for the international class struggle.

*Listening to Revolt* describes how the civil rights movement drew on the tradition of class struggle and took 'the struggle to new heights'. Significantly, Rawick grasped the principle of Black autonomy. He analysed the ways in which Black workers' experience with racial segregation and slavery led them to develop a semi-autonomous culture. There are big stakes, he argued, in learning to listen to the ethical perspectives embedded in expressive culture, as exemplified in the brilliant work of Black writers such as Claude Mc Kay, Langston Hughes, Richard Wright and James Baldwin. Furthermore, he recognised that Dr Martin Luther King became the great leader he was by learning to listen to the voice of the Black masses and by representing their perspectives accurately in his speeches. To paraphrase Rawick, Dr King didn't make the movement; rather, the masses made the movement that produced Dr King.

The chapter 'The historical roots of Black liberation' explains how the roots of the Black freedom movement can be found in the everyday struggles of slaves before the Civil war to create an independent Black community. The chapter entitled 'Race and class in auto' provides a critical historical context for understanding the events of 1967 and 1968 in Detroit's automobile factories. According to Rawick, the Detroit rebellion of July 1967, the largest urban uprising in US history at the time, was essentially a working-class rebellion. Shortly after the revolt, Rawick (along with his comrades at Facing Reality) suggested that the Detroit rebellion exposed the vulnerability of the automobile corporations to the pressure of working-class struggle at the point of production. Nine months later, 4,000 workers shut down the plants that contained the lowest paying and most dangerous jobs, leading to the formation of the Dodge Revolutionary Union Movement (DRUM) and the League of Revolutionary Black Workers. As Rawick put it in the chapter 'The American working class', co-written with Martin Glaberman, workers won access to housing, transportation and consumption as a 'result of massive battles and continuous wars, going back over a hundred years, marked by violence, looting, destruction, strikes large and small'.

Italian scholar Ferruccio Gambino recounts, in his contribution, Rawick's late-1960s speaking tour in Italy and western Europe when he lectured at universities that had been occupied by radical students and workers. His piece provides insights into how Italian activists grappled with the meaning of the civil rights campaigns in the South, the shop-floor struggles in Detroit's auto factories, and the ways in which Rawick anticipated the centrality of migrant workers in struggles today. Enoch H. Page's 'My friendship with George Rawick' describes the impact of Rawick's scholarship and mentoring on the study of whiteness. Drawing on his experience of studying and working with Rawick, Page situates Rawick's relationships with radical African American students and scholars in St Louis and underlines his unique skills as a dialectician, teacher and oral historian. Calling up his experience as a teaching assistant for Rawick in the 1970s, George Lipsitz examines his contributions as a scholar and activist to understanding African American resistance, working-class solidarity and the multiracial fight against white supremacy. First published in Within the Shell of the Old,4 this revised and expanded version provides generative insights regarding Rawick's analysis of the self-organisation of the civil rights movement and the 'importance of labor as a key crucible for revolt'. He concludes that Rawick's direct engagement with labour and social movements better enabled him to 'envision antiracist coalitions based on what he called selfactivity - the things people do by and for themselves in the face of repressive power'.

Like Rawick's colleagues, Martin Glaberman in *Punching Out*, C. L. R. James in *American Civilization* and Stan Weir in *Singlejack Solidarity*, this edited collection of posthumous pieces provides a materialist analysis of the actual workings of antiracism and class struggle by one of the leading analysts of labour and social movements in the twentieth century.5 As Roediger suggests, Rawick ought to be considered one of the leaders of the revolution in historical scholarship, especially considering his influence 'over such leading historians of the working class as Noel Ignatiev, Robin Kelley, Peter Linebaugh, Rosemary Feurer, Huw Beynon, Margaret Washington, Bruno Cartosio, George Lipsitz, Nando Fasce, Ferruccio Gambino, Peter Rachleff and others'. *Listening to Revolt* has given us a new reason to study the work of this radical tradition and consider its method in our own studies. It links us to the perspectives we need to reclaim the history of anti-racist and working-class struggle during the worst economic crisis since the 1930s. It does so right on time. I recommend the widest possible readership.

*Fuel on the Fire: oil and politics in occupied Iraq* By GREG MUTTITT (London, Bodley Head, 2011), 433pp. Paper, £14.99.

Greg Muttitt adroitly demolishes the myth that the Iraq invasion in 2003 was a *mission civilisatrice* and the persistent denial by the US and UK governments that it had anything to do with oil. Here we have the testimony of an activist who immersed himself in the politics of Iraqi oil for almost a decade. Not only did he doggedly make Freedom of Information (FOI) inquiries for years in the UK to obtain vital minutes of meetings, but he also followed the machinations of oil monopolies, governments and think-tanks and interviewed key players. By also engaging with the Iraqi oil trade unions resisting to the privatisation of oil, he unravels for us the web of deceit permeating the invasion and occupation of Iraq.

He provides a chronicle in five parts: the first recalls the struggle over oil over the last eighty years; the second from the invasion to mid-2004 when the occupation set in; the third describes the spiralling violence as US counterinsurgency asserted control; the fourth begins with the formation of the new government in spring 2006, when the occupying powers pressed for the oil law, which led to a popular campaign against it. The final section, from 2007 onwards, is about how the oil monopolies gradually took over control of the oil fields.

The brief historical detour is a salutary reminder of the tortuous history of Iraq since its colonisation in 1914 by the British. Despite the 1958 revolution and the coup in 1963 that decimated the socialist and nationalist resistance movements, the long struggle over the control of oil ended in 1972 with nationalisation.
