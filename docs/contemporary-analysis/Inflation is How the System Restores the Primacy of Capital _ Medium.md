Following the unequal recovery from the COVID-19 pandemic, the economy has entered a [highly bizarre state.](https://www.vice.com/en/article/wx5g9b/why-is-the-economy-so-weird-right-now) On the one hand, the events of Striketober show many workers are feeling more powerful than they have in years, [walking off of jobs](https://www.cnbc.com/2021/10/19/the-great-resignation-why-people-are-quitting-their-jobs.html) and [creating militant labor formations](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2021/oct/23/striketober-unions-strikes-workers-lasting-change) at a rate not seen in decades in the United States. On the other hand, [the population in](https://www.nytimes.com/2021/11/06/upshot/inflation-psychology-economy.html) [general is absolutely despondent about the state of the](https://www.nytimes.com/2021/11/06/upshot/inflation-psychology-economy.html) [economy,](https://www.nytimes.com/2021/11/06/upshot/inflation-psychology-economy.html) and the ruling party, the Democrats, are suffering tremendous early losses in [approval ratings](https://www.nytimes.com/2021/10/23/us/politics/biden-approval-ratings.html) and [off-year](https://www.nytimes.com/2021/11/09/opinion/republicans-biden-virginia-trump.html) [elections.](https://www.nytimes.com/2021/11/09/opinion/republicans-biden-virginia-trump.html) Why are people expressing such cynicism and resignation in the face of mostly improved individual situations? The answer, in part, is fear that the wage gains have been or will soon be consumed by the decaying value of the money composing those wages, known as inflation.

From a Marxist perspective, inflation can be a major source of confusion. Strictly speaking, shouldn't the costs of commodities go down with time, rather than up, as technological advancement makes goods possible to create with fewer, more highly mechanized labor-hours? The answer is that they do, and yet the value of the money you use to buy them decreases even faster, so the monetary price of goods is increasing even as their labor-value is decreasing. Inflation is entirely absent in Marx, because as we discussed in the [essay](https://medium.com/@re. Marx/you-create-78-of-value-with-each-hour-of-labor-130f8100304d) [on money,](https://medium.com/@re. Marx/you-create-78-of-value-with-each-hour-of-labor-130f8100304d) prior to the 20th century, money tended to be a commodity (such as gold) which was produced by labor and thus had its own inherent value. This remained relatively insulated from inflation unless technological advancement made its raw material require fewer labor-hours per ounce. Now, in the era of nominal "fiat" currency, money does not actually contain (labor-)value, and thus can have its value manipulated at will.

We are not here to take a partisan position on inflation, i.e. speculate on why inflation is a necessary effect of some policy failure of the Biden administration; not least because this discussion usually takes a reactionary turn, as if inflation is just what happens when the government spends money (See this [Jacobin article](https://jacobinmag.com/2021/11/inflation-solutions-neoliberalism-biden-manchin-volcker-shock-economy/?fbclid=IwAR14Z_5nLQ-TQDevrAUzRAhbmg-p HzDSHo7kj Nbt Lly ZalT9gwf2POi94l8) for some discussion of the failures of this approach, and the modern history of inflation explanations). Instead, we will take [modern monetary theory](https://www.investopedia.com/modern-monetary-theory-mmt-4588060) (MMT) as a guide, and believe them when they say currency-issuing entities can create currency without causing inflation, if they direct it towards the right ends. However, we retain a more pointed critique of Biden and the capitalist system which puts him to use, which is that standard monetary policy is in fact designed, even required, to have regular inflation. Even though there are bouts of hand-wringing, outrage, and [melodramatic](https://twitter.com/brikeilarcnn/status/1456227125346832384?ref_src=twsrc%5Etfw%7Ctwcamp%5Etweetembed%7Ctwterm%5E1456227125346832384%7Ctwgr%5E%7Ctwcon%5Es1_&ref_url=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.vice.com%2Fen%2Farticle%2Fk7wqde%2Fcnns-viral-milk-segment-gets-inflation-completely-wrong) [news pieces](https://twitter.com/brikeilarcnn/status/1456227125346832384?ref_src=twsrc%5Etfw%7Ctwcamp%5Etweetembed%7Ctwterm%5E1456227125346832384%7Ctwgr%5E%7Ctwcon%5Es1_&ref_url=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.vice.com%2Fen%2Farticle%2Fk7wqde%2Fcnns-viral-milk-segment-gets-inflation-completely-wrong) that come out when inflation gets "too high," i.e. exceeds maybe a 4% decay in value over a year, it is not so different from a "normal," "healthy," level of inflation at more like 2–3%. This normative language is quite misleading. It is, in fact, not obvious why it would be good for average people to have the value of their dollars decay with time, so long is it is not too much. There is some truth to all of the confusing claims about this topic: [inflation is good for debtors and bad for](https://theintercept.com/2021/11/10/inflation-economy-debt-milk-prices/) [creditors,](https://theintercept.com/2021/11/10/inflation-economy-debt-milk-prices/) [bad for small businesses and good for monopolies,](https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2021/nov/11/us-inflation-market-power-america-antitrust-robert-reich) [devastating to renters](https://www.ocregister.com/2021/07/06/soaring-rents-are-sticky-inflation-with-staying-power/) and [neutral for waged workers.](https://www.washingtonpost.com/business/2021/11/10/inflation-wages-workers-october/) We want to get to the bottom of this: who really benefits from inflation, both when it runs at normal speed and when it goes into overdrive?

In this essay we will analyze the structural role played by inflation, and then in future works we will discuss the psychological and propagandistic purpose this serves, above and beyond these "material" effects. Structurally, inflation on the one hand restores primacy to real asset capital: buildings, machinery, technology, and land (in other words, the industrial means of production), at the expense of finance and small businesses — and on the other hand, it reduces all the economy's workers back to their role as free-floating, unorganized proletarians, at the expense of their labor unions, contracts, and to the detriment of the elderly, disabled, or disempowered.

## **Who benefits?**

In the last essay, we discussed the roles of the [exploiting and](https://medium.com/@re. Marx/we-are-the-65-c4a253c1eaa1) [the exploited classes in our economy,](https://medium.com/@re. Marx/we-are-the-65-c4a253c1eaa1) and basically disagreed with the standard Marxist line: an exploiter, for us, is someone who gets more value out per hour than they put in [\(if they work](https://medium.com/@re. Marx/you-create-78-of-value-with-each-hour-of-labor-130f8100304d) [with normal skill and intensity, about \\$78/hour\)](https://medium.com/@re. Marx/you-create-78-of-value-with-each-hour-of-labor-130f8100304d), and the excess arises by siphoning off surplus-value from the exploited class, who get out less value per hour than they put in. Capitalists are exploiters merely because they tend not to do as much work as workers, yet are paid more — not for any metaphysical reason surrounding who owns the means of production (owning the means of production, in this view, is simply what gives them the power to exploit workers).

Certainly one can and should say the exploiting class dominates social and political life, using their hegemony to prevent solidarity and revolutionary energy by the majoritarian exploited class. But inflation only benefits a small subset of the upper class — the ones who own something other than money, or a contract to get money at some future date. Let us take a tour through some social relations, and in each case analyze which side benefits when the value of money diminishes.

### 1. Debtor vs. Creditor

A debtor is generally locked into a fixed contract to pay back some initial value. When the value of money decreases, it is generally not the case that the terms of the agreement change alongside it. So, the fixed monthly payments become, in relative terms, smaller and smaller, and the initial loan is at its largest value at the very beginning (i.e. when it is given to the debtor). This is unequivocal: debtors benefit from inflation. While the interest payments on their debt are in general a form of exploitation of the debtor, being extracted from them in exchange for no labor whatsoever, if inflation exceeds the rate of interest, then the roles are reversed: the loan becomes a net unprofitable choice for the creditor. (Of course, this is not to say creditors won't hike interest rates immediately to protect themselves).

## 2. Big business vs. Small business

In general, businesses are not overly affected by inflation they merely pass on their cost increases to their customers, raising prices of their output in such a way as to match the higher cost of inputs. However, any dynamism in the market is, in general, to the benefit of big businesses over small. These are more efficient, use more optimized technology, and have an economy of scale which raises their rate of relative surplusvalue far beyond the capacity of the small business. When prices go up, the privileged position of small businesses, presumed to be less alienating/corporate, more local/friendly/ comfortable, is shaken up. The old premium you would pay for this better service is questioned: will you be willing to pay \$5 more for a sandwich at a local place, as opposed to \$2 more as before? On the opposite end of the spectrum, truly monopolistic businesses are protected from any impacts from inflation; they are already calibrated to maximize profit, and this does not meaningfully change. Small businesses either streamline production, become less profitable, or go under, and their larger competitors are immediately ready to stake their claim on the market that remains.

## 3. Unionized vs. non-unionized workers

I am not such a revisionist as to argue that being unionized is ever not preferable to the alternative. However, inflation causes a severe weakening of the union's effective gains. Organizing union action requires an immense amount of work in developing solidarity, resilience, and strategy, and often unions fail to form even in the most obviously exploitative workplaces. This is, of course, by design of the capitalists and their business-friendly labor law. When successful, many commentators have noted that [union workers tend to be better](https://www.epi.org/publication/briefingpapers_bp143/) [paid, have more extensive benefits, and greater job security.](https://www.epi.org/publication/briefingpapers_bp143/) But what union bargaining gains are generally manifested in, specifically, is a contract, and here is where inflation comes in: the value of the wages and benefits fought for in the contract become diminished over time. If inflation gets high enough, even if the bargaining team won a cost of living adjustment (COLA), it becomes insufficient to maintain the worker's real income. The non-union worker experiences the same problems, or even worse. However, having not fought for their gains, they are thus not demoralized by their erosion, instead merely leaving jobs which become untenable (this is why the Great Resignation should not be considered equivalent to a general strike — instead of banding together to fight for improved workplaces and seizure of the worker's portion of the profits, these workers individualistically leave the job for a presumably "better" employer). The unions can and should keep fighting to maintain their position when inflation strikes, and they remain a potent tool in the arsenal of the workers, but the point is rather than fighting for a larger cut of the profits, union members fight merely to stay afloat.

## 4. Homeowner vs. Renter

These groups drastically diverge under inflation: new homeowners are generally debtors (with a mortgage), and all of them are asset-owners. They may have increased utility and other bills, but their housing costs go down, relatively, and their asset values stay the same. Tenants, on the other hand, are faced with preposterous rent increases, because with mass movement of workers towards higher-paying industries and regions, a larger proportion of the workforce competes for apartments. At best, rent increases match inflation, and at worst they massively outpace it. But there is no alternative to paying, because to add insult to injury, becoming homeowners becomes more out of reach than ever, with the asset values becoming incomparable to any pre-inflationary savings. Incidentally, landlords get the best of both worlds: their cost of housing, i.e. mortgage, goes down, relatively, but real rent revenue stays the same or increases, and thus landlords increase profits in an inflationary moment, as soon as they have the legal right to raise rents accordingly.

### 5. The Poor vs. the Working Class vs. the Middle Class

These groups are all similar in the fact that they rely on a wage; however inflation does not have an equal effect on them. The poorest are working minimum-wage jobs and are massively rent-burdened. When their costs go up, but their wage does not, they are as likely to become homeless and unemployed as they are to migrate to some higher-paying new work, and in the process, they become a transient underclass, AKA the "lumpenproletariat." A special case of this is the elderly or pensioners on fixed incomes. They have very few tools with which to fight back against inflation, and no mechanisms for increasing their income — they often can only respond by cutting back on food or necessary medications, unless they can rely on children to support them. Slightly above them, once a worker has just enough breathing room to quit or move, the working class person becomes a proletarian, who is in a classic Marxist sense "doubly free": free to move around for the best possible pay and working conditions, and also free from any assets which would provide an alternative path to self-sufficiency besides waged work. Inflation is painful for this group, but they can simply quit, move, and some other employer will be paying a wage which in this new economy is now "standard." While it is true that with inflation their need to take action to maintain their standards of living increases, at the same time the bargaining power they have to demand more increases with it. Finally, the middle class, which strictly speaking is in a better economic position than the working class, is in fact more damaged by inflationary pressures. Their savings are weakened, and their rents, union gains, or any other lucky breaks that put them in a relatively good position, are with the rapid changes in the economy subject to reevaluation. These people are pushed back towards their siblings in the true working class — as if they never saved at all.

What each of these comparisons shows is that inflation is not in itself a mechanism for increasing exploitation, but rather a way of fragmenting these otherwise similar social groups along the lines of their access to real assets. Purely financial tools are weakened (savings, contracts, and loans), pushing workers towards the "free" state of proletarianism, and pushing capital towards investment in fully industrialized production of goods and services.

This graph orders these groups by roughly their socialeconomic power, where from right to left is an increasing amount of actual work performed, and from top to bottom is increasing compensation (as wages or profits) — so the bottom right corner is the most exploited, and the top left corner is the most powerful exploiter. These groups are ordered in roughly a layman's estimate of their political and social power, but the impact inflation has on their status does not correlate with that at all. The groups that retain roughly their place are 2 and 6, AKA the proletariat (those below them are cast out of society altogether, and those above lose the security which kept them out of this class) and the bourgeoisie (those below them are cast towards proletarianism, those above are directed towards becoming them). Group 4 is in the strange position of being hurt as a middle-class worker with savings, but helped as a debtor and asset-owner. They are pushed in both directions at once, becoming the paradoxical ["landed poor."](https://www.ifpri.org/publication/strengthening-property-rights-poor)

The deeper structure that inflation draws out is what we should finally refer to as capitalism, in the sense that it is an economy which truly revolves around capital. This mechanism is the economy's way of brushing aside the hangers-on — the "upwardly-mobile," the "middle-class," and the speculative "financial wizards," who have ingeniously built little grifts and scams into the nooks and crannies of the economic machine to reveal that underneath all this superficial complexity, capitalism is still the same old 19th-century monster. The core of the capitalist mode of production is that there are really just two social classes which define the reproduction and maintenance of society: the bourgeoisie and the proletariat, or in other words the ones who own the physical buildings, machines, goods, and land throughout the world, and the ones who perform labor with those materials (a.k.a. the means of production).

Inflation effectively returns workers from their (aspirational) "middle class" to their role as proletarians. It demolishes their savings if they have them, and if they are too old, weak, or unreliable to be workers again, nudges them instead towards an early grave. Union bargaining gains are nullified — worker power under an inflationary scheme requires constantly renewed organizing and fighting, but no longer to increase the worker's social power, but merely to maintain it (think about how many union campaigns depend on basically the idea, "our wages are not keeping up with inflation!"). In a sufficiently inflationary world, it is no longer possible to imagine actually getting ahead, only barely hanging on. The presumptuous small businesses, which are not owners of truly industrial, efficient capitalist enterprises, are put back into their place and cut out by the cheaper and more effective big-box stores and industrial plants. Finally, the financial elites are punished for the hubris of creating new money out of nothing.¹ Only real assets on the one hand and labor on the other really count for something in the long run, and inflation serves to restore their primacy.

It's true that, contrary to reactionary hysteria, inflation doesn't really make the lives of the proletariat worse, because it decreases their current wages only as it increases their bargaining power. But that's not the role it is supposed to serve anyways; it's not like the capitalist system could survive if it was "always making everything worse for the vast majority." Its real purpose is to discipline everyone who starts to rise out of that class back down into it, and to push whoever started to organize that class for power, back towards square one. Inflation reminds everyone that it is property, not people, and certainly not money, which defines the fundamental social relations in our society.

It is in this sense that inflation forces us to return to Marx. And the point of emphasizing this, which Marx would surely endorse, is that rather than trying to name the policy prescription which would prevent inflation, the Left should focus on attacking the power of capital itself. Only this would deprive inflation of any reason to exist in the first place.
