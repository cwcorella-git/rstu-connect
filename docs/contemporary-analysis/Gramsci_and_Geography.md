# **Gramsci and Geography**

## **Jordan Camp**

**LAST MODIFIED: 21 FEBRUARY 2022 DOI: 10.1093/OBO/9780199874002-0241**

# **Introduction**

On 16 May 1925, Antonio Gramsci delivered his first and only speech in the Italian Parliament. An elected deputy of the Italian Communist Party, he spoke out against Fascist efforts to "liquidate" his party. He denounced arrests of his fellow leaders, arguing, "You are doing what they used to do in the South, when they hired thugs to arrest anyone who voted for the opposition." The Fascists in Parliament, including his adversary Benito Mussolini, continually interrupted Gramsci, asserting that he "knew nothing of the South." Gramsci responded emphatically, "I am a southerner." He proceeded to offer a distinctly spatial set of insights. He noted that the state was expropriating resources from peasants in the South and using them to exploit workers in factories of the North. The defeat of Fascism, he believed, would require a class alliance between northern workers and southern peasants. "Upon this terrain," he argued, workers and peasants could organize "against their common enemy." After the speech, Mussolini approached Gramsci as he drank coffee at the bar. Mussolini reached out his hand in congratulation, but, as biographer Giuseppe Fiori recounts, Gramsci "continued sipping his coffee indifferently." This article demonstrates Gramsci's relevance for the study of geography. It includes biographical information about Gramsci, a discussion of his main published works, and a brief summary of some important works in the field. It describes Gramsci's long history as a student of the field, having studied geography and linguistics at the University of Turin under Professor Mario Bartoli in the 1910s. It traces Gramsci's development as a spatial thinker in his speeches, political writing, and journalism as a leader of the Italian Communist Party. It describes how his attention to issues of space developed alongside Fascism's rise as a state form. It considers how his unfinished 1926 essay, "Some Aspects of the Southern Question," offered a historical and geographical method for analyzing the dialectics of class, region, and political economy and culture—including the articulation of race and class. Though this article includes "The Southern Question," as well as critical engagements with it, it does not limit his contributions to geography to this one piece. It specifically foregrounds Gramsci's method of conjunctural analysis articulated in the *Prison Notebooks*. It features an array of geographers who have engaged with his work, and also includes spatial thinkers across the disciplines who approach Gramscian concepts and categories. Amid a global resurgence of white nationalism and neofascism, and what, following Gramsci, we might call an "organic crisis" of US hegemony and global capitalism on a world scale, engagements with Gramsci's spatial insights are warranted.

# **Historical-Geographical Context**

"Some Aspects of the Southern Question," as Joseph A. Buttigieg notes, contains the most-essential elements of Gramsci's theory of geography and politics (Gramsci 1992–2007). Gramsci penned the unfinished essay in October 1926 to intervene in debates within the Italian Communist Party, a party he was a founding member of, and in its relationship to workers and peasants (Gramsci 2000). The essay argues for the necessity of building broad class alliances in the struggle against Fascism. It is difficult to know how Gramsci would have concluded the essay. Its writing was cut short when he was arrested on 8 November 1926. Mussolini's Fascist government had declared a state of emergency earlier that month (Fiori 1971). Gramsci was prevented from attending a parliamentary session scheduled for the day after his arrest. He was arrested despite his parliamentary immunity and imprisoned on the Island of Ustica alongside almost a thousand other political prisoners (Gramsci 1994a). (1) During the six weeks of his confinement, Gramsci taught history and geography at a political education school that had been self-organized by the incarcerated revolutionaries at Ustica (2) his *Letters from Prison* depict a range of relevant experiences critical for understanding him as a spatial thinker (Gramsci 1994a);. They reveal the evolution of his political thought from his time as student at the University of Turin, as a journalist, and as a theorist of the factory councils in Turin's factories after the Bolshevik Revolution of 1917. The letters document his long struggle to secure the conditions to pen what would become his magisterial carceral studies project, the *Prison Notebooks*. He describes being denied the capacity to write and study until February 1929, when he is finally able to begin, over two years after his initial confinement. Throughout the writing of his notebooks during the 1930s he worked to adopt a more "disinterested" point of view than his previous writing (Gramsci 1994a, and Gramsci 1994b). His prison writings and letters clarify how Gramsci was able to transform the prison as a space of Fascist confinement into a space of revolutionary writing (Gramsci 1992–2007). His notebooks would be posthumously published in Italian during the 1940s, and then the most important selected notebooks in English in the 1970s (Gramsci 1971). Though Gramsci's theoretical contributions to understanding the geographical foundations of political economy and culture have been well studied, important sections of his prison writings have not yet been translated into English. Important new work in Gramscian studies has drawn attention to the significance of the Italian philological scholarship for interpreting the texts as well the politics of the period (Loftus 2019, Gramsci 2021, Brennan 2006, and Thomas 2009, the last two cited under Gramsci's Heirs). This section concludes with work suggesting the relevance of his prison writing for understanding the articulation of race and class in different historical and geographic conjunctures (Hall 1980).

#### **Fiori, Giuseppe.** *Antonio Gramsci: Life of a Revolutionary***. New York: E. P. Dutton, 1971.**

This biography is a vital resource for understanding Gramsci's relevance for the study of geography. First published in Italian, it includes a discussion of his writing of his unfinished essay, "Some Aspects of the Southern Question," composed in 1926. As it describes, the essay marked a transition to the themes that he focused on in the *Prison Notebooks*, including the role of the intellectual, 19th-century Italian history, democracy, and "hegemony."

### **Gramsci, Antonio.** *Selections from the Prison Notebooks***. Edited and translated by Quintin Hoare and Geoffrey Nowell Smith. New York: International Publishers, 1971.**

This classic volume introduced Gramsci's political theory to the English-speaking world. It contains an extended reflection on the relationship between the defeat of the Italian working-class movement after World War I, the rise of Fascism in Italy, and the role of intellectuals in struggles for hegemony. It introduces his method of conjunctural analysis and his approach to understanding the various moments in the "relations of forces" in specific historical and geographical contexts.

# **Gramsci, Antonio.** *Prison Notebooks***. Vols. 1–3. Edited with introduction by Joseph A. Buttigieg. New York: Columbia University Press, 1992–2007.**

This is the most complete English edition of Gramsci's *Prison Notebooks*. It is based on the model of the *Quaderni del carcere* edited by Valentino Gerratana at the Institutto Gramsci. As Buttigieg's authoritative introduction notes, the *Notebooks* show Gramsci moving beyond the propaganda and polemics required by the Communist Party, when he had been a journalist, and toward the development of the "philosophy of praxis."

### **Gramsci, Antonio.** *Letters from Prison***. Vol. 1. Edited by Frank Rosengarten. Translated by Raymond Rosenthal. New York: Columbia University Press, 1994a.**

This volume gathers Gramsci's prison letters from 1926 to 1930. It features correspondence with his family and comrades, particularly his sister-in-law Tatiana Schucht and friend economist Piero Sraffa, who provided Gramsci's access to books and reading and writing materials. They also depict the surveillance imposed the Fascist prison regime, as well as the impacts on his health. Taken together, they testify to the depth of his commitment as a spatial thinker and politician.

### **Gramsci, Antonio.** *Letters from Prison***. Vol. 2. Edited by Frank Rosengarten. Translated by Raymond Rosenthal. New York: Columbia University Press, 1994b.**

This second volume gathers prison letters penned by Gramsci between 1931 and 1937. It provides a distinct primary source for understanding the punitive and authoritarian character of carceral spaces in Fascist Italy. It captures his ostracism from former comrades over disagreements with the party line. This was also the period when he engaged in the most-intense writing of his *Prison Notebooks* even as his health deteriorated.

### **Gramsci, Antonio.** *The Antonio Gramsci Reader: Selected Writings, 1916–1935***. Edited by David Forgacs. Introduction by Eric Hobsbawm. New York: New York University Press, 2000.**

This skillfully edited volume includes key works penned by Gramsci for understanding his geographical imagination, including essential preprison writings such as "Workers and Peasants" and "Some Aspects of the Southern Question." It also features his prison writings, a chronological outline, a glossary, and suggestions of secondary works in English. It illustrates critical aspects of Gramsci's geographical thought, particularly how conjunctural analysis enables concrete analysis of political, economic, and ideological forces in specific geographies.

### **Gramsci, Antonio.** *Subaltern Social Groups: A Critical Edition of Prison Notebook 25***. Edited and translated by Joseph A. Buttigieg and Marcus Green. Introduction by Marcus E. Green. New York: Columbia University Press, 2021.**

This important volume features the entirety of Gramsci's notes on subaltern social groups penned during his imprisonment. It makes available the English translation of his "special" *Notebook 25* and related materials, which had been unavailable. It demonstrates the centrality of the concept of subalternity to his analysis of race and class, religion, the "southern question," and "common sense." In doing so, it shows how Gramsci conceived subaltern groups as a "category of analysis" to understand material conditions and politics.

### **Hall, Stuart. "Race, Articulation, and Societies Structured in Dominance." In** *Sociological Theories: Race and Colonialism***. Edited by United Nations Education, Scientific and Cultural Organization, 305–345. Paris: UNESCO, 1980.**

This essay intervenes in the race and class debates that rocked Britain, South Africa, the United States, and beyond in the late 1970s and early 1980s. It mobilizes Gramsci to better understand how "race" can be theorized as "the modality in which class is lived." It offers a historically and geographically specific way of understanding and theorizing the political purpose of racism as a "cementing ideology," one that "secures" the entire social formation in particular conjunctures.

### **Loftus, Alex. "Gramsci as Historical Geographical Materialist." In** *Revisiting Gramsci's Notebooks***. Edited by Francesca**

### **Antonini, Aaron Bernstein, Lorenzo Fusaro, and Robert Jackson, 9–22. Leiden, The Netherlands: Brill, 2019.**

This essay rigorously explores how Gramsci's analyses of geography, place, and common sense can reinvigorate historical geographical materialism. It suggests the multiple geographical contributions made by the "Sardinian," as well as the need to "spatialize historical materialism." It calls for more-focused analyses of the *Prison Notebooks* and the contributions the "philosophy of praxis" makes to a "spatialized Marxism."

### **Santucci, Antonio A.** *Antonio Gramsci***. Preface by Eric Hobsbawm. Foreword by Joseph Buttigieg. New York: Monthly Review, 2010.**

This indispensable study powerfully depicts Gramsci's "theoretical contributions to the solution of the Southern question." It also demonstrates his contributions to debates about the roots of geographical divisions between the North and the South in the uneven development of capitalism. It suggests that Gramsci was the first to illustrate how the "national question" was internal to "workers' movements and parties," which, it argues, represents his most original contribution to Marxist political theory.

# **Historical and Geographical Engagements**

Gramsci's relevance for the study of geography is not only as a political theorist, but as a socialist. After all, as Eric Hobsbawm observed, Gramsci is one of the few Marxist theorists with the practical experience of being a leader of a mass-based movement. In turn, he went on to become one of the most cited critical theorists in the humanities and social sciences (Hobsbawm 2007). Gramsci's reflections on the failures of his party to exercise leadership in the democratic movement against Fascism led him to develop his theory of hegemony. As Gramsci's observations in the *Prison Notebooks* suggest, there was no hegemonic vision that had cohered among the workers and peasants in the aftermath of World War I in Italy. Instead, the Fascist ruling class secured a hegemonic consensus; that is, leadership in the realm of ideas secured through control over institutions including the media, the church, and education (Rudé 1995). Gramsci argued that these were specific sites and places where hegemony was struggled over, won, or lost. The concept emphasized the centrality of moral and intellectual leadership rather than simply dominance. Beyond the available theories of the Far Left, he argued for the development of better theories to confront the hegemony of the Far Right. He was attendant to possibilities of social change illuminated through conjunctural analysis of concrete moments and geographical situations (Hart 2002, Said 1995). Inspired by Stuart Hall's and Gillian Hart's provocations to consider Gramsci's relevance for the study of race, class, and region during the transition to neoliberalism in Britain and South Africa, this essay suggests his relevance for geography in our own time (Hall 1986, Hart 2020). This section highlights works that take up Gramsci to examine struggles over meaning and material conditions in particular places in the neoliberal era (Hart 2002). They suggest Gramsci's relevance for the renewal of the global justice Left (Ekers, et al. 2013). These texts aim to enable readers to develop the methodological skills to read Gramsci within his historical and geographical conjuncture, and to argue for the need to "translate" his insights to understand the specificities of our own (Said 1994, Kipfer 2002). In this way, they convey the relationship between consent and coercion during the neoliberal turn (Harvey 2005). At the same time, the essay clarifies how his "spatio-historical method" of conjunctural analysis, to use Hart's term, makes decisive contributions to ongoing assessments of the articulation between race and class, nationalism, the imperial state, and capitalist political economy (Hart 2020).

# **Ekers, Michael, Gillian Hart, Stefan Kipfer, and Alex Loftus, eds.** *Gramsci: Space, Nature, Politics***. Malden, MA: Wiley-Blackwell, 2013.**

This powerful volume brings together some of the most generative Gramscian scholarship in geography. It provides insights gleaned from Gramscian spatial and historical investigations from Brazil to Nepal to South Africa. It illustrates how Gramsci's socio-spatial method of conjunctural analysis enables more-rigorous understandings of politics in particular places and historical moments. It concludes by suggesting how Gramsci's method continues to resonate amid the resurgence of the racist Right and nationalist extremisms from the United States to South Africa and beyond.

#### **Hall, Stuart. "Gramsci's Relevance for the Study of Race and Ethnicity."** *Journal of Communications Inquiry* **10.2 (1986): 5–27.**

This essential essay illuminates the relevance of Gramsci's core concepts and categories, particularly his attention to the "complex dialectic of class and regional factors," for theorizing racisms in the late 20th century. It famously argues that Gramsci's relevance stems from his "emphasis on historical specificity," and his innovative approach to questions of ideology, popular culture, and social formation. It demonstrates the political stakes in theorizing the precise forms that racisms take in particular conjunctures.

### **Hart, Gillian.** *Disabling Globalization: Places of Power in Post-apartheid South Africa***. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2002.**

This groundbreaking work of Gramscian geography offers a conjunctural analysis of the politics of place during the neoliberal turn in South Africa. It utilizes Gramsci's method to analyze this specific "historical and geographical conjuncture." It demonstrates the ongoing relevance of his critique of economism and opportunism. It explores how his "theory of praxis" provides an opening for understanding the political possibilities for social change in "multiple arenas of everyday life."

**Hart, Gillian. "Why Did It Take So Long: Trump-Bannonism in a Global Conjunctural Frame." In** *Special Issue: Innovation in the Periphery: Nordic and International Perspectives***. Edited by Katrin Grossmann and Alan Mallach.** *Geografiska Annaler: Series B, Human Geography* **102.3 (2020): 239–266.**

This original and generative essay consides the resurgence of racist nationalisms and right-wing populist politics since the 1990s. It provides a global conjunctural analysis of these forces in South Africa, India, and the United States. It does so through an investigation of "global conjunctural moments," or "turning points," where an accumulation of political, economic, and ideological forces at different geographical scales come together to produce "new conditions with worldwide implications."

#### **Harvey, David.** *A Brief History of Neoliberalism***. New York: Oxford University Press, 2005.**

This study offers an historical-geographical materialist analysis of the neoliberal turn in global capitalism. It draws on Gramsci's concept of "common sense" to interrogate how "consent" to "coercion" has been secured to the "restoration of class power." It shows how the neoliberal state deployed extensive coercive measures to "reshape common sense" in the wake of the New York City fiscal crisis in the 1970s, measures that it suggests led it become an epicenter of the neoliberal turn.

#### **Hobsbawm, Eric.** *How to Change the World: Reflections on Marx and Marxism***. New Haven, CT: Yale University Press, 2007.**

This book includes two chapters situating Gramsci's interventions and his reception amid debates about the relevance of Marxism. Chapter 12, "Gramsci," argues that Gramsci is "the most original thinker produced in the West since 1917," at the same time as it warns against limiting him to "Western Marxism." Chapter 13, "The Reception of Gramsci," traces his international reception of his writings on the Left and beyond.

**Kipfer, Stefan. "Urbanization, Everyday Life and the Survival of Capitalism: Lefebvre, Gramsci and the Problematic of**

### **Hegemony."** *Capitalism Nature Socialism* **13.2 (2002): 117–149.**

This generative article examines the urban dimensions of antiglobalization protests in the late 20th and early 21st centuries. It establishes connections between Gramsci and Henri Lefebvre to "translate" their theoretical insights for our "own times." These intellectuals' experiences with a "dialectic of defeat" led them to develop new ways of building anticapitalist projects during difficult periods, particularly through an analysis of hegemony as the "process through which capitalism survives despite itself."

### **Rudé, George.** *Ideology and Popular Protest***. Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1995.**

This crucial work of Marxist social history, first published in 1980, conveys how Gramsci's analysis was singular in its argument that Fascism was a product of a failed revolution in Italy. It explores Gramsci interrogation of the inability of the Italian working-class movement in the North to win the consent of the peasants of the South to a proletarian program. It shows how this spatial dynamic shaped Gramsci's elaboration of the concept of hegemony.

#### **Said, Edward.** *Culture and Imperialism***. New York: Vintage Books, 1994.**

This influential work of postcolonial theory offers a read of Gramsci's unfinished essay "Some Aspects of the Southern Question," and his *Prison Notebooks*. It argues the texts represented a shift from Gramsci's journalism to the more focused political-theoretical work in the *Prison Notebooks*, where he depicted the "territorial, spatial, geographical foundations of social life." In doing so, it shows that Gramsci provided a crucial "geographical model" for the study of culture and imperialism.

### **Said, Edward. "History, Literature, and Geography." In** *Reflections on Exile***. By Edward Said, 453–473. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1995.**

This generative chapter suggests Gramsci's political writings offered a "geographical, territorial apprehension of human history and society." It examines how Gramsci conceived politics as a "contest over territory," and therefore geography as something to be struggled over. Gramsci articulated a "critical consciousness," one that was "geographical and spatial in its fundamental coordinates." In this way, it demonstrates how Gramsci was sensitive to the "concrete intellectual work" required of politics "embedded in actual geographical situations."

# **Conjunctural Interventions**

Gramsci introduced the historical and geographical methodology of conjunctural analysis in his *Prison Notebooks*. It provides methodological criteria for evaluations of the historically specific and contingent relationships among political, economic, ideological, and geographical factors in shaping the social formation. "When an historical period comes to be studied," he explained, "the great importance of the distinction becomes clear." Analyzing dramatic moments of crisis, he argued, would illuminate "the political forces which are struggling to conserve and defend the existing structure." For him, "These incessant and persistent efforts (since no social formation will ever admit that it has been superseded) form the terrain of the 'conjunctural.' And it is upon this terrain that the forces of opposition organise." In other words, the purpose of conjunctural analysis is to enable a concrete analysis of concrete politicaleconomic events in order to effect change. Despite the constraints of prison life, Gramsci developed a conjunctural analysis of the rise of Fascism. In doing so, he developed a spatial strategy for confronting it. Drawing on and elaborating Gramsci's interventions, Stuart Hall and his colleagues Chas Critcher, Tony Jefferson, John Clarke, and Brian Roberts emphasized the importance of conjunctural analysis of race and class, the state, and political culture and economy (Hall, et al. 2013; first published in 1978). They did so alongside global revolutionary thinkers stirred by Gramsci's theory of hegemony and the state (Poulantzas 1979). Their emphasis on historical and geographical specificity has informed Gramscian analyses of the articulations of race, class, gender, and sexuality across the humanities and social sciences in the late 20th and early 21st centuries (Carby 1999) This period has also been marked by decisive interventions to confront the apartheid state in South Africa, authoritarian state in Britain, Fascism in India, the military dictatorship in Brazil, the neoliberal carceral state in the United States, and beyond (Saul and Gelb 1981, Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies 1982, Gilmore 2007, Hart 2014, Camp 2016). Taken together, these works underscore the "geographical universality" and ongoing relevance of his theories of the state, democracy, and socialism (Coutinho 2012).

#### **Ahmad, Aijaz. "Fascism and National Culture: Reading Gramsci in the Days of Hindutva."** *Social Scientist* **21.3 (1993): 32–68.**

This essay provides an influential read of Gramsci, India in the 1990s, and the "space that conjoins . . . his world and ours." Drawing on Gramsci, it describes how colonial divides between the North and South were "felt" as a "racial difference." It argues that Gramsci's conception of Fascism as a resolution of capitalist crisis is essential to understanding the popular base of Fascist forces in India since the 1990s.

### **Camp, Jordan T.** *Incarcerating the Crisis: Freedom Struggles and the Rise of the Neoliberal State***. Oakland: University of California Press, 2016.**

This book, inspired by Gramsci and Hall, provides a conjunctural analysis of the rise of the "neoliberal carceral state" in the United States. It traces its emergence to a series of turnings points, including the rebellions in Watts and Detroit in the 1960s, the Attica revolt of 1971, and the Los Angeles uprising of 1992. It shows how the expansion of policing and prisons has become a "common sense" feature of life during this precise historical and geographical conjuncture.

### **Carby, Hazel V.** *Culture in Babylon: Black Britain and African America***. London: Verso, 1999.**

This generative book takes up and extends Gramsci's and Hall's interventions about common sense and hegemony to better understand how "moral panics" around race, gender, sexuality, and crime have depicted Black working-class women as a source of violence and disorder in urban spaces. These moral panics, it argues, have served to justify the policing of Black women's bodies in urban spaces throughout the 20th century.

### **Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies, ed.** *The Empire Strikes Back: Race and Racism in 70s Britain***. London: Hutchinson, 1982.**

This collaboratively produced work by cultural-studies scholars including Carby, Paul Gilroy, Pratibha Parmar, and John Solomos explores the articulations among racism, gender, the "organic crisis" of the capitalism, and the consolidation of "authoritarian statism." It provides a conjunctural analysis of how racism became a central strategy to manage the "effects of organic crisis" that took shape in the wake of the Brixton uprising in 1981, and the working-class rebellion it sparked.

### **Coutinho, Carlos Nelson.** *Gramsci's Political Thought***. Foreword by Joseph Buttigieg. Chicago: Haymarket Books, 2012.**

This study of Gramsci's political theory considers the "geographical universality" of his thought. It situates the Gramscian tradition in Brazil from the perspective of a prominent radical scholar and translator of Gramsci's work in the region. In perhaps its most generative contribution to debates in Marxist theory, it shows that Gramsci's concept of hegemony provided a new theory of democracy *and*

socialism as the "*gradual* conquest of spaces."

### **Gilmore, Ruth Wilson.** *Golden Gulag: Prisons, Surplus, Crisis, and Opposition in Globalizing California***. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2007.**

This foundational book shows how unprecedented prison expansion in California represented a "geographical solution to political economic crisis," a crisis unleashed by shifts in the political economy that produced surplus "finance capital, land, labor, and state capacity." Prison expansion, it argues, spatially resolved the crisis through the "prison fix" in one of the largest economies on the planet. It also considers how movements render "critical already existing activity," as Gramsci put it.

### **Hall, Stuart, Chas Critcher, Tony Jefferson, John Clarke, and Brian Roberts.** *Policing the Crisis: Mugging, the State, and Law and Order***. London: Palgrave, 2013.**

This thirty-fifth-anniversary edition of this game-changing collaborative work of Marxist cultural studies, originally published in 1978, emphasizes the importance of conjunctural analysis for understanding the crisis of the 1970s. It suggests Gramsci's relevance stemmed from his confrontation with actually existing conditions and political situations. It theorizes how a tilt toward state authoritarianism stemmed from a "crisis of hegemony" for capital and the state. It demonstrates the centrality of racism in legitimating authoritarian solutions to crisis.

### **Hart, Gillian.** *Rethinking the South African Crisis: Nationalism, Populism, Hegemony***. Athens, GA: University of Georgia Press, 2014.**

This important study mobilizes Gramsci to provide a conjunctural analysis of the political situation in South Africa that took shape in the aftermath of the global economic crisis of 2008. It argues that this conjunctural moment has been marked by contested notions of nationalism and "de-nationalization," unprecedented concentrations of wealth, growing surplus populations, and the deployment of police to contain these problems in particular spaces. Bringing Gramsci into dialogue with Frantz Fanon and Henri Lefebvre, it clarifies how hegemony unraveled amid a populist turn in South Africa.

### **Poulantzas, Nicos.** *Fascism and Dictatorship: The Third International and the Problem of Fascism***. London: Verso, 1979.**

This critical text engages Gramsci to poignantly observe how the political crisis of the state gave rise to Fascist solutions. It explores how the uneven development of capitalism shaped the rise of Fascism in Germany and Italy. Its extension of Gramsci's theory of the state as the "crisis form of state" has transformed debates in radical geography and socialist theory. These insights demonstrate the importance of extending Gramsci's insights to understand Fascism as a "specific form of *exceptional* regime." First English-edition published in 1974.

### **Saul, John S., and Stephen Gelb. "The Crisis in South Africa: Class Defense, Class Revolution."** *Monthly Review* **33.3 (1981): 1–156.**

This highly original, if underappreciated, study extends Hall's Gramscian framework to theorize the "organic crisis of South Africa's racial capitalism" in the late 1970s and early 1980s. It argues the political economy of apartheid became "the political economy of a cheap labor supply." It suggests the implications of this conjunctural analysis of the situation for North American movements in solidarity with struggles against South African racial capitalism.

# **Gramsci, Hegemony, and the Critique of Political Economy**

As a leader of the Italian Communist Party, a member of the executive committee of the Communist International, and as a prisoner of the Fascist Italian state in the 1920s and 1930s, Gramsci grappled with the conjuncture marked by the defeat of the revolutionary workers movement and the ascendancy of Fascism. His *Prison Notebooks* took stock of the "morbid symptoms" in Italian political culture and economy that appeared amid the crisis of global capitalism during the Great Depression. The Communist movement had entered a new historical and geographical conjuncture, and, as Gramsci puts it, an understanding of the specificity of the "relations of force" was the key to developing the "analysis of situations" required to effectively intervene in this decisive moment. He explained that "an analysis of the balances of forces—at all levels—can only culminate in the sphere of hegemony and ethico-political relations." In this way, Gramsci conceived of hegemony as a dialectical moment of force and consent, rather than mere dominance (Arrighi 2007, Arrighi and Silver 1999). He warned that political struggles could not be read as simple expressions of economic crises, but rather that they should be understood as creating a "terrain" more favorable to "certain modes of thought," and "ways of posing and resolving questions." Analysis of the ways that bourgeois hegemony works through popular culture and nationalism, he argued, could inform the development of a theory of praxis. This conceptualization of hegemony has proven to be critical to understanding the uneven development of capitalism (Glassman 2009). Readings from this section are inspired by Gramscian critiques of political economy (Wainwright 2010). They consider his contributions to spatial theory (Jessop 2005). They include works expanding his definition of hegemony to understand the problem at the world scale (Silver 1995, Arrighi and Silver 1999). These scholars use a Gramscian framework to analyze critical turning points in the historical geography of the capitalist world system. In doing so, they suggest how the rise of the Far Right since the defeat of the United States in Vietnam and the collapse of Fordism is inconceivable outside the expansion of the globalizing "welfare-warfare state" since the early Cold War (Arrighi 2007). At the same time, this section suggests the central role of the US South and Southwest in transformations of US and global political economy and culture since the 1970s (Davis 1986, Woods 2017). As such, it clarifies the political stakes in theorizing how US hegemony has been reshaped around "economic and military interventions" (Davis 1986). It features scholarship attentive to the "southern question," race and class, and region (Portelli 1991). In doing so, it illuminates the importance of Gramsci's concept of hegemony for understanding antiracist and class struggles over meaning and material conditions in the United States and the world (Woods 2017).

### **Arrighi, Giovanni.** *Adam Smith in Beijing: Lineages of the Twenty-First Century***. New York: Verso, 2007.**

This dynamic book shows how and why the United States, the most powerful capitalist state, built the largest military apparatus in world history. It deploys a Gramscian analysis of how the "globalizing welfare-warfare state" has increased "the global reach of capitalism." At the same time, it shows how the United States' increase in military expenditure to contain civil rights and antiwar struggles contributed to a "terminal" crisis of US hegemony.

**Arrighi, Giovanni, and Beverly J. Silver, eds.** *Chaos and Governance in the Modern World System***. Minneapolis: University of Minnesota Press, 1999.**

This essential edited volume takes up and extends Gramsci to theorize a series of "hegemonic transitions" that lead to "systemic chaos," or moments of severe breakdown marked by intensifying social conflicts and distinct "spatio-historical" qualities. It clarifies how Gramsci's concept of hegemony describes dominant groups' ability to maintain their legitimacy, not only by protecting their class interests but more centrally by presenting them as in the general interests of the society.

**Davis, Mike.** *Prisoners of the American Dream***. New York: Verso, 1986.**

This pathbreaking analysis locates the rise of the Far Right within shifts in the US political economy and culture since the 1970s. It traces its geographical roots in Southern California and the transformation of US hegemony toward more-unilateral military interventions, which led to greater military expenditures in the "Sunbelt." It contextualizes how the Right rose to power in a crisis where, as Gramsci put it, the "old is dying and the new cannot be born."

### **Glassman, Jim. "Hegemony." In** *International Encyclopedia of Human Geography***. Edited by Rob Kitchin and Nigel Thrif, 80– 90. Amsterdam: Elsevier, 2009.**

While Gramsci was not a professional geographer, this article insists on the geographical insights of his arguments. His insistence on the historical and geographical specificity of Italian situation, it argues, remains one of his most-lasting contributions, one that informed his theory of hegemony. This concept is essential to understanding the uneven development of capitalism and the role of intellectuals in political and class struggles in particular geographical contexts.

### **Jessop, Bob. "Gramsci as Spatial Theorist."** *Critical Review of International Social and Political Philosophy* **8.4 (2005): 421– 437.**

This essay illuminates Gramsci's status as a "spatial theorist," even though it suggests Gramsci didn't "explicitly prioritise spatial thinking." It argues that Gramsci's "philosophy of praxis" involves both a "historicization" and "spatialization" of his core concepts and concerns. It suggests the relevance of his analyses of "common sense" and popular culture for understanding the historical and geographical specificity of social relations, as well as "their specific location in place, space, and scale."

### **Portelli, Alessandro.** *The Death of Luigi Trastulli and Other Stories***. Albany: State University of New York Press, 1991.**

This innovative book offers a historical analysis of the political cultures of two industrial places, in Italy's Umbria region and in Harlan County in eastern Kentucky. Written by one of the leading practitioners of oral history, it is attentive to race and class, the "southern question," and region. It defines fieldwork as an "experiment in equality." It utilizes Gramsci's theory of hegemony to understand the "class struggle for control of meaning" in particular places.

# **Silver, Beverly. "World-Scale Patterns of Labor-Capital Conflict: Labor Unrest, Long Waves, and Cycles of Hegemony."** *Review* **18.1 (1995): 155–192.**

This generative article traces the connection between world wars and waves of labor unrest from a world systems perspective. It defines world wars in Gramscian terms as "morbid symptoms" of a more general "crisis of hegemony." In so doing, it traces the relationship between waves of class struggle and labor protest and the "cycles of hegemony" that have shaped the history of the capitalist world system.

#### **Wainwright, Joel. "Was Gramsci a Marxist?"** *Rethinking Marxism* **22.4 (2010): 617–626.**

This article argues that Gramsci's "critique of economism" represents an "extension of Marx's critique of political economy." It explores how Gramsci disagreed with the thesis that the contradictions of capitalism would inevitably lead to revolution. It also discusses how he employed Marx's value theory in his theory of hegemony. In doing so, it suggests how Gramsci's theory of hegemony might deepen the critique of political economy.

**Woods, Clyde.** *Development Arrested: The Blues and Plantation Power in the Mississippi Delta***. Introduction by Ruth Wilson Gilmore. New York: Verso, 2017.**

This original and generative study examines competing development visions for the Mississippi Delta during the 19th and 20th centuries. It extends Gramsci's concept of "historical blocs" to understand the struggles between the "plantation bloc" and the "blues bloc" in the region. It also follows Gramsci's lead in conceptualizing blues musicians, writers, and singers as "organic intellectuals" who represent the interests and values of poor and working people in a struggle to build a society "where working-class knowledge and participatory democracy are truly treasured." First published in 1998.

**Woods, Clyde.** *Development Drowned and Reborn: The Blues and Bourbon Restorations in Post-Katrina New Orleans***. Edited by Jordan T. Camp and Laura Pulido. Athens, GA: University of Georgia Press, 2017.**

This posthumously published study is a historical geography of capitalist development and Black working-class resistance in New Orleans and the wider region. Covering over two centuries of political, cultural, and economic development, the book foregrounds a dialectic of Bourbonism and the blues. Woods deploys the Gramscian concept of Bourbon hegemony to better understand the historical and geographical roots of policies of "planned abandonment" deployed in the wake of Hurricane Katrina in 2005.

# **Americanism and US Hegemony**

During the long 20th century, US hegemony was transformed through an interstate system that enabled it to "act within the capitalist world as a state above other states." US hegemony was secured through the "spread of the revolution in industrial relations," referred to as "Fordism" (Arrighi 1981). In his writings on Americanism and Fordism in the *Prison Notebooks*, Gramsci deployed the concept of "Fordism" to illuminate how this mode of production created "complications, absurd positions, and moral and economic crises often tending toward catastrophe." Fordism was inaugurated with Henry Ford's introduction of the five-dollar, eight-hour workday for workers who passed the company's sociology exam. While the fiction of high wages was contradicted by the facts of everyday life in the industrial city, Fordism's combination of the wage relation with an intensification of exploitation at the point of production represented a crucial turning point in the history of capitalism, and the emergence of a new epoch (Camp 2016, cited under Conjunctural Interventions). In theorizing Fordism, Gramsci sought to understand how the structure of US power had entered into a crisis during the 1930s. While there has been some reluctance to engage with Gramsci by scholars of US political geography and political economy and culture, there are a number of resources available for understanding Gramsci's relevance for the study of Americanism and US hegemony (Roediger 1988, Lipsitz 1988, Denning 1996). This section examines how Socialists in the United States confronted the Fordist system during the period in which Gramsci produced his prison writings (Roediger 1988, Denning 1996). It considers how labor and social movements in the radical 1930s represented a kind of "passive revolution," which failed to fundamentally alter the relations of power and "haunted" subsequent generations (Denning 2004). At the same time, it highlights work on the role of "organic intellectuals" in Black freedom, labor, and Socialist movements in the age of US hegemony between the 1940s and 1970s (Lipsitz 1988, Ransby 2003). It considers how the crisis of US hegemony in the 1970s took place due to the failure to secure consent in the United States and in the periphery (Arrighi 1981). In turn, it showcases conjunctural analyses of the age of neoliberal globalization, mass incarceration, and the policing of the planet (Denning 2004, Peck 2006, Gilmore and Gilmore 2007, Camp and Heatherton 2016). In doing so, it suggests Gramscian geographical analyses of racism, capitalism, and U. S. counterinsurgency are vital to understanding forms of global hegemony specific to the neoliberal era (Camp and Greenburg 2020).

**Arrighi, Giovanni. "A Crisis of Hegemony." In** *Dynamics of Global Crisis***. Edited by Samir Amin, Giovanni Arrighi, Andre Gunder Frank, and Immanuel Wallerstein, 55–108. New York: Monthly Review Press, 1981.**

This chapter traces the crisis of the world political economy established under US hegemony in the aftermath of World War II. It examines how this crisis took shape in response to the revolutionary events of 1968. In turn, it shows how the world monetary crisis and the US defeat in Vietnam undermined the institutional basis of its hegemony, since it was unable to secure consent in a "key region of the periphery."

**Camp, Jordan T., and Jennifer Greenburg. "Counterinsurgency Reexamined: Racism, Capitalism, and U. S. Military Doctrine."** *Antipode* **52.3 (2020): 430–451.**

This article examines how racist constructions of enemies have been critical to US counterinsurgency projects from the late 19th century to the present. It shows how these constructions have underpinned the production of the world's largest carceral-warfare state. Employing a Gramscian analysis, it explores how racism and counterinsurgency have enabled capitalism to survive challenges to its hegemony. It shows how these dynamics work through an analysis of the "relations of force."

**Camp, Jordan T., and Christina Heatherton, eds.** *Policing the Planet: Why the Policing Crisis Led to Black Lives Matter***. New York: Verso, 2016.**

This collection describes struggles against policing from New York to London, San Juan, San Salvador, and beyond. It utilizes a Gramscian methodological approach inspired by the Birmingham School in suggesting that the outcome of the policing crisis is not determined in advance but is instead the product of a struggle. Guided by the work of organizers and activists, it considers the policing of the planet as a central political and intellectual challenge of our time.

**Denning, Michael.** *The Cultural Front: The Laboring of American Culture in the Twentieth Century***. New York: Verso, 1996.**

This essential monograph provides a Gramscian analysis of the rise of the "Popular Front social movement" in the 1930s and 1940s. It offers a conjunctural analysis of the "Age of the Congress of Industrial Workers," the New Deal, and the culture of labor and socialist movements. It illuminates how Socialist writers, artists, and intellectuals built an "historical bloc" or alliance with the multiracial Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO) to transform the "common sense" of this decisive conjuncture.

### **Denning, Michael.** *Culture in the Age of Three Worlds***. New York: Verso, 2004.**

This decisive book suggests how the radical 1930s represented a kind of "American Risorgimento," or a "passive revolution from above" that failed to fundamentally transform social relations and "haunted later generations," just as the Risorgimento of the 1860s and 1870s did in Gramsci's time. It offers methodological criteria critical for understanding how the struggle over the memory and meaning of the thirties shaped social movements in the period between 1948 and 1989.

**Gilmore, Ruth Wilson, and Craig Gilmore. "Restating the Obvious." In** *Indefensible Space: The Architecture of the National Security State***. Edited by Michael Sorkin, 141–161. New York: Routledge, 2007.**

This indispensable chapter provides a theory of the neoliberal state, or what it terms the "anti-state state," in the era of the globalization and the prison-industrial complex. It defines the state as a "territorially bounded" set of "institutions that change over time." Following Gramsci and Hall, it illustrates the centrality of prisons in establishing state legitimacy. It shows how ideologies of "race" purported to justify unprecedented prison expansion in the United States.

#### **Lipsitz, George.** *A Life in the Struggle: Ivory Perry and the Culture of Opposition***. Philadelphia: Temple University Press, 1988.**

This influential biography considers how Black organic intellectual and civil rights activist Ivory Perry helped direct the aspirations of the poor and exploited Black workers in the specific geography of St. Louis, Missouri. It argues that "organic intellectuals" such as Perry should be understood as "permanent persuaders" in Black freedom and labor struggles in the city and region. It illustrates how shifts in the relations of race and class power emerge from social conflicts in a particular time and place.

### **Peck, Jamie. "Liberating the City: Between New York and New Orleans."** *Urban Geography* **27.8 (2006): 681–713.**

This timely essay focuses on how neoliberal intellectuals associated with the Manhattan Institute, a think tank based in New York City, waged a "battle of ideas" amid the fiscal crisis in New York in the 1970s. It demonstrates how these intellectuals transformed an unnatural social crisis after Hurricane Katrina in New Orleans in 2005 into a neoliberal program based on the planned shrinkage of the public sector in New York.

### **Ransby, Barbara.** *Ella Baker and the Black Freedom Movement: A Radical Democratic Vision***. Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 2003.**

This dynamic biography of freedom movement activist Ella Baker conceptualizes her role as an organic intellectual. It suggests how her knowledge emerged from the political cultures of grassroots struggles in particular places from Harlem in the radical 1930s to the Jim Crow South in the 1960s. Throughout, it depicts how she exercised moral and intellectual leadership in the Black freedom struggle in this decisive moment.

# **Roediger, David. "Americanism and Fordism—American Style: Kate Richards O'Hare's 'Has Henry Ford Made Good?'"** *Labor History* **29.2 (1988): 241–252.**

This essay demonstrates how the writings of US Socialist author Kate Richards O'Hare depicted the Fordist system in the St. Louis– based Socialist magazine the *National Rip-Saw*, in the period before Gramsci penned his famous prison writings on Americanism and Fordism. Her writing was appreciative of the ways in which Fordism effected "productive advances and human transformation," but also failed to fully account for the "extreme alienation" of "Fordist mass production."

# **Gramsci's Heirs**

Writing amid the crisis of the 1930s, Gramsci argued that Fascism had come to power by appeals to the petty bourgeoisie. This was the social basis of Fascism, which had found a "form of organization" that enabled the bourgeoisie to unite with the middle stratum, which had "lacked any structure or unifying ideology" before. In fact, Benito Mussolini represented the "concentrated essence of the Italian petty bourgeois" (Fiori 1971, cited under Historical-Geographical Context). Gramsci's warning about Fascism's popular appeal to the lower middle class or petty bourgeoise is relevant to understanding the class basis of resurgent "authoritarian populism" and racist nationalisms in the neoliberal era. The consolidation of "authoritarian statism" has been accompanied by the production of a historically specific racist and nationalist "common sense" (Hall 1988, Poulantzas 2000). In using the concept of common sense as Gramsci did to describe the generally held assumptions and beliefs common to any society, scholars insist that attention must be paid to its role in the rise of authoritarian state forms. In doing so, they clarify the problem with defining hegemony as mere domination or even class power (Arrighi 2010). In a mid-2010s essay by Perry Anderson, the author of the influential "Antinomies of Antonio Gramsci" published in the *New Left Review* in 1976, he identifies a range of scholars who represent Gramsci's heirs in the current period (Anderson 2016). He includes Giovanni Arrighi, Ranajit Guha, Stuart Hall, and Ernesto Laclau, whose works are included in this section (Laclau 1977, Hall 1988, Guha 1999, Arrighi 2007). This section also highlights other neglected examples of Gramsci's heirs in radical geography and political and social theory (Poulantzas 2000, Berger 1998, Brennan 2006, Thomas 2009, and Loftus 2019, the last cited under Historical-Geographical Context), as well as Gramscian geographies of social and ecological crises in the 21st century (Ekers, et al. 2009).

#### **Anderson, Perry. "The Heirs of Gramsci."** *New Left Review* **100.2 (2016): 71–97.**

Published on the fortieth anniversary on his famous essay "The Antinomies of Gramsci," Anderson's "The Heirs of Gramsci" suggests that Gramsci has no peer in Marxist theory. It explains that Gramsci's significance was also geographical, "since Italy combined an advanced capitalist industry in the North with an archaic pre-capitalist society in the South." It considers Arrighi, Guha, Laclau, and Hall as the most-prominent engagements with Gramsci's political thought since the 1980s.

#### **Arrighi, Giovanni.** *The Long Twentieth Century: Money, Power and the Origins of Our Times***. London: Verso, 2010.**

This book, originally published in 1994, is an exemplar of the tradition of Gramscian world systems analysis. It uses Gramsci's concept of hegemony to move beyond the nation-state as the unit of analysis in theorizing the world system as a whole. It introduces the concept of "world hegemony" to describe leadership and "governance over a system of sovereign states," rather than the more common use of the term as synonymous with dominance and coercion.

### **Berger, John. "An Open Letter to Subcommandante Marcos in the Mountains of Southeast Mexico."** *Los Angeles Times***, 4 January 1998.**

This letter to the spokesperson of the Zapatista movement in Chiapas, Mexico, suggests that Gramsci was the "least dogmatic" theorist of revolution in the 20th century. It explores how his experience growing up in Sardinia shaped his theories of time, place, and condition. It is particularly sensitive to role of Sardinia in the development of his theories of revolutionary change. Gramsci's geographical consciousness came from a way of knowing he developed growing up as southerner, one that he then elaborated as a revolutionary theorist.

#### **Brennan, Timothy.** *Wars of Position: The Cultural Politics of the Left and Right***. New York: Columbia University Press, 2006.**

This crucial book demonstrates Gramsci's resonance for critical theory in the present moment. It suggests that his theory of intellectuals articulated in "Some Aspects of the Southern Question" and elaborated in the *Prison Notebooks* challenges postcolonial readings of Gramsci, which, it argues, neglect "context and detail." It considers the significance of philological readings of Gramsci's writing on race, colonialism, and the "southern intellectual" for the renewal of critical theory.

# **Ekers, Michael, Alex Loftus, and Geoff Man. "Gramsci Lives." In** *Themed Issue: Understanding Networks at the Science-Policy Interface***. Edited by Michael Ekers, Alex Loftus, and Geoff Man.** *Geoforum* **40.3 (2009): 287–291.**

This editorial introduction to a special issue on Gramscian political ecologies suggests his relevance for "transformative politics" in the current moment. It outlines some of the broad contours of Gramscian ecological research, including a commitment to using Gramsci's concepts to understand social and environmental crisis. It suggests how engaging with his writings enable new approaches to political ecology. It considers how the terrain on which "hegemonic struggles are waged has been recast."

### **Guha, Ranajit.** *Elementary Aspects of Peasant Insurgency in Colonial India***. Durham, NC: Duke University Press, 1999.**

This study takes up and extends Gramsci's analysis of spontaneous struggles to better understand the "traces of consciousness" apparent in the movements of the peasant masses in India under colonialism. It utilizes Gramsci's interventions about the "subaltern" to better understand the history of peasant insurgencies from the late 1700s to 1900. It shows that the consciousness that motivates peasant revolts and insurrections has often been misconstrued in the hegemonic narrative of counterinsurgency.

#### **Hall, Stuart.** *The Hard Road to Renewal: Thatcherism and the Crisis of the Left***. London: Verso, 1988.**

This book offers the most distinct analysis of what it terms "authoritarian populism" in the age of Thatcherism in Britain. It uniquely theorizes the role of "moral panics" around race, crime, and law and order in producing the common sense underpinning consent to Thatcherism and authoritarian statism. It explains how this hegemonic project took shape in a distinct conjuncture, which it defines as less a "slice of time" and more an "accumulation/condensation of contradictions."

#### **Laclau, Ernesto.** *Politics and Ideology in Marxist Theory***. New York: Verso, 1977.**

This book engages with Gramsci's concept of hegemony to offer a new theory of articulation. It seeks to clarify the "type of relationship that can win popular masses," and the "class articulation" that is essential to the organization of hegemony. In turn, it develops a Gramscian theory of populism with reference to Latin America after the 1930s but does not limit the concept to "a determinate historical or geographical context."

#### **Poulantzas, Nicos.** *State, Power, Socialism***. London: Verso, 2000.**

This study, first published in 1978, utilizes a Gramscian theory of the state as a relationship of forces. It specifies that the state, like capital, is not "simply a relationship," but rather a "specific material condensation" of "relations between classes" and a mechanism for "organizing social space." Its conception of the emergence of "authoritarian statism" informs its analysis of the democratic transition to socialism. In doing so, it provides a geographical strategy for reorganizing social space.

### **Thomas, Peter D.** *The Gramscian Moment: Philosophy, Hegemony and Marxism***. Chicago: Haymarket Books, 2009.**

This careful study explores the theoretical conjuncture required for understanding Gramsci's political theory in his time and its significance for the development of a Marxist philosophical research program in the early 21st century. It particularly notes the importance of Gramsci's categories for ongoing debates about the state, globalization, imperialism, and crisis. Through a rigorous analysis of the *Prison Notebooks*, it highlights the signficance of philological readings for understanding the concept of hegemony as "philosophical fact."

# **The Current Crisis**

The current state of affairs represents a crisis of US hegemony and for capitalism on a global scale (Silver and Payne 2020, Bello 2021). Gramsci referred to crises of hegemony as moments where the "ruling class has lost its consensus"; that is, it is "no longer 'leading' but only 'dominant,' exercising coercive force alone." The ruling class has been unable to secure the consent it once did. The global economic crisis and the failure of the US state to contain the COVID-19 coronavirus pandemic amid widespread protest has "shaken the confidence of those in power" (Silver and Payne 2020). The resurgence of white Christian nationalist and violent reactionary forces is therefore not incidental or accidental to capitalism's survival. As has been the case in other places, such as India, South Africa, Brazil, and Britain, the proliferation of racist nationalisms in the United States in the neoliberal era has been part of a "contradictory hegemonic project" (Hart 2021). The task is to evaluate the extent to which Gramsci's theory of language can be "translated" to grasp the articulations of nationalist common sense and law and order in the present moment (Ives 2004). As entries in this section explore, the law-and-order agendas since the 1980s are evolving into a white Christian nationalist insurgency (Hart 2013). It is in this specific historical and geographical context that Gramscian scholarship on articulations of race and class continues to inform cutting-edge scholarship in radical geography and socialist theory (Chari 2017). After all, conjunctural analysis enables a systematic focus on struggles over meaning and material conditions in specific "historical and geographical conjunctures" (Ekers, et al. 2020). Reflecting on the relevance of Gramscian or conjunctural analysis to the early-21st-century crisis, the section concludes with work noting the importance of his theory of politics for social movements in South Africa, the United Kingdom, the United States, and the world (Hall and Massey 2010, Hart 2013, Denning 2021, Dainotto and Jameson 2020).

#### **Bello, Walden. "America Has Entered the Weimar Era." Foreign Policy in Focus, 7 January 2021.**

This essay explains how the 6 January 2021 siege on the US Capitol was a morbid symptom of a broader crisis of the United States. It suggests that the ideology of "American exceptionalism" unraveled amid the failure to contain the coronavirus pandemic, causing the country to suffer "from a profound loss of credibility." This crisis of authority, it warns, has led to a resurgence of right-wing paramilitary organizations, militias, and vigilante groups.

### **Chari, Sharad. "Three Moments of Stuart Hall in South Africa: Postcolonial-Postsocialist Marxisms of the Future."** *Critical Sociology* **43.6 (2017): 831–845.**

This study situates Stuart Hall's interventions in debates within the South African Left over race and class since the 1980s. It highlights the "Gramscian turn" in Hall's arguments and suggests the stakes in theorizing this "society structured in dominance" both in "spatial and temporal terms." In this way, it delineates how Hall's analysis of the "dialectics of space and time" is central to South African political projects seeking to break with "fatal repetitions of racial space."

#### **Dainotto, Robert M., and Frederic Jameson, eds.** *Gramsci in the World***. Durham, NC: Duke University Press, 2020.**

This essential anthology explores how Gramsci became what Jameson calls the "philosopher of the 'Southern question'" and the most profound political theorist of the workers' movement in the aftermath of World War I. It suggests how he developed a theory of history and geography that continues to shape leftist theory and practice across the world. In this way, the book illustrates the range of approaches taken by scholars demonstrating Gramsci's relevance in distinct geographies.

#### **Denning, Michael. "Everyone a Legislator."** *New Left Review* **129 (May–June 2021): 29–44.**

This vital article considers how Gramsci's "critique of political science" can inform resistance to far-right movements and regimes today. It suggests the importance of his theory of organizing for analyzing social movements in the United States from Joe Hill and the Wobblies in the 1910s to struggles following the police murders of George Floyd and Breonna Taylor in 2021. It explains how Gramsci's conception of "politics as organizing" has particular importance for "rethinking popular politics."

### **Ekers, Michael, Stefan Kipfer, and Alex Loftus. "On Articulation, Translation, and Populism: Gillian Hart's Postcolonial Marxism."** *Annals of the Association of American Geographers* **110.5 (2020): 1577–1593.**

This essay considers Gillian Hart's contributions to anticolonial Marxism and suggests how her deployment of Gramsci enables a focus on "both materiality and meaning in particular historical and geographical conjunctures." It highlights the political stakes in her work to translate Gramsci's insights from his conjuncture to understand resurgent right-wing populism and nationalism in the current conjuncture. It this way, it illuminates the importance of her postcolonial Marxism for critical geographical scholarship.

#### **Hall, Stuart, and Doreen Massey. "Interpreting the Crisis."** *Soundings* **44 (Spring 2010): 57–71.**

While there are different ways of interpreting the crisis that took shape after the financial crash of 2008, this piece intervenes that the most effective way is to theorize it as a conjuncture. This approach enables a focus on a specific conjuncture when the contradictions are "condensed," or fused into a kind of "ruptural unity." Crises are moments that open up the potential for radical social change, it argues; their resolutions are not determined in advance.

**Hart, Gillian. "Gramsci, Geography, and the Language of Populism." In** *Gramsci: Space, Nature, Politics***. Edited by Michael Ekers, Gillian Hart, Stefan Kipfer, and Alex Loftus, 301–320. Malden, MA: Wiley-Blackwell, 2013.**

This chapter provides a Gramscian analysis of populist politics in South Africa in the post-2008 conjuncture, which is ongoing. It illustrates how his theory of language is fundamental to interrogating the resurgence of nationalisms in the aftermath of apartheid. It theorizes how struggles in language over the meaning of the national liberation movement in the past relate to ongoing conflicts over "conditions of life and livelihood" in the present.

**Hart, Gillian. "Decoding 'the Base': White Evangelicals or Christian Nationalists?"** *Studies in Political Economy* **102.1 (2021): 61–76.**

This critical essay argues that Trumpism and white Christian nationalism in the United States needs to be understood as a "contradictory hegemonic project." It suggests how apartheid South Africa can be seen as a "precursor to conditions in the U. S.," particularly since the rise of Donald Trump in 2016. It compares white Christian nationalism in the United States to religious nationalism in India and South Africa, in order to understand the "unexceptional" and "profoundly contradictory character" of such racist nationalisms around the world.

#### **Ives, Peter.** *Language and Hegemony in Gramsci***. London: Pluto, 2004.**

This book explores Gramsci's theory of language and hegemony in his *Prison Notebooks*. It argues against definitions of hegemony limiting it to consent, and for an understanding of hegemony in relationship to struggles in language. It explains how Gramsci developed his analysis of hegemony and the "Southern Question" to theorize the role of language in the uneven capitalist development of northern and southern Italy.

**Silver, Beverly J., and Corey Payne. "Crises of World Hegemony and the Speeding Up of Social History." In** *Hegemony and World Order: Reimagining Power in World Politics***. Edited by Piotr Dutkiewicz, Tom Casier, and Jan Aart Scholte, 17–31. New York: Routledge, 2020.**

This timely essay offers a world systems analysis of ongoing social protests, which taken together—albeit not formulated organically—

represent a "crisis of US hegemony" and "for global capitalism" on a "scale not witnessed since the 1930s." This moment, it argues, has been marked by the largest refugee crisis since World War II, the resurgence of far-right movements, climate change, militarism, and unemployment. As this crisis deepens, coercive solutions to these problems will only deepen "systemic chaos."

# **Audio and Video**

Gramsci understood popular culture as a critical terrain of class struggle (Baratt 1994). In particular, he saw culture as essential to the production of consent (Alexander and Eadie 1987). His experiences growing up in Sardinia, organizing in Communist and Socialist movements in Turin, and writing in a Fascist prison shaped his theory of politics (Hobsbawm 1987). He promoted the geographical strategy of building broad popular alliances to defeat Fascism and restore democracy. In doing so, he uniquely theorized the role of intellectuals in the movement, arguing that intellectual and cultural activity itself was a form of political practice. This strategic conception of politics and culture continues to resonate amid neofascist and police violence in the United States and the world in the early 21st century (Denning 2018). This section features audio, video, and relevant cultural productions critical to understanding Gramscian geographies. These entries take up Gramsci's theoretical work in several ways: they offer new political understandings of the construction of regions, challenge common sense notions of geographical divisions as natural, and, in keeping with the "southern question," they critique the exclusion of migrant workers as inevitable (Monicelli 1963). They therefore offer important Gramscian insights for students and scholars of mass migration in the age of globalization (Mayo 2010). At the same time, the entries demonstrate Gramsci's critical influence on scholarship about the capitalist state and ideology, work that could inform the renewal of radical theory in the 21st century (Jhally 2012). In doing so, it suggests that Gramsci's intervention about "everyone being a legislator" is a key to movement building in this decisive conjuncture (Denning 2018).

**Alexander, Mike, and Doug Eadie, dirs.** *Gramsci: Everything That Concerns People***. Glasgow, Scotland: Channel 4, 1987. International Gramsci Society: Audio and Video.**

This documentary dramatizes the conjuncture that gave rise to Gramsci's "philosophy of praxis," and the moral and intellectual vision that animated his understanding of the "southern question." Through interviews with his comrades and contemporaries, it dramatizes key turning points in his life, including his formation in Sardinia, the ferment of his organizing factory councils in Turin, and his experiences in various spaces of confinement.

**Baratt, Giorgio, prod.** *New York and the Mystery of Naples: A Journey through Gramsci's World***. Rome: Le Rose e i Quaderni, 1994. International Gramsci Society: Audio and Video.**

This documentary illuminates how Gramsci saw the political, cultural, and economic forces emerging in the United States under Fordism as a terrain of struggle. It suggests how the country has been managed through the production of consent. Through interviews with critics and scholars including Giuseppe Fiori, Edward Said, and Cornel West, it suggests that it is impossible to study a country's cultural forms without an understanding of its political economy.

**Buttigieg, Joseph A. "Power, Consent, and Gramsci." Berkeley, CA: KPFA, 2004. International Gramsci Society: Audio and Video.**

In the wake of the global protests against the US invasion of Iraq in 2003, this vital lecture and interview considers popular uses of the concept of "civil society." It argues that most movement intellectuals have defined civil society as a site of opposition to the state, rather than as the terrain of hegemonic power and opposition to it, as Gramsci conceived of it. It suggests how an analysis of civil society informed by Gramsci might be developed.

### **Buttigieg, Joseph A. "Reading Gramsci: Philology and Critical Method." Msida: University of Malta, 2007. International Gramsci Society: Audio and Video.**

This lecture, occasioned by the seventieth anniversary of Gramsci's death, provides metholodigical criteria for reading his *Prison Notebooks*. It argues for a reading of his core concepts and theories with attendance to the critical consciousness that animated them. It identifies the centrality of geography in Gramsci's political thought and argues for the importance of his focus on specific situations. Therein, it concludes, lies the distinctiveness of his philological method.

### **Denning, Michael. "Everyone a Legislator: Reflections on the Legacy of Gramsci's Conception of Politics." Burnaby, BC: Department of Humanities, Simon Fraser University, 21 October 2018.**

This lecture extends Gramsci's insights about "everyone being a legislator" to analyze the current situation in the United States. It argues that conditions in the United States after the 2016 election resemble Italy in 1924, with the "specter of fascist and police violence" that marked the consolidation of "authoritarian populism" under Trump. It concludes that place-based struggles over workplaces, schools, and neighborhoods are the key to building social movements where "everyone is a legislator."

### **Hobsbawm, Eric. "Gramsci and Marxism." 1987. International Gramsci Society: Audio and Video.**

This talk was delivered on the fiftieth anniversary of Gramsci's death and was organized by *Marxism Today* in Britain. It is relevant for understanding Gramscian geographies, given that it situates the historical and geographical context for the publication of Gramsci's *Letters from Prison* and *Prison Notebooks*. It explains how Gramsci's experience growing up in the impoverished south of Italy, working in a mass party, and being incarcerated within a Fascist prison shaped his theory of politics.

#### **Jhally, Sut. "The Last Interview: Stuart Hall on the Politics of Cultural Studies." Media Education Foundation, 20 August 2012.**

This interview explains how the Gramscian tradition informed the development of *Policing the Crisis*, with its "more explicitly political edge" as well as its focus on race and class in the 1970s. It also describes the weakening of that political influence in the late 20th and early 21st centuries. At the same time, it suggests the Gramscian theoretical advances that were secured in this decisive conjuncture, ones that could be revisited in the renewal of this radical tradition of scholarship in the 21st century.

### **Mayo, Peter. "Gramsci, the Southern Question, and the Mediterranean." Work in Progress Seminar Series, University of Malta, 21 April 2010. International Gramsci Society: Audio and Video.**

This lecture situates Gramsci's unfinished essay "Some Aspects of the Southern Question" in the context of debates about the construction of regions. It suggests its ongoing relevance for building solidarity across the Global North and Global South in the age of globalization and mass migration. It describes educational strategies that challenge the notion that divisions between the North and the South are immutable and inevitable.

**Monicelli, Mario, dir.** *The Organizer***. Irvington, NY: Criterion Collection, 1963.**

This film depicts the class struggle in Turin during the late 19th century. Its central character is a labor organizer referred to as "the professor" who helps workers articulate their grievances and leads them in a strike. It includes a migrant worker who has come to Turin to secure waged work. In accordance with Gramsci's analysis of the "southern question," he is excluded by the other workers. He refuses to join the strike.
