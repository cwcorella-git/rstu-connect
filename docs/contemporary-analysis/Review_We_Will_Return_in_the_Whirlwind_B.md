## Institute of Race Relations ARUN KUNDNANI

*We Will Return in the Whirlwind: Black radical organizations, 1960–1975* By MUHAMMAD AHMAD (Chicago, Charles H. Kerr, 2007), 332 pp. Cloth \$35.00, Paper \$18.00.

*Liberated Territory: untold perspectives on the Black Panther Party* By YOHURU WILLIAMS and JAMA LAZEROW (Durham and London, Duke University Press, 2008), 304 pp. Cloth \$84.95, Paper \$23.95.

'Mr. Chairman, Ladies and Gentlemen, Black Power.'1 C. L. R. James's provocative opening statement to his 1967 talk in London spoke to Black radicals across the Atlantic world. James's words are key to comprehending the unity of antiimperialism, anti-racism and class struggle against 'racial capitalism' itself.2 During his lecture, James argued that autonomous Black struggles against racism and for political and economic rights in the United States were an attack on capital and 'such independent struggles were a contributory factor to the socialist revolution'.3 His words teach us much about the stakes in interpreting the *freedom dreams* of students, civil rights activists, radical intellectuals and workers asserting themselves as a people and a class*.* 4

*We Will Return in the Whirlwind: Black radical organizations*, *1960–1975* combines participant observation, interviews and survey research to produce a political history of Black radical organisations in the long 1960s. Author Muhammad Ahmad, formerly known as Maxwell Stanford, Jr, draws on his experience as a key theorist and organiser in the Black freedom struggle to elaborate an original examination of the historical moment. Combining insurgent knowledge with historical materialist analysis, Ahmad grapples with concrete case studies of the Student Non-Violent Coordinating Committee (SNCC), the Revolutionary Action Movement (RAM), the Black Panther Party (BPP) and the League of Revolutionary Black Workers (LRBW).

With an introduction by John Bracey, a prologue, six chapters and an important appendix on research methodology, this memoir/political history is a welcome contribution to the new historiography of the civil rights movement and the new Black Power studies literature.5 In his first chapter, Ahmad provides a 'context for change' by narrating the tradition of Black radicalism that informed a generation of historical consciousness and political commitments in the Black liberation movement during the 1960s and 1970s. Drawing on his memories and activities as a militant African–American activist who grew up in Philadelphia, he focuses on six historical personalities as fundamental to a generation's visions of freedom. By describing the influence of Queen Mother Audley Moore, Robert F. Williams, Malcolm X and James and Grace Lee Boggs, Ahmad renders vivid the intergenerational transfer of knowledge, ideology and tactics within the Black radical tradition. In recalling this tradition, Ahmad gives a special acknowledgement to Ella Baker's seminal contribution that the 'masses make the revolution'.

In the second chapter, Ahmad foregrounds the knowledge and experience of Black activists in SNCC to better understand how the student movement escalated the struggle against capitalist misery and white supremacy by linking concretely to the activity of rural Black workers and sharecroppers in Georgia and Mississippi. He provides a context to understand the impact of Ella Baker, Diane Nash and other Black women's articulations of the philosophy of social change. As part of this effort, he draws upon an insightful interview with long-time activist, scholar and poet Gloria House to reconsider the historical process that shaped SNCC's critique of whiteness in the context of racist violence. That Ahmad focuses on the activities and knowledge of Black women in the movement both extends the insights of the scholarship that no longer takes Black male preachers as the principal protagonists of the civil rights movement and challenges powerful regimes of representation in popular culture that allow a handful of male 'leaders' to stand in for the freedom movement. Thus his account brings us much closer to understanding the revolutionary consciousness of the Black masses themselves.

Max Stanford's theoretical and practical activity in RAM was crucial to revealing the connections between urban rebellions, student revolts and Third World social movements in the political imaginations of Black radical activists. Thus, it should not surprise radical historians that Ahmad's meditation on RAM in the third chapter is a crucial contribution to historical interpretations of the period, especially given the fact that so few have appreciated the ways in which the organisation linked Black and Third World struggles against empire. Stanford combined a Marxist framework gained during his study sessions with Queen Mother Audley Moore with the political philosophy of revolutionary nationalism and self-defence promoted by Robert Williams and Malcolm X in order to articulate a radical theory of the organisation. As Black radical historian Robin D. G. Kelley shows in his generative study *Freedom Dreams*, Stanford expressed a Black radicalism that theorised the struggles of African Americans in the context of shared global liberation struggles against racial capitalism and empire, before the eruption of the urban rebellions across the country. Ahmad, for example, provides vivid memories of his historic encounter with future LRBW members during a 1964 trip to Cuba to meet with Robert Williams, a trip which in turn inspired Ernie Allen to form a RAM in northern California. Allen was formerly part of the Afro-American Association at Berkeley and Merritt College, with Donald Warden, Cedric Robinson, Huey Newton, and Leslie and Jim Lacy, among others. Following the lead of anti-colonial freedom struggles erupting in what W. E. B. Du Bois called 'the darker nations',6 Ahmad recounts how Black artists, creative writers, poets, students and workers in RAM sought to articulate the radical content of the civil rights movement and urban rebellions. By considering the roles of Malcolm X as the international spokesperson, Robert F. Williams as the international chairman and Stanford as the national field chairman, we can begin to grasp the seriousness of purpose of RAM's goal of elevating the struggle against white supremacy and for political and economic rights.

In the fourth chapter, Ahmad discusses the alliances between SNCC and RAM that would lead to the emergence of the BPP. He explains that Huey Newton and Bobby Seale became politically involved in the struggle through the Afro-American Association at Merritt College in Oakland where they met Donald Warden. Ahmad writes that Newton and Seale joined the RAM group, the Soul Student Advisory Council, before forming the BPP in Oakland. Radicalised by the Watts insurrection of 1965, many young Blacks would identify with the critique of state violence advanced by the Oakland Panthers. Here Ahmad reflects on the tactical and strategic differences between the Panther approach to revolution and that of RAM. From 1965 on, RAM had forged political and military alliances with the Deacons for Self-Defense in the context of conflicts with southern police and the Klu Klux Klan in order to develop an underground revolutionary movement. Based on this experience, they called into question the Oakland Panthers tendency towards highly visible public confrontation. RAM critiqued what they viewed to be 'left adventurism', which they argued sparked repression that would prove to be detrimental for building a mass people's movement. According to Ahmad, RAM activists felt that the Oakland Panthers did not practise armed self-defence but rather 'armed agitpropoganda'. In turn, Ahmad challenges the masculinist narrative often associated with Panther rhetoric through his examination of the activities and ideas of Black women in the Panthers. Taking Assata Shakur as his lead, Ahmad interrogates how the organisation's failure to develop a better approach to political education and its heavy dependence on Newton's political strategy led to tactical errors like attacking potential Black middle-class allies with a positive political record. This, Ahmad argues, led to a failure to articulate the importance of building unlikely alliances within the Black community.

Despite these problems, Ahmad notes that Newton was perhaps the first person since Robert Williams to organise what many militants referred to as the lumpenproletariat into a 'public paramilitary organization'. The Panthers grew tremendously in 1968 and began to develop substantive political programmes including their celebrated breakfast programmes and liberation schools. Yet, as their day-to-day organising came together with the everyday anti-racism and socialist politics practised in the Panther survival programs, Ahmad suggests that we can note the intensification of an ideological and political counter-insurgency war directed at them by the state. Not only does Ahmad reflect on the politics of Panther interventions in cities, in the context of an emergent counterinsurgent domestic warfare, he also examines the key role that George Jackson played as revolutionary theoretician of the anti-prison movement in California. Jackson's radical political thought had garnered him the status of a leading revolutionary theorist of the anti-prison movement in the wake of the publication of *Soledad Brother* in 1970*.* Jackson's 'political assassination' in August 1971 was no less than a manifestation of domestic counter-revolutionary war. It is within this material context, Ahmad argues, that Newton's political strategies expressed a 'central weakness of the organization': centralised power and paranoia, which in turn produced political missteps. In a conclusion that can be read as tragedy, Ahmad suggests such problems marked the key internal contradictions of one of the most prominent Black radical organisations to form in the long 1960s in the context of external contradictions and state violence.

Ahmad's chapter on the LRBW, from their beginnings as the Dodge Revolutionary Union Movement (DRUM), is a classic account of the most important Black working-class socialist organisation of the generation. Here Ahmad poses the critical question of why the organisation emerged in Detroit. A key source of evidence for the chapter is an interview with General Gordon Baker, who, alongside Ken Cockrell and John Watson, formed the initial cadre for the organisation. Drawing on this evidence, Ahmad considers the factors that led to the emergence of DRUM, including the spatial concentration of the auto industry and the relationship between Wayne State University students and the postwar radical tradition in Detroit – including such figures as James and Grace Lee Boggs, C. L. R. James, Reverend Albert Cleage, as well as Baker's own roots in the Robert F. Williams Defense Committee. Ahmad shows DRUM's crucial connections with other Black radicals, as shown for example in the donation of cars to members of SNCC. He recounts the story of the emergence of the Detroit BPP in the wake of the dramatic Detroit rebellion of 1967, which also included members of the League. Some of these radicals had also joined RAM to build an African American-led student movement in urban areas.

Importantly, Ahmad reviews the League's critique of the BPP for 'engaging in self-defeating adventurist activities based on their romantic orientation toward the lumpen rather than a more realistic one towards workers'. The League's theoretical analysis was based on the premise that the best way to advance a revolutionary workers' struggle in the context of the Black liberation movement was to organise at the point of production. He convincingly argues that the League's political strategy was a direct response to African–American working– class self-activity and increased racial and national consciousness in the wake of the Detroit rebellion and the May 1968 wildcat strikes in the Chrysler Corporation's Hamtramck assembly plant. Ahmad situates the events in the context of a local Black radical tradition of workers' struggles in the auto factories. Ahmad concludes that the League 'was a logical outgrowth in the north of intensified efforts to desegregate the south led by the Student Non-Violent Coordinating Committee' and 'the most advanced African-American workers organization to emerge in the period of 1960–1975'. Thus, it is not coincidental that Ahmad's text was published by one of the oldest radical labour presses in the US, Charles H. Kerr Company.7

In his final chapter, 'Where do we go from here? The legacy of Black radical organizations, 1960–1975', Ahmad provides a historical meditation on the 1975– 2005 period of covert war directed against Black radicals. Given that state officials developed domestic militarised policing based on counter-insurgency warfare models developed in the Third World, they must have agreed on some level with the many radicals who saw themselves as part of a global resistance movement. During this period, J. Edgar Hoover identified the BPP as the most dangerous threat to national security in the United States. This declaration was followed by an FBI-led assault on political militants across the country. Ahmad interrogates COINTELPRO as a manifestation of counter-insurgency in US cities and prisons in response to Black struggles for autonomy and dignity.

Ahmad's critical commentary on the extra-legal and state-sanctioned violence directed against Blacks is evidence of the rather complicated critique of power from the perspective of Black radical activists themselves. Yet he also taps the other side of the dialectic to tell the story of claims on the social wage won by Black radical organisations, which, he argues, is crucial for regenerating socialist politics. In the appendix on research methodology, Ahmad provides some important notes on the study of Black radical struggles for 'socialism as a solution to the problem of racial, political, and economic and social inequality in the United States'. His argument that a dialectical and materialist approach is the most effective method to study Black social movements is, of course, suggestive. Yet Ahmad does not reduce his notes on research techniques to a crude economic determinism, given that he foregrounds the 'epistemology of Black radical organizations and participants' themselves in his important commentary about methodological precautions. Ahmad argues that to understand the interplay of race and class exploitation, or what he calls 'dual oppression', scholars should follow W. E. B. Du Bois, Eric Williams, C. L. R. James, Cedric Robinson and Rod Bush's lead in order to grasp the dialectic of racialised class oppression and Black and poor people's struggles for a new society.8 Students of the Black freedom struggle who overlook Ahmad's timely and important concluding observations on the crucial role of the self-activity of the masses in the struggle against racial capitalism do so to deleterious effect.

*Liberated Territory: untold perspectives on the Black Panther Party*, edited by Yohuru Williams and Jama Lazerow, historicises the emergence of the BPP in the context of the Black freedom struggle of the 1960s and 1970s. Williams and Lazerow have gathered essays which explore the ways in which the BPP's mode of political organising was able to capture the imagination of radical struggles on a national political stage. In the introduction, Williams develops this perspective by focusing on their ten-point platform and survivor programmes, as well as two dramatic events in particular: the armed march by Blacks on the California Legislature on 2 May 1967 to protest the policing of their neighbourhoods and the emergence of the 'Free Huey' campaign following his arrest and subsequent trial later the same year. While this story has been often told, what is less well understood, Williams argues, are the unique local histories of the BPP outside of the Bay Area. As a case in point, he recounts the history of the party in Omaha against the backdrop of a 'northern-style apartheid' which would culminate in rebellions sparked by police harassment in 1966 and produce a 'spawning ground for the Panthers'. Emphasising the importance of local memories, this collection of essays – consisting of an introduction, five chapters and an epilogue – presents local histories of the BPP in Boston, New Bedford, Birmingham, Detroit, Milwaukee, Omaha and Winston-Salem.

In the first chapter, Lazerow and Williams situate their study of the Panthers in the context of post-1960s tendencies in historical scholarship. They provide a helpful review of the secondary literature on the Party and commentary on sources of evidence ranging from college textbooks, journalistic accounts, memoirs, documentaries, Panther speeches and manifestos, autobiographies, documentary film and popular cultural sources. Based on an assessment of these primary sources, Lazerow and Williams claim that an historical analysis of the Party, particularly beyond Oakland, remains to be elaborated. Their goal is to complicate Oakland-centred narratives of the Panthers. Despite the important insights they find in new literature, Lazerow and Williams intervene with a methodological discussion in order to suggest the ways in which a more historical understanding of the role of the BPP in US radicalism and revolution might be achieved.

In the next chapter, Lazerow examines the emergence of the New Bedford BPP in the wake of the dramatic events in the city in July 1970, as a way to contribute a new understanding of the impact of 'small city riots' on radical politics in the region. These events were the political form of social protest against unemployment, educational inequality, inadequate housing, the enclosure of spaces and, of course, police brutality taken up by wageless Blacks during the period in question. Challenging dominant narratives on the upheavals, Lazerow situates this emblematic event within the longer history of dissent and struggle waged by Black, Puerto Rican and poor whites confronting centralised power, which is of course a welcome corrective to the depoliticised social science discourse usually deployed to describe the events. Lazerow is able to render a historicised understanding of the consciousness which the Panthers tapped, in order to develop their programme in the context of local political and economic circumstances as well as cultural traditions and social movements.

In the third chapter, Robert W. Widell, Jr explores the activities of the Alabama Black Liberation Front (ABLF) in Birmingham. While the organisation never achieved the recognition it sought as an official affiliate of the national BPP, Widell shows that there were a number of connections made with the Oakland headquarters. Interpreting the emergence of the ABLF in the wake of the dramatic Birmingham rebellion waged by the Black poor in 1963, Widell describes the ways in which ABLF members – many of whom were politicised through their experiences as soldiers in Vietnam – organised themselves as a people to oppose poverty, segregation, inadequate healthcare, unemployment and police violence. Much like other Panther formations across the country, by the early 1970s, state repression had crushed the political organisation represented by these Black struggles for dignity and self-determination.

Ahmad A. Rahman's brilliant investigation of the BPP in Detroit in the fourth chapter helps us to better understand the historic encounter between Black radicalism and Marxism. Rahman situates the emergence of the Detroit Panthers in the context of the Detroit Rebellion of 1967. By interrogating the class character of the racialised tropes used to describe the insurrection, he also provides a welcome counterpoint to the pejorative language often deployed by historians and social scientists. This historical analysis also has the benefit of Rahman's own participation in Detroit's Black radical politics through his activity as a Panther, as both a source of evidence and analytical tool. In this way, he provides his readers with a more nuanced and careful understanding of the relationships between the BPP, RAM, Uhuru (Freedom) and the LRBW in the city. For example, Rahman notes that the month of May 1968 not only produced the formation of the Panthers but was also 'the month that the RUM [Revolutionary Union Movement] at Dodge led a wildcat strike at the city's Chrysler plant', which was, of course, the impetus for the formation of the LRBW. Perhaps most importantly, his section on 'the seven canons of armed struggle' provides a critical commentary on theoretical sources for the underground movement in Detroit, including Malcolm X, Frantz Fanon, Robert Williams, Mikhail Bakunin, Ernesto 'Che' Guevara, Samuel Greenlee (*The Spook Who Sat by the Door*) and Carlos Marighella. In addition to Rahman's fine analysis of radical theory and political cultures of dissent, he also provides an analytic to understand the criminalisation of dissent and of the Black poor.

In the fifth chapter, Yohuru Williams examines the activities of the BPP in Milwaukee. He situates its emergence in the context of local conflicts with the Milwaukee police department especially in the wake of the rebellion in the city during the long hot summer of 1967. Emphasising the ways in which the events increased the radicalism of an already organised Black community, Williams interrogates how media representations perpetuated a racialised violent image of the Panthers, one that obscured the political content of their survival programmes to combat hunger, illiteracy, poverty and racism experienced by Black and poor people. Williams offers historical analysis to counter these misrepresentations by showing the actual political work they did to counter intensified race and class antagonisms and to articulate new democratic institutions. In the epilogue to *Liberated Territory*, Devin Fergus sets out to challenge the ways in which Black Power has been represented in mainstream historiography. He explores how the Panthers negotiated Black nationalist, communist and liberal tendencies. Fergus examines the case of a North Carolina Panther chapter in particular and argues that, by the mid-1970s, Panther ideology was less about conflict with the state and more a manifestation of 'civic nationalism'. Fergus writes that the Panthers moved pragmatically away from a Marxist dialectical materialist analysis and towards a Weberian impulse towards capitalism. This he unfortunately renders as a 'strategic shift'. Fergus writes that the Panthers' focus in the mid-1970s on winning the sympathies of liberals, the media and the elite, through electoral politics and pragmatic alliances with the Democratic Party, was a shift away from 'Marxist doctrine itself' and towards civic nationalism. The author discusses the Panther's severed relationship with Angela Davis who had maintained her commitments to communism. But in the next paragraph he suggests the Panthers had a political quality of 'openness … [that] marked the party in the period'. In Fergus' view, the turn toward electoral politics under the direction of Elaine Brown was important to the Party's transition to civic nationalism in BPP chapters across the country. He concludes that the local case of Winston-Salem was an exemplar of this shift. The fact that this occurred during a massive questioning of state legitimacy and constitutional crisis, Fergus argues, was important for a reinvigorated civic nationalism. While there are clearly merits in a better understanding of the legal and political challenges to racism and anti-democracy, scholars are well served to consider such evidence in the context of the changing form of the state during a period of domestic counter-insurgency in cities and prisons, in response to radical social movements.

Taken together, Ahmad's original study of Black radical organisations and Williams and Lazerow's edited collection on local histories of the Black Panthers compel us to consider the lessons for contemporary freedom struggles. Despite the strengths of each of the books, there are some weaknesses. *We Will Return in the Whirlwind* without doubt brings us closer to an understanding of the revolutionary consciousness of the period but some editorial problems can be jarring for readers. The publishers Charles H. Kerr could perhaps address these limitations if and when they print a new version of the text. *Liberated Territory* is a welcome contribution to local histories of the BPP beyond Oakland. Yet the editors' efforts to move the scholarly conversation in that direction ironically leave one wondering why Oakland was so central to the Panther political and spatial imaginary. What was it about the forces coalescing in California in the late 1960s that produced the Panther political formation? And how do we explain the implications of the counter-revolution directed at Black and Third World struggles? As Ruth Wilson Gilmore has demonstrated, the state's response to the Black, Third World and proletarian challenges to racial capitalism in the long 1960s was organised abandonment.9Under the guise of a 'war on crime' and 'drugs', 'law and order' and 'national security', state discourse in the post-1970s period was articulated to legitimise mass imprisonment and militarised policing. With these two historical contributions on the Black freedom struggle we can better comprehend how the US began 'policing the crisis' through a reconsideration of dramatic events, crucial organisations and important turning points in the struggle.

The Black revolution of the long 1960s was a leap forward in freedom struggles yet, of course, was one side of a dialectical process. The withdrawal of the social wage and imposition of privatisation and militarism under neoliberalism can thus be most effectively read as reactionary responses to the Black freedom struggle for the redistribution of social wealth, autonomy and dignity. The historical moment also marked the contradictory emergence of 'neoliberal racial regimes of security'.10 The stakes in the excavation of this subjugated history are perhaps nowhere more clearly demonstrated than in C. L. R. James's conclusion to his 1967 London talk 'Black Power' that 'no clearer or stronger voice for socialism has ever been raised in the US … the socialist society is not a hope, not what we may hope, but a compelling necessity.'11
