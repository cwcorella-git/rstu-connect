## ABSTRACT

Zareian, Ash Conspicuous Asceticism: Collapsing Consumers' Hungry Ghosts. Doctor of Philosophy (PhD), August, 2022, 131 pp., 8 tables, 7 figures, references, 221 titles.

The author investigates an emerging construct called conspicuous asceticism positioned from a narcissism-centric view. The understanding of this construct is approached by drawing an idiomatic semantic parallel in opposition to conspicuous consumption and by utilizing its current theoretical framework of narcissism. Moreover, this paper seeks to extend conspicuous consumption's theoretical framework as synonymous with the construct of compulsive consumption by elucidating adverse childhood experiences. Furthermore, I illustrate that consumers must transcend narcissism through engaging in treatment modalities requisite to operationalize a pioneering effort in which consumers can engage in marketing campaign efforts to advertise conspicuous ascetic contexts. This dissertation gleans from extant literature in sociology, anthropology, psychiatry, nutrition, and consumer research to elucidate the history, implications, and difficulties of treating narcissism that are linked to narcissistic consumption, and to additionally explore a number of relief-providing treatment modalities such as entheogenic medicines, gratitude, twelve step efforts, meditation and how each program can minimize the expression of narcissism in consumers. My dissertation is divided into three studies: (1) Study 1 provides a comprehensive overview of how narcissism develops in consumers and maps the unsustainability of narcissistic states in which they then become open to treatment and the different treatment plans available to them, (2) Study 2 details the historical responses of how Native American cultures used nutrition to treat narcissism in its community members, (3) Study 3 deploys an experiment of how keeping a gratitude journal minimizes narcissism in consumers. The implications of this study provide key insights for the characteristics of a post-narcissism segment in response to growing and global trends of narcissism. Findings reveal effective treatments for narcissistic consumption as well as discussing theories newly introduced to the marketing literature such as dislocation theory, and the emerging concept of brand splitting that explain the emergence of conspicuous asceticism, that has other implications for other research streams in consumer research.

## CHAPTER I

## INTRODUCTION

The mandala, the Buddhist wheel of life, revolves through six realms. Each realm is populated by characters representing aspects of human existence—our various ways of being. In the beast realm we are driven by basic survival instincts and appetites such as physical hunger and sexuality, what Freud called the id. The denizens of the hell realm are trapped in states of unbearable rage and anxiety. In the god realm we transcend our troubles and our egos through sensual, aesthetic, or religious experience, but only temporarily and in ignorance of spiritual truth. Even this enviable state is tinged with loss and suffering. The inhabitants of the hungry ghost realm are depicted as creatures with scrawny necks, small mouths, emaciated limbs, and large, bloated empty bellies. This is the domain of addiction where we constantly seek something outside ourselves to curb an insatiable yearning for relief or fulfillment. The aching emptiness is perpetual because the substances, objects, or pursuits we hope will soothe it are not what we really need. We don't know what we need, and so long as we stay in the hungry ghost mode, we'll never know [. . .] Some people dwell much of their lives in one realm or another. Many of us move back and forth between them, perhaps through all of them in the course of a single day [. . .] No society can understand itself without looking at its shadow side. (Gabor Matè In the Realm of Hungry Ghosts Close Counters with Addiction)

In the last three decades, research streams such as conspicuous (Kang and Park 2016; Fastoso, Wang, Bartikowski 2018; Bang, Choi, Wojdynski, and Lee 2020) and compulsive consumption (Hirschmann 1992; Rindfleisch 1997) have made headway in top level journals that implicitly describe "the aching emptiness . . ." and "substances, objects, or pursuits we hope will soothe" (Matè 2009) consumers afflicted by these consumer pathologies. While the objects (consumer goods) or pursuits (services) that can be manifested as conspicuous consumption has not been typically associated as destructive, remiss research has influenced scholars to reframe this type of consumption as an "addiction to esteem" (Baumeister and Vohs 2001). This reframing allows us to pigeonhole compulsive and conspicuous consumption as both addictive consumer behaviors, and moreover as symbolic forms of self-regulation that allow the consumer to experience love, connection, relief, and pleasure as a substitute for human relationships (Matè 2009).

Historically, in Buddhist texts, these descriptions of compulsive and conspicuous consumption are rife and metaphorical (Gombrich 1971; Matè 2009). Most notably, the hungry ghost depicted as having a small throat as thin as a needle, yet a big belly speaks to the difficulty of these afflicted consumers relative to processing and absorbing knowledge and nutrition imperative for well-being, while simultaneously feeling insatiable. Thus, these images speak to the difficultly for consumer alleviation. In pursuit of consumer related activities, consumers possibly wish to escape from the hell realm, as characterized by an array of moments tinged with anxiety and rage, and once they experience temporary relief through profane acts of consumption, are left feeling empty and confused. This dissertation seeks to address how consumers are rejecting dominant institutions, the hegemonic pursuit of blind materialism, in pursuit of alternative ways of being as posited by Armstrong et. al in (2016).

## **The Hungry Ghost throughout History**

Various cultures depict their version of the hungry ghost. For example, Native American cultures depict the hungry ghost as a virus of the mind that is nonlocal and invasive called wetiko/wendigo, which is characterized by greed, selfishness, and indulgence (Rohrl 1970; Levy 2013). In Scandinavian cultures, the archetype of the berserker is portrayed as an enraged warrior with a loss of sense of self (Levy 2013). In historical texts and imagery, the hungry ghost has been highly linked to miserliness, a facet of narcissism (Rotman 2021). From greed, selfishness, indulgence, rage, a loss of identity, and miserliness—all these characteristics can be said to describe symptomatic or pathological narcissism in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual (DSM-V) (American Psychological Association 2013). The hungry ghost's physical description infers just how difficult it is to treat these consumers, and given the portentous rise in narcissism (Twenge and Campbell 2016; Lasch 1979), we find that the hungry ghost metaphor is apt to describe this growing narcissistic consumer culture concomitant with its pathological forms of consumption.

According to Christopher Lasch (1979), a growing narcissistic culture is not one that is associated with clinical symptoms, but rather pre-occupation with celebrity, oral cravings, an irrational fear of death, repressing insurmountable rage; it is a cult of consumption that is fixated on grandiose products that attempts to reconcile a sense of gnawing sense of anxiety, guilt, emptiness, and depression, along with the deterioration of relationships, namely between men and women. Within these accounts, are clear brief indications of narcissism describing the propensities of compulsive consumption and conspicuous consumption.

## **Narcissism in marketing**

Narcissism is still a relatively fresh topic in marketing studies. Narcissism was first mentioned in 1995 as a facet of consumer segmentation (Netemeyer, Burton, and Lichtenstein 1995), but very briefly, and it was not until near the middle of the first decade in the 21st century, that narcissism made another slow but gradual appearance in a conceptual paper discussing narcissism as segmentation (Sedikides, Cisek, and Hart 2007). For example, narcissism's thesis is agentic (i.e. obsessed with power, beauty, enhancement, intelligence, and the self) (Sedikides, Cisek, and Hart 2007), where as the antithesis to narcissism is communal (i.e. community, kindness, and resourcefulness). Theoretically, consumers scoring low in narcissism should portray communal values, whereas consumers scoring high in narcissism would portray agentic values.

Narcissism has had both an implicit and explicit role in explaining relationships between adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), compulsive consumption, and conspicuous consumption. Not very many articles nor books have documented the relationship with narcissism and ACEs (Vaknin 2001; Vaknin 2018; Kernberg 1975; Kohut 2009; Kohut 2013). Moreso, the links between compulsive consumption and narcissism have been seldom researched, although they both share their roots in ACEs (Rose 2007). An increase in ACEs positively correlates with an increase in compulsive consumption and conspicuous consumption. Under the label addiction in the psychology literature, examining compulsive consumption outside of the marketing literature can open possible avenues of research regarding narcissism's relationship with compulsive consumption (Baumeister and Vohs 2001).

Extant marketing literature on narcissism has implicitly adopted a modern discourse. Critiquing this discourse with a postmodern lens in mind might help us understand some of the limitations, issues, and biases replete through these discussions (Firat and Venkatesh 1995). Consumer segmentations that describe consumers' narcissistic orientations are confounded by these modern demarcations. Articles published in the last decade allude to a discourse on segmentation, and how these segments, grandiose/vulnerable narcissism, differ in their consumer behaviors (Fastoso et. al 2018; Desmond and Lambert 2013). Implicit in these discussions is how dominance is assigned to these distinctions, such as grandiose narcissistic consumers, associated with the masculine, are more likely to adopt responsible consumption patterns, while vulnerable, associated with the feminine, are more likely to behave irresponsibly in their consumer habits, such as compulsive, conspicuous, and counterfeit consumption (Kang and Park 2016). To reiterate this modern conception, the masculine is assigned as auspicious over the feminine. Moreover, alternative dichotomies have been proposed such as somatic/cerebral narcissism, further confounding our understanding of the different modes of dichotomies which manifest in these consumer behaviors.

Because narcissism is a continuum subject to vicissitude, rather than assuming consumers are permanently relegated to either end of the spectrum, it might be helpful to view narcissism as contingent and erratic. What is also taken for granted by this modernist interpretation of narcissism, and emphasized by postmodernists, is the separation of mind and body. Narcissistic traits or values, described as agentic values, focus on the mind, but not at all the body. There is scant, but fortunately established research discussing the psychological pain, but nothing of the physical bodies of narcissistic consumers. While the psychological suffering of narcissistic orientations is intermittently described in a growing body of consumer research, there is no position that asserts narcissists may use self-regulatory forms of consumption as compulsive or conspicuous consumption to alleviate or distract from physical pain. Narcissism shows a significant correlation between these traits and physical illness (Blazer 1981; Krystal 1988; Fanos, Gelinas, Foster, Postone, and Miller 2008).

Finally, in this dissertation I wish to entertain the idea of post-narcissistic phenomena, and speculate, in a scholarly fashion, what it means to transcend narcissism, and what this transcendence looks like and means for marketing. Having briefly identified some of the issues such as the pathological forms of consumption that accommodate narcissism, what does it mean for consumers to also transcend the types of consumption that are indicators of narcissism by nomological necessity? What emerging constructs will marketing scholars be able to ascertain when solutions are found to these problems in consumer research?

## **Statement of the Problem**

Although narcissism has been linked to conspicuous consumption, it has not been linked to compulsive consumption in the marketing literature. Another theoretical issue is that consumers exhibiting highly narcissistic traits rarely seek treatment, as they are unable to recognize their own maladaptive behaviors (Levy 2013). However, consumers' narcissism may eventually render itself as unsustainable, and thus colors their social milieu with alienation and throws them into a collapsed state. A collapsed state can be described as a collaboration of unhinged rage and clinical depression, where they can no longer maintain relatively functional relationships (Diamond, Leomans, and Levy 2011; Back 2018). It is in this state consumers have the opportunity to transform their narcissism.

More often than not, narcissistic consumers refuse to acknowledge their own psychic structures that they reject or split off from through external adaptations, which makes the collapsed state particularly difficult to transcend. Carl Jung calls this conception the shadow (Jung 1939), that is, these consumer behaviors are driven by their unconscious mechanisms, and for consumers to emancipate themselves from these consumer pathologies, they must expose themselves to certain processes, and willingly so, to make "the unconscious conscious," (Mabit 2014), or metaphorically, "to open their throats" (Rotman 2021), that in turn deflates their bellies and adds flesh to their emaciated bones.

Typically, narcissistic consumers' psychic structures are seared with a traumatic past (Irwin 1995). Because of the refusal or difficulty to integrate the subconscious into the conscious, narcissistic consumption is notoriously difficult to treat (Kernberg 1975; Vaknin 2001; Mosquera and Knipe 2015), and thus, their stalwart consumer behavior external reflects their psychic scars. Most methods proposed by psychologists specializing in psychoanalysis have been quite disastrous in developing consumer emancipation programs for these afflicted consumers (Ogrodniczuk 2013).

Consider the following excerpt from Millon (2011), "personality disorders are among the most difficult of disorders to treat because they involve well-established behaviors that can be integral to a client's self-image." Consumers' self-image become defined by their traumatic pasts, and to address the traumatic pasts is to release their gripping identities, whose transcendent self is unknown, and it is this unknowingness coupled with unwillingness that frightens these consumers. Moreover, their traumatic past is an internal reflection of their external forms of behavior, namely ritualistic consumption, such that, their consumer identities are constructed by the organizing principle of hedonic consumption, which is also symbolic of an unresolved inner conflict. Consumers' grandiose fantasies underpin their self-image, and these fantasies are laden with hedonism. To disidentify with these fantasies of consumption means to strip the consumer of their identity, address their traumatic pasts, thus highlighting the difficulty of reforming rituals of consumption.

Some marketing and psychology scholars have constructed theory that is myopic or lacking a comprehensive framework of narcissism's chronology, and have failed to provide a comprehensive overview of its root cause (Vaknin 2001), especially in marketing. Without emphasizing narcissism's etiology, treatment modalities are unfounded and aimless. The etiology of narcissism will be further elaborated in the literature review. Furthermore, scholars have simplified and taken for granted notions of narcissism using modernist constructions, namely consumer segmentation, such as grandiose/vulnerable narcissism that can be reconceptualized using a postmodern critique as aforementioned.

Although discussions on narcissism have been an attractive discussion in consumer research (Cluley and Dunne 2012; Fastoso, Wang, and Bartikowski 2018; Lambert and Desmond 2013; Fastoso et. al 2020), there have been no clinical treatments proposed by marketing scholars to help attenuate narcissistic qualities in consumers scoring high on the NPI (Raskin and Hall 1979; Raskin and Hall 1988). Moreover, scholars have focused on the findings of narcissistic consumption in consumer contexts, rather than to focus on its retrospective origins, which is in ACEs (Baumeister and Vohs 2001; Cisek, Hart, and Sedikides 2008; Vaknin 2001; Nguyen and Shaw 2020). Possibilities of transforming narcissism are seldom scientifically documented (Van Mulukom, Patterson, and Van Elk 2020), and thus lie between remis and mythology.

Comprehensive theory has not necessarily been proposed by scholars that emphasize the etiological ingrained behaviors that underlie hungry ghost consumption (Horney 1939/2013; Levy 2013), let alone outline various treatment plans, which in turn, can then inform marketing scholars, practitioners, and consumers. Such information can be used to devise marketing programs and advertisements involving selfie-marketing campaigns used on social media (Fox, Bacile, Nakhata 2018; Taylor 2020). The context of these marketing campaigns will primarily be used to translate conspicuous asceticism in these transformed consumers that challenges the pervasive logic and expansion of the market (Firat 2013), signifying victory against harmful forms of consumption.

## **Statement of the Purpose**

In this dissertation, I propose new constructs to be introduced into the marketing literature that derive from the discipline of psychology (Vaknin 2018), along with scientifically cited treatment modalities that may present a potential interplay with extant marketing constructs of which sets this study apart from previous conceptual and empirical research. While consumer emancipation (Firat and Venkatesh 1995; Kozinets 2002) is not a new topic in consumer research, its applications to narcissistic consumption are unprecedented. This dissertation seeks to elucidate transformed narcissism concomitant with its consumer behavior, by proposing an emerging construct, conspicuous asceticism, a transformative practice of renouncing consumer culture in a public and explicit context. Conspicuous asceticism can be either intentional or unintentional, and can be performed from a place of admiration or humility, which may or may not convey narcissistic elements, but nevertheless, underpin sustainable practice. However, conspicuous asceticism may represent and constitute new ways of consumer (well) being.

Some of these unbroached constructs with respect to the marketing literature, such as collapsed narcissism and the moderating effect of consumer treatment programs might show promise in allowing scholars to better understand the narcissistic lifecycles, and when treatment can be deemed appropriate. New constructs and treatment modalities such as meditation, entheogenic medicines, gratitude, and dietary choices are introduced in a theoretical framework that illustrate the dynamic in (a) overcoming narcissistic consumption, and (b) when treatment is suggestible for consumers suffering from unsustainable consumption. What possibly entails these elaborate treatment plans are scattered and aimless throughout different scientific articles and require a unifying framework that organizes the effectiveness of consumer transformation. Furthermore, introducing these concepts may provide a comprehensive overview toward materializing consumer asceticism, namely, conspicuous asceticism.

As stated before, narcissism has been associated with agentic values (i.e. power, intelligence, enhancement, beauty, individualism) and can be traced back to modernity, specifically Descartes's disposition on the human subject that placed importance on shifting the construction of the individual from communal to agentic practices, such as prioritizing reason over tradition (Hoffman 1995). On the other hand, conspicuous asceticism is associated with communal values (i.e. kindness, community, honesty, relationships), and the aim of this theoretical narrative is to explore the possible avenues of healing for consumers to transcend agentic values to adopt communal ones.

Heinz Kohut in (1966) introduced the concept of transformed narcissism—where through transference, the consumer's (analysand) feelings and thoughts respective to their primary sources of narcissistic supply (i.e. parent or spouse) are redirected to the analyst (the catalyst), which could help them transform their narcissistic tendencies into creativity, empathy, and wisdom. The author would like to propose a fourth quality, asceticism—since one major component of narcissism is addiction (Baumeister and Vohs 2001; Vaknin 2001). Consumers must collectively transform their agentic values into reflective communal values of creativity, empathy, wisdom, asceticism, resourcefulness, community (Sedikides, Cisek, and Hart 2007). Thus, conspicuous asceticism transmutes qualities of narcissism (i.e. charm, and arrogance into confidence) and asceticism into a paradoxical state.

Transformed narcissism and conspicuous asceticism can be contagious and analogous to fashion, in how it trickles down from the rich to the poor (Atik and Firat 2013), suggesting that more sustainable forms of consumer culture are possible and viable. Consumers who transform their narcissism can set a conspicuous precedent of anti-consumption via social-media campaigns, such as selfie-marketing campaigns (Fox and Bacile 2018) and this imaginary can trickle down to other consumers in the context of social media posts for consumers in their battles with addiction. In this vein, transformed narcissism can be considered a new organizing principle that transcends the collective propensity of over/harmful consumption.

Overall, this dissertation seeks to (1) provide a comprehensive overview of narcissism, (2) challenge popular and trending modernist conceptualizations of narcissism using a postmodern critique, (3) reframe the theoretical framework of compulsive consumption through the lens of narcissism to demonstrate the following: (a) consumers elevate consumption over relationships, (b) document how narcissism and consumer emancipation from compulsive consumption are without complete recovery , (c) elucidate how narcissism and compulsive consumption and conspicuous consumption are rooted in ACEs, (d) demonstrate how both types of consumption are considered untreatable according to western models, (e) contend that harmful forms of consumption impair consumers' peers and families (f) compulsive consumption and conspicuous consumption constitute forms of narcissistic supply (4) compulsions and narcissism are both self-regulatory tools (5) unite conspicuous and compulsive consumption as selfregulatory consumption, (6) narcissism and its modes of consumption are unsustainable, and (7) list the emerging treatments that show promise in treating narcissistic consumption, where emancipatory and ascetic effects become possible for the consumer via a transformative process, and in which salient marketing implications can be utilized by marketing scholars and marketing managers alike.

## CHAPTER II

## LITERATURE REVIEW AND THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK

## **The main effects of Adverse Childhood Experience on Narcissism, Conspicuous Consumption, and Compulsive Consumption**

The relationship between ACEs and narcissism has not been discussed in consumer research. ACEs have been indirectly mentioned in the 1980s and 1990s under the phrasing of "dysfunctional" or "negligient" families/parents (Belk 1988; Rindfleisch, Burroughs, and Denton 1997). Consumer research's discourse on narcissism is limited, and therefore lacks a comprehensive framework. Understanding narcissism's etiology requires placing it in its context of origins with respect to the consumer.

Prior research on children being reared by dysfunctional caregivers dates back to the mid 1970's, as written by leding scholars of narcissism at the time (Kernberg 1975; Kohut 1977). This theoretical link grew throughout the decades, and its viewpoint is becoming mainstream. Consider the following excerpt:

"Psychodynamic speculation pinpointed the origin of narcissism in early experiences with caregivers. Cold, inconsistent, and inconsiderate parental practices, as well as excessively positive, admiring, and idealizing parental practices, have been postulated as the source of narcissistic hostility and relational distrust," (Cisek, Hart, Sedikides, 2008, p. 205).

Others have linked physical and sexual forms of abuse to narcissistic aggression and shame (Keene 2016). These phenomena have been typically associated with ACEs (Vaknin 2001). Moreover, the authors argue that narcissism, can range from normal narcissism (i.e. subclinical) to pathological (i.e. clinical) (Cisek, Hart, Sedikides 2008). This spectrum is a reliable indication of the quality of consumers' interpersonal relationships as described by the friendship-divergence model (Sauls and Ziegler-Hill 2020), and to the number of ACEs faced by consumers (Clemens, Fegert, and Allroggen 2022). The more criteria consumers meet on the narcissistic personality inventory (NPI) (Raskin and Hall 1979; Raskin and Hall 1988), the likelier to be ostracized or lack social support among their peers in relation to their traumatic past (Horton, Bleau, and Drwecki 2006; Otway and Vignoles 2006).

ACEs contribute to the development of vulnerable narcissism, which is characterized by emotional instability, while parental overvaluation contributes to grandiose narcissism, which is characterized by ego inflation (Nguyen and Shaw 2020). However, parental overvaluation can be considered a form of abuse as it distorts the boundaries of the parent-child dynamic (Van Schie, Jarman, Huxley, and Grenyer 2020), thus the child is not able to separate and individuate from the parents. It is important to note that these categories of narcissism cannot be measured in the same way (Rohmann, Neumann, Herner, and Bierhoff 2012). However, it is also important to keep in mind that the etiologies of these two types of narcissism must be measured in distinctive ways, though, regardless, may be reframed as abuse. Curiously, both parental overvaluation (Thomaes and Brummelman 2018) and ACEs are linked to lower empathy in consumers

(Narvey, Yang, Wolff, Baglivio, and Piquero 2022). Low empathy is consistently cited as a dimension of narcissism (Urbonaviciute and Hepper 2020).

## **Narcissism in Postmodernism**

Modernity has given leverage to the duality of narcissism, such that it leverages and values the grandiose over the vulnerable (i.e. grandiose/vulnerable) (Firat and Venkatesh 1995). It is quite possible that modern discourse on narcissism fails to capture the complexity of these two narcissistic orientations, thus giving leverage to a postmodern discourse on challenging theories of narcissistic consumption. In a postmodern scope, narcissism is neither black or white. It is a spectrum of which different states can alternate between varying elements of cerebral, somatic, grandiose, and vulnerable (Vaknin 2001). The continuum of narcissism has been theoretically assessed and has discussed how consumers fluctuate between two states of grandiose/vulnerable (Gore and Widiger 2016; Edershile and Wright 2022), which challenges the rigidity of consumers being relegated to one of the two states. Moreover, the literature on narcissism seldom documents the epochal and capricious styles of how narcissism expresses itself in consumer behavior (cf. Fastoso, Wang Bartikowski 2018; Lasch 1979).

Consumption may be an appropriate indicator to identify the state (i.e. vulnerable or grandiose) of narcissism consumers undergo, such as luxury brand consumption (Kang and Park 2016; Fastoso, Bartikowski, and Wang 2018; Neave, Tzemou, and Fastoso 2020), though marketing scholars argue that these states are exclusively hegemonic or conditional to consumers' narcissistic identifications (i.e. their segmentation). For example, past findings have revealed vulnerable narcissistic consumers purchase counterfeits, while grandiose purchase authentic goods (Fastoso, Bartikowski, and Wang 2018; Kang and Park 2016; Neave, Tzemou, and Fastoso 2020). Postmodernism may suggest because of the oscillation between the two states, consumers may purchase both counterfeit and authentic products.

Not much research has documented how compulsive consumption emerges and impacts the two different states of narcissism. One paper has demonstrated that substance use either reinforces current states of vulnerability, or intentionally facilitates a transition to the grandiose state through alcoholism which offsets negative emotions, such as shame (Bilevicius, Neufeld, Sing, Foot, Ellery, Keough, and Johnson 2019). Similar findings were expressed that highlight the addiction-like features that underlie narcissism when cocaine use is used to heighten grandiosity (Kastner-Bosnek, Dajic, Mikus, Weidenhauer, and Willeit 2021). Thus, vulnerable narcissism transforms into a temporary grandiose state. Furthermore, the authors cited that the impacts of drug neuropharmacology on narcissism is not exclusive to cocaine abuse as consistent with how alcoholism affects narcissism.

One key theoretical issue that responds to a potential research gap are drug abuse's side effects. For example, it was mentioned that cocaine affects vulnerable and grandiose narcissists differently. Vulnerable narcissists are more prone to developing anxiety as a side effect and are unable to regulate self-control, while grandiose narcissists are better able to manage their compulsive consumption. Lacking from the discourse, however, is the temporal element regarding how long grandiose narcissists are able to successfully manage their addictions. As it has been noted by neuropharmacologists that over time, addictions erode neural networks responsible for self-regulation (Volkow, Wise, and Baler 2017). This implies grandiose narcissists will eventually behave like their vulnerable counterparts, thus blurring the distinction between the two consumer segments.

Long-term negative emotions such as shame originate in childhood (Sedighimornani, Rimes, and Verplanken 2021), and consumption takes place to bypass negative emotions so consumers can elicit narcissistic supply from their external environment. Moreover, external factors, called external locus (i.e. the outside exclusively dictates the behavior of one's self) of control may shift grandiose narcissistic consumers into a vulnerable state characterized by depression, anxiety, or suicidality (Pincus, Cain, and Wright 2014; Papageorgiou, Denovan, Dagnall 2019).

Postmodernism has been critical of dualistic classifications and how one is more prized than the other (e.g. order/chaos, producer/consumer, masculine/feminine, etc.) (Firat and Venkatesh 1995), and the grandiose/vulnerable model is no different in its current and modern conceptualization in how the grandiose is more esteemed than its grandiose counterpart. Notwithstanding, both types of narcissism are impacted by ACEs (Clemens, Fegert, and Allroggen 2022), and accordingly, consumers asymmetrically fluctuate between the two states.

H1: ACEs increase narcissism in consumers.

## **The Main Effect of ACEs on Conspicuous Consumption and Compulsive Consumption**

One research stream of materialism is conspicuous consumption (Segal and Podoshen 2013), which has been associated with narcissism (Wang and Park 2016; Fastoso, Bartikowski, and Wang 2018; Neaves, Tzemou, Fastoso 2020). Though no alternative framework has extended consumer research's discussion on the emergence of conspicuous consumption. The link between adverse childhood experiences and conspicuous consumption is scant in the marketing literature.

What the literature has yet to bridge in regards to ACEs is that the conditions of ACEs can be created in the womb of the mother that can eventually lead to pathological forms of consumption, such as conspicuous and compulsive consumption (Mate 2009). For example, after the events of 9/11, pregnant women developed post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), which lead to increased cortisol levels, and at one year of age, children had abnormally elevated cortisol levels which suggests the conditions for compulsive and conspicuous consumption are created not just in childhood, but also in the womb (Mate 2009). While this study did not mention narcissism, one study has suggested that mechanisms of narcissism can also be ascertained in the womb (Kestenberg 1990). Thus, all three conditions can begin prenatally.

As previously stated, consumer research on ACEs has been discussed in marketing since the 1980s and 1990s (Belk 1988; Rindfleisch, Burroughs, and Denton 1997). Consumer upbringings and their pathological relations to possessions have been theorized in relation to ACEs. Most of the discussions in consumer research mentioning ACEs have focused on the institution of families and their dysfunctions and separations due to absences of parents that can lead to consumers scoring higher on materialism (with no emphasis on luxury good consumption) (Belk 1988), and compulsive consumption (Rindfleisch, Burroughs, and Denton 1997).

Podoshen, Andrzejewski, and Hunt (2014) noted that the African-American segment tend to score higher in conspicuous consumption than other ethnic groups. Hampton-Anderson et. al (2021) ascertained that African-Americans disproportionately suffer from ACEs using a biopsychosocial-cultural framework. This view provides an alternative framework toward the discourse on conspicuous consumption as one that can be tantamount to the traditional view of conspicuous consumption as a narcissistic driven phenomenon. Thus, ACEs bridge the gap of our understanding of the emergence of conspicuous consumption. Therefore, I posit the following hypothesis:

H2a. ACEs increase the likelihood consumers engage in conspicuous consumption.

The extant literature on conspicuous consumption as explained by consumers' narcissistic orientations lacks a comprehensive framework, in such, that it does not necessarily detail the retrospective upbringing of consumers' childhood development in relation to conspicuous consumption. A comprehensive theoretical framework reveals the development of how consumers experience early onset trauma, in which narcissistic defenses manifest, and thus, how these same defenses are reinforced by conspicuous consumption. Research does not necessarily explore the narcissism's mediation of ACEs on conspicuous consumption, but rather draws direct parallels between each construct in relation to conspicuous consumption, i.e., ACEs and narcissism have directly corresponded to conspicuous consumption, but not comprehensively, such that, ACEs foster narcissism, and accordingly, narcissism fosters conspicuous consumption.

H2b. Narcissism mediates the relationship between ACEs and conspicuous consumption.

## **ACEs effect on Compulsive Consumption**

Conspicuous consumption has been reframed as an addiction to esteem, although narcissism has been linked to an addiction to esteem, narcissism has not been linked to a more general compulsive consumption in the marketing literature. In 1992, Elizabeth Hirschman theorized two themes that were associated with a general theory toward compulsive consumption—namely distressed consumers and sociopathy (Hirschman 1992). Sociopathy, the colloquial term for anti-social personality disorder as according to the DSM-V (American Psychological Association 2013), has been seldom vetted in consumer research, along with its comorbidities. Anti-social personality disorder is significantly correlated with narcissism as a comorbid state (Gunderson and Ronningstam 2001; Sylvers, Brubaker, Alden, Brennan, and Lilienfeld 2008). Therefore, the link between narcissism and compulsive consumption has been implicit in the context of consumer research over the last three decades since Hirschman's (1992) salient publication.

Researchers of psychoanalysis attempted to reframe narcissism as a state of addiction to esteem (Baumeister and Vohs 2001), suggesting that compulsive consumption does not have to orient itself around tangible products, such as food or substances, but rather an addiction to esteem can manifest itself as an addiction to the symbolic, such as brands. Brand addiction as a relatively new construct has been introduced to the marketing literature (Cui, Mrad, and Hogg 2018; Mrad, Majdalani, Cui, and El Khansa 2020), and its dynamic exists at the level of the abstract as symbols of value. It can also constitute addiction to services, such as gambling and internet addiction (Mate 2009; American Psychological Association 2013).

Consumer ideology, to the extent that consumers overidentify with their minds, can appropriately be classified by Dostoevsky, "To think too much is a disease," which implies negative consequences as a result of addiction to thinking, and whose phenomenon has also been written about in journals of mindfulness (Dostoevsky [1864/2009]; Peltz and Black 2014). One modality of narcissism, fantasy, functions as a form of addictive introspection that functions as a defense mechanism against traumatic mental apparatus that occupy consumers' deepest parts of their psyches (Asper-Bruggisser 1987). Addictive introspection in the form of fantasy can thus constitute narcissistic supply (Fenichel 1938), i.e. a self-regulatory mechanism that further isolates and atomizes consumers from authentic human connection, at least in the context of addiction. This psychic baggage is often painful to cope with, and the hegemony of consumer culture further underpins this coping mechanism, as objects of consumption are constantly incorporated into translating imaginary vignettes (Raskin and Novacek 1991). No matter the behavior or consumer context, compulsive consumption manifests to elicit esteem which allows a reconceptualization of narcissism in the framework of addiction.

Furthermore, conspicuous consumption can be characterized as a distinct, discriminant branch of compulsive consumption, given luxury brand consumption acts as a vehicle to elicit admiration in the form of esteem from consumers' peers in an addictive manner (Baumeister and Vohs 2001; Fastoso, Wang, and Bartikowski 2018), this is to say, narcissism inspires an addictive nature in consumers. Likewise, brands, as vehicles of admiration, characterize an addiction to fantasy in which remove consumers from being present, and thus disconnecting from community. In other words, fantasies of power or admiration are characteristic of agentic values which blockade community involvement, acts of kindness, and understanding. Conspicuous consumption, as an addiction to esteem, represents a niche of addiction that may be translated in other addictive consumer contexts.

Often, consumers with weak social ties, particularly with the family unit turn toward compulsions to regulate their internal mechanisms (Vaknin 2001). This pattern eventually becomes deeply entrenched in consumers that they eventually elevate and prioritize the act of consumption over human relationships. In a combination of low empathy, exploitation, and viewing others as vehicles for manipulation and raising status, a recipe for this elevation can become consistently manifest over time (Watson, Grisham, Trotter, and Biderman 2010).

H3a: ACEs increase the likelihood that consumers will engage in compulsive consumption.

## **Temporary narcissism and pathological consumption**

According to Li et. al in (2016), consumers can enter temporary narcissistic states rather than be ongoingly and pervasively restricted to inflated self-concepts, relative lack of interest in warm interpersonal relationships, and reliance upon self-regulatory strategies (Cisek, Hart, and Sedikides 2008). Temporary inducements of narcissism, due to unforeseen overstimulation or turmoil, may even incite ephemeral states of pathological consumption such as conspicuous consumption or compulsive consumption, and can even be triggered by economic turmoil in adults (Bianchi 2014). These temporary inducements may trigger a lack of interest in relationships, in which narcissistic consumers substitute their relationships by addiction to regulating one's internal system.

While ACEs are the foundation of compulsive consumption, ACEs don't necessarily explicate how compulsive consumption manifests as a result of acquired trauma, and require a more elaborate mediation effect to bridge the gap between what is only described as a coping mechanism to deal with pain. One possible explanation according to Straker and Jacobson (1981) describes that children who acquire trauma tend to be less empathetic, a hallmark of narcissism. To the extent that empathy determines the quality of consumers' social relations, compulsive consumption may be a substitute to human relationships when empathy is low, and thus describes how narcissistic indicators elicit compulsive consumption in practice. Such theorization is consistent with Belk in (1988) how consumers' relationship to consumption functions as a surrogate to absent or negligent human relations.

The link between ACEs and compulsive consumption outside of the marketing literature is abundant and overwhelming (Mate 2009). An increase of ACEs drives up the risk of addiction exponentially (Perez et. al 2016) Historically, Lasch (1979) argued that consumers' narcissism was fastened through compulsory industry as a way of mastering the id. For example, the notion of compulsory industry was pushed onto the consumer as an apprehensive response to the organizing principles of nature, i.e., the modern consumer wished to avoid being mastered by nature, so in turn, developed the ego which exacerbated what was previously considered a healthy narcissism. In this vein, consumers were unconsciously giving rise to a narcissistic archetype that Lasch labels the American Adam. Moreover, Lasch speaks to the poor relationships of narcissists in which is substituted by consumption where he uses sex addiction as a prime example, "they experience intense feelings of emptiness and inauthenticity . . . his devaluation of others, together with his lack of curiosity about them, impoverishes his personal life and reinforces the 'subjective experience of emptiness' . . . Chronically bored, restlessly in search of instantaneous intimacy," (pp. 53).

One aspect of this paper seeks to explore consumers' disconnection to themselves they've initiated from the developmental stage to adulthood. For example, narcissistic wounds are acquired in early childhood development, and is just one of the defense mechanisms of the consumer in their arsenal. In tandem to narcissistic coping mechanisms, compulsive habits are used to regulate consumers' self-esteem, and can also be used to enhance narcissistic styles of coping with their unresolved and repressed or suppressed wounds (Sawrie, Watson, Sherbak, Greene, Arredondo 1997). Linking ACEs to narcissism, and thus compulsive consumption, corrects the myopia of how consumers arrive at compulsive consumption. Finally, narcissistic consumers, out of fear of dependence on other consumers, tend to avoid close relationships (Lasch 1979), and substitute human relationships with consumption. Thus, I posit the following: H3b: Narcissism mediates the relationship between ACEs and compulsive consumption.

## **The Main Effect of Conspicuous Consumption and Compulsive Consumption on Collapsed Narcissism**

Long-term narcissism is unsustainable in that it eventually alienates and corrupts the relationships of consumers. While conspicuous consumption and compulsive consumption are distinct constructs, they often fall under the guise of commodity narcissism, such that, consumers engage in consumption that separates and isolates themselves from communal values through this very alienation, and thus empowers agentic values (Cluley and Dunne 2013). This will to engage in commodity narcissism is pathological, and in a way, functions as an addiction to hurting others that results in unforeseen consequences its perpetuators. Unwittingly, they find themselves alone, in divorces, lost friendships, and unemployed, catalyzed by the symbolic form of destruction that is narcissism. Ultimately, their behaviors throw them into the wake of atomization, paradoxically, which is the foundation for salvation.

Collapsed narcissism is defined by an experiential state where consumers continuously fail to seek sources of admiration and objects of self-regulation (Vaknin 2001), otherwise known as narcissistic supply, a term coined by Otto Fennichel in 1938 (Vaknin 2001; Fennichel 1938). In the midst of consumers' relationships becoming unstable, what may drive instability is their failure to maintain relationships due to having no interest in upholding, let alone adopting communal values, such that their relationships are based upon constructing and upholding their grandiose selves created in the vignettes of the idealized self (Cisek, Hart, and Sedikides 2008). According to Lasch (pp. 53, 1979), "depression in narcissistic patients takes the form not of mourning with its admixture of guilt, described by Freud in 'Mourning and Melancholia,' but of impotent rage and 'feelings of defeat by external forces.'"

Given that the consumption of brands and goods that portray symbols of status have failed narcissistic consumers, a phase of collapsed narcissism becomes imminent and thus they seek alternative methods to regain lost admiration (Vaknin 2001). One alternative mode of regaining consumer power is for them to seek treatment by being exposed to one or more various treatment modalities. Within treatment, the action of self-reflection via active imagination is cardinal to reassemble a sense of self-coherence or identity (Levy 2013; Chodorow 1997), that conspicuously reveals consumers' suppressed or repressed memories that put them through arrested development in their developmental stage.

Having burned bridges, narcissism continues to perpetuate its autonomous egoic mechanism that continuously bends the will of the consumer to its own modus operandi, of which Carl Jung in (1966) called the autonomous complex. It is only when a consumers' autonomous complex implodes that consumers then become open to treatment modalities. This openness to receive help or change these behaviors in the midst of emotional turmoil is pivotal in transcending collapsed narcissism. Consumers can indefinitely be mired in a state of collapse until it becomes too unbearable that they seek help, and despite this, they still rarely seek restitution (Macdonald 2014; King et. al 2020). Blindly, however, in the reassembling of their fragmented identities, little do they know that they are responsible for the alienation they've brought upon others, as narcissism is a kind of psychic blindness, in which they cannot distinguish the agentic from the communal (Levy 2013). Treatment then becomes a wakeup call with hit or miss outcomes to emerge from the collapsed state.

## **Conspicuous Consumption's Effect on Collapsed Narcissism**

Conspicuous consumption can often lead to massive consumer debt (Dutt 2019), such that they see their shopping addiction or addiction to impress, elicit esteem, is curbed by the limitation of debt, thus resulting in a loss of narcissistic supply. Consumers' grandiose selfconcepts can no longer be sustained due to financial constraints. Moreover, if consumers are no longer able to use the vehicle of conspicuous consumption as a means of eliciting narcissistic supply from their peers, i.e., peers are not able to imbue narcissistic consumers with admiration. This can influence the state of collapse and challenge them to rethink their consumption patterns as a way to achieve self-regulation.

The cycle of impressing consumers' peers that underlies addiction to esteem requires that the symbolic and intangible cycle to thrill others is nevertheless still an addiction that has analogous effects on the brain, such as affecting dopamine receptors, just like any other form of addiction, including, and especially substances (Mate 2009). Notwithstanding, be it tangible or intangible, mature stages of such behaviors increases susceptibility toward tolerance and anhedonia, i.e., consumers are no longer able to derive self-regulatory feelings to conspicuously consume at a consistent level, and must escalate their conspicuous consumption to derive sufficient self-regulation. This increasing tolerance and anhedonia can instigate a state of collapse.

H4a. Conspicuous consumption positively mediates the relationship between narcissism and collapsed narcissism.

## **Compulsive Consumption's effect on Collapsed Narcissism**

Often, compulsive consumption exacerbates its effects on the consumer over time, such that this leads to a loss of narcissistic supply, and enter in a collapsed state, a state of emotional immobilization that is quite searing, and thus enables opportunities for new transcendental experiences. The loss of narcissistic supply can emanate from the consequence of lawsuits, incarceration, divorce, loss of friendships, a health diagnosis, death, and financial distress (Counts and Sacks 2018; Pechorro, Gentile, Ray, Nunes, and Gonçalves 2016; Harnish and Bridges 2015; Horowitz 2009; O'Reilly, Doerr, and Chapman 2018; Coleman, Pincus, and Smyth 2019), whose outcomes can be a direct consequence of compulsive consumption (Mate 2009).

An increasing tolerance to compulsive consumption can often lead to anhedonia, a characteristic of the collapsed state, that can inspire some consumers to seek transcendental and spiritual experiences to emancipate from this positive feedback loop (Lasch 1979; Cinque et. al 2018). Additionally, anhedonia is typically tied to depression, and for Freud, both depression and narcissism could be tied to object loss for the ego (Lewin 1954). Another dimension of collapsed narcissism can be characterized by rage, such that object loss can render consumers' disposition as one with nothing to lose leaving them free to act out (Blum 2003). For Jung, a narcissistic state and a collapsed state are when psychic contents become perpetually constellated and activated, i.e., consumers overidentify with archetypes, at both the personal and collective unconscious. Thus, collapsed narcissism is a multi-dimensional construct characterized by rage and depression in relation to object loss.

It has been thoroughly researched that substance or behavioral withdrawal can subsequently be tied to rage (Boles and Miotto 2003; Kaptsis et. al 2016), which is made more susceptible by narcissism (Kohut 1966). In a state of withdrawal, consumers can elicit selfatomization via transgression that alienates their social circles, thus making them more dependent on pathological consumption in relation to social loss be it materialism (Belk 1988), gambling (Cunningham-Williams et. al 2009; Korman et. al 2008), or drug use. According to Levy (2013), consumers will continue to bury themselves in the collapsed state by acting out the unconscious until they integrate their suppressed or repressed trauma into the conscious mind. As consumers are inherent social creatures despite one's narcissism (Mc Leod 2007), relationship loss evokes the potentiality for a collapsed state (Somary and Emery 1990), and thus, I posit the following hypothesis:

H4b. Compulsive consumption positively mediates the relationship between narcissism and collapsed narcissism.

## **The Moderating Effect of Treatment Modalities on Collapsed Narcissism and Conspicuous Asceticism**

**"The surest way to get to hell is to resist going to hell." - Chögyam Trungpa**

Contextually, the above quote signifies the inevitability of consumer suffering. By engaging in conspicuous consumption and compulsive consumption, these coping mechanisms are modes of avoiding suffering that is concomitant with the existential. To quote the Buddha's First Noble Truth in The Four Noble Truths, he says "all of life is suffering" (Anderson 2013), but suffering in the language of Pali/Sanskrit translates to dukkha meaning stress, which is common across all consumers (Shah et. al 2009). Consumer behavior, as a self-regulating mechanism is capricious, and because of increasing tolerance and neurobehavioral changes, this invites more suffering to be experienced by the consumer. Conversely, if consumers are to emancipate from their overidentifying with hedonic consumption, being drawn in by the pain that attracted their hedonic behavior in the first place is also inevitable. This section introduces treatment modalities that are quite jarring for the consumer, and invite the experience of "hell" that is symbolically transformative in its going beyond the entrenchment of pathological consumption. In the words of Carl Jung, "no tree, it is said, can grow to heaven unless its roots reach down to hell." In eastern philosophy, hell is a symbol of narcissism as seen in the stories of the experience of hungry ghosts (Rotman 2021), and heaven, a symbol of transformed narcissism.

Consumers experiencing momentary pessimism emanates from narcissism according to Levy (2013). Pessimism facilitates the passing through the hungry ghost realm from time to time, as some stay longer in this phase than others in relation to their unconscious behaviors, and hence perpetuates the collapsed state. An overidentification with pessimism, as fueled by depression and rage, is a hallmark of the collapsed state (Kohut 1966; White 2022). An untethered narcissistic consumer, depending on the episode of rage or grief, determines their willingness to receive assistance in overcoming this unhinged state. Rage may signify vindictiveness, and thus unwillingness and hopelessness, while a state of grief may be more inclined toward support. Overcoming the pessimistic dimension of narcissism through active imagination yields optimism, hope, and well-being—cornerstone elements of conspicuous asceticism.

During the treatment phase, acknowledging's one past in hindsight is imperative to reconciling the fragile self, and making one's self whole again, which is the basis of Kohut's self-psychology mode of treatment, where empathy and letting go of trauma is emphasized to the patient (Kohut 2013; Kohut 2009). The goal of these treatment modalities is to help consumers heal narcissistic structures by having them engage in treatment modalities that allow the recipient to process their past traumatic experiences (Vaknin 2018). At first, they might blame others for collapsing, or even realize anything is wrong with them (Kohut 2013; Kohut 2009; Kernberg 1975; Vaknin 2001), but if they are willing to accept and implement therapeutic procedures from the perspective of their own wrongdoings these healing processes can lead to a transformed narcissistic state—of empathy, humor, and wisdom (Kohut 1966; Kohut 2013; Kohut 2009; Gleig 2010).

Jung's concept of prima materia is what consists of the shadow, the unconscious, trauma, and harmful belief systems linked to consumption. Hippocrates once said, "Before you heal someone, ask them if they are willing to let go of what makes them sick." What is it exactly that makes consumers sick in relation to profane acts of consumption? Is it holding on to their past memories and emotions, or is it the act of consumption that is merely a symbolic externalization of consumers' inner landscapes? To the latter question, it is the prima materia that each treatment modality mentioned that attempts to bridge the subconscious with the conscious, which is to be interacted through self-dialog and delineation of which Jung calls active imagination (Levy 2013; Chodorow 1997). The integration of the shadow is often described as uncomfortable (Masters 2010). For example, active imagination can be described as hellish, of which Jung called the most effective and powerful tool for therapeutic transcendence (Levy 2013; Chodorow 1997).

"Curiously, people resist the noble aspects of their shadow more strenuously than they hide the dark sides. To draw the skeleton out of the closet is relatively easy, but to own the gold in the shadow is terrifying. It is more disrupting to find that you have a profound nobility of character than to find out you are a bum," says Johnson in (1993). In expounding the duality of the shadow is to say consumers with narcissistic qualities suppress their non-narcissistic or communal qualities of which Kohut in (1966) suggested constitutes wisdom, empathy, and humor. These characteristics are the symbolic gold Johnson is alluding to under the phrasing of "profound nobility." However, it is the communal aspects of the self that are more difficult to integrate than the agentic. Theoretically, treatment modalities integrate and reveal the communal aspects of consumers that enables their asceticism. The road to conspicuous asceticism is a crucible for consumers, in that, transforming narcissism by integrating communal values is enduring. As all these treatment modalities are centered upon shadow work, they share similar dynamics in their ability to transform (Maia et. al 2021; Mate 2021; Frizzell and Banner 2018; Gray 2020).

The collapsed consumer state is an invitation for transformation. Perhaps narcissism in its different stages present evolutionary challenges for consumers to experience greater forms of consciousness, in that narcissism is a prima materia to be transmuted whose means to an end is to sustain a vessel of enlightened states of emancipation and well-being (Levy 2013; Jung 1944/2014). There are various treatment modalities yet to be reviewed by marketing scholars in regards to pathological consumption. In this section I will be discussing are four-fold, three esoteric treatment modalities, along with one normative practice that could possibly benefit the consumer in transformation that lay the foundation of emancipatory asceticism. The three esoteric treatment modalities include meditation, ayahuasca, and iboga/ibogaine, along with the application of forgiveness.

## **Classical Serotonergic Psychedelic Medicines: Ayahuasca**

There is little empirical research conducted on classical serotenergic psychedelic medicines (CSPs). This paper will mostly focus on two types of entheogenic plant medicines, ayahuasca and iboga. Ayahuasca and iboga are traditional plant medicines used by indigenous tribes in both South America and West Africa respectively (Riba et. al 2001; Pope 1969). Both these medicines are known for their abilities to heal disease of the mind and body, including pathologies of addiction to autoimmune issues (Kargbo 2022; Inserra 2018; Hamill et. al 2019; Fabregas et. al 2010; Belgers et. al 2016; Mate 2021). These medicines are becoming increasingly popular, and its research is of particular interest to both researchers and the public (Estrella-Parella et. al 2019).

There has been shown great promise between compulsive consumer behaviors, such as shopping or illicit drug consumption and CSP experiences. Only few studies to date have documented overcoming shopping addictions by using ayahuasca (Mabit 2007; Fabregas et. al 2010). In one longitudinal study (Fabregas et. al 2010), two control groups were examined pre-CSP and post-CSP experiences for one year, and their scores were significantly lower on the Addiction Severity Index (ASI) regarding alcohol use, in addition to other illicit substances, with the exception of cannabis. Unfortunately, the criterion of the moderating effect of treatment modalities are extensive, as the participants in Fabregas's et. al study in (2010) required two doses over the course of fifteen years. Ayahuasca, as a treatment modality, may not be effective in aiding consumers to overcome their compulsive consumption in relation to the substance, which may require different styles of treatment modalities in triumphing over the dependency.

Reliving ACEs or any trauma may be a reluctant task for the consumer. Integrating repressed memories and qualities of the self can be daunting (Masters 2010). Depending on the number of healing ceremonial and clinical experiences consumers engage, even consumers experiencing narcissism at the pathological level can experience stronger feelings of connectedness and empathy, followed by a decrease in maladaptive narcissistic traits (Van Mulukom, Patterson, Van Elk 2020). According to Healy et. al in (2021), CSPs have been used to treat shame, a difficult emotional archetype to heal, and could possibly show promise to ameliorate other deleterious emotions that constitute collapsed narcissism, e.g., grief and rage.

Some issues concerning entheogenic medicines are that they are illicit substances (Tupper 2008), where consumers inhabiting outside of Central and South America must travel great distances, and to consume entheogens must do so under the supervision of ayahuasqueros/shamans. Even just one ayahuasca ceremony has consumers reported feeling significant improvements in anxiety and depression (Sarris et. al 2021); depression is especially characteristic of the collapsed state (Vaknin 2001). However, the moderating effect of ayahuasca as a treatment modality has not necessarily been examined in response to affecting mental illness and the outcome of lifestyle. Personality changes have thus been noted on retreat styled ceremony use, meaning consumers have used ayahuasca in a ceremonial context between one to five times that catalyze lasting changes that permeate lifestyle (Weiss et. al 2021).

Through the use of CSPs, qualitative inquiry reveals that consumers overwhelmingly experience transcendental and immaterial numinous visualizations (Dean 2022), a characteristic of active imagination. It is by evoking experiences of relativistic and subjective phenomena that invites consumers to question their belief systems, which in turn can invoke change that lifts them out of the collapsed state, and they then seek to sustain a connection to numinous, intangible, and gnostic psychic content. By experiencing imaginal realms (Levy 2013), mediated by CSPs, consumers elevate the immaterial over the material, in which they may intentionally or unwittingly become a quiet or conspicuous exemplar of non-consumption.

Cultures, such as the Shipibo, use ayahuasca in ceremonial contexts, and place great importance outside of materialism, and instead, prioritize divinity, prophecy, and healing (Dean 2022). This includes, but is not limited to, the realm of compulsive consumption (Thomas et. al 2013). Thus, when a consumer is able to transcend the collapsed state, a reinvigoration takes place, inviting them to share their states embodying a sense of wholeness that derives from within than the external without (Gonzalez et. al 2021), the foundation of material nations (Dean 2022). Conspicuous asceticism enlists duality to convey a betweenness of Mercurian and Hermetic "light" and "shadow" juxtaposition, such that conspicuous asceticism is both communal and agentic. Hermetic society embraced asceticism to a moderate degree, as they were not a renouncing totalitarianism (De Conick 1996). While Mercury portrays the archetype of both Christ and the Devil, in which both communal and agentic values are consciously integrated (Levy 2013).

H5a: Ayahuasca moderates the relationship between collapsed narcissism and conspicuous asceticism.

## **Classical Serotonergic Psychedelic Medicines: Iboga**

More than ayahuasca, iboga is used to treat compulsive consumption (Mallarino 2022). Like ayahuasca, the ceremonial experience induces a sublime transcendental state where consumers intuitively glean insight belonging to the state of gnosis, a type of spiritual knowledge, rather than episteme, empirical knowledge. Gnostic insight is utilized to transcend collapsed aspects of the consumer. Emergence of gnosis invites a paradigm for consumers to operate from a place of intuition and optimism that signifies an external locus of control, rather than the limited and egoic operations of a myopic nonflow. It is through gnosis that conspicuous asceticism rises from, where then consumers accept a niche to be quiet examples of nonconsumption. As hermetic vessels, they inform other consumers that are mired by the hegemonic imaginary of consumption, and propose to them an alternative way of being in public consumer contexts.

The act of using iboga is more dangerous than ayahuasca, in that it has been known to induce heart failure due to complex chemical interactions (Iyer et. al 2021), and although fatalities are rare, gaining insight to construct the foundation of conspicuous asceticism is not without its risks. In transitioning from the collapsed state, iboga can help consumers transcend conditioned place preferences (CPP), i.e. dependencies, such as ethanol, that mire consumers in the collapsed state, as noted in treatments with mice conducted by Henriques et. al in (2021). To date, there have been no studies conducted on iboga's effects on narcissistic states, although its effects on other forms of mental anguish have been documented (Mate 2021).

H5b: Iboga moderates the relationship between collapsed narcissism and conspicuous asceticism. **Meditation**

In the early 1980s, researchers illumined meditation as a possible treatment modality for well-being (Epstein 1986; Engler 1983; Engler 1984; Epstein and Lief 1981). Researchers of meditation have noted that those drawn to meditative abilities tend to display narcissistic tendencies (Epstein 1986; Epstein and Lief 1981). Consumers use consumption to fulfill their grandiose projects, and when the goals of their grandiose fantasies are not reified, this threatens the sense of self, making them feel unstable. Grandiosity is a function of the ego personality (Freud 1914). According to Lacan in (1988), there are two main components of personality regarding the ego ideal, that is, how consumer subjects view oneself from an ideal perspective, and from that point of view is to thwart the interpretation that one's life could not be seen as anything else than vain and useless. In effect, one must reinterpret their normal life as repugnant, and reluctantly, include their grandiose fantasies, in order to transcend the collapsed state. On the other hand, the ideal ego is the ideal and perfect imaginary that the ego aims to emulate. The ideal ego comes into play during the mirror stage at 6-18 months, and this fantasy construction would come to dominate the rest of the subject's psychic life (Freud 1914; Lacan 1988), which lends credibility to the task of transcending narcissistic dimensions of the grandiose ego ideal as presenting tremendous difficulty.

Unless enlightened states of consciousness are obtained, narcissistic structures that dominate the consuming subject will never be fully released (Pitsch 1991). Though not completely free of narcissism, persistent practice dissolves infantile narcissistic residue, and thus transforms the egoic structures that is impregnated with feelings of shame, guilt, anger, and desire into, with feelings of gratitude, empathy, humor, and wisdom (Epstein 1986; Kohut 1966). This dissolving of the narcissistic residue transforms into a more functional and healthy narcissism (i.e. a healthy ego, or healthy narcissism).

Once past trauma is processed through the dynamic of active imagination, consumers are not bound by conspicuous consumption or compulsive consumption to maintain relationships or to regulate their self-esteem. Rather, a new emergence of being can take place—to be ascetic, and in tandem with empathy, wisdom, and humor in its place where the past has been let go, this newly transformative being (Kohut 1966; Kohut 2009; Kohut 2013), consumers can conspicuously symbolically embody anti-consumption rather than elicit narcissistic supply via external consumption (Cisek, Sedikides, and Hart 2008; Rindfleisch et. al 1997; Hirschman 1991; Belk 1988).

However, according to Pitsch in (1991), he has also stated that during the phase of active imagination, consumers must confront their collapsed state, and the mechanisms of meditation beckon a surrender process. To their surprise, this may exacerbate the rage they are being asked to let go, as emotions for them are compulsive. In turn, they may walk away from treatment modalities, perpetuating their indefinite collapsed state, which may even worsen or stay the same (Pitsch 1991). The worsening of narcissism may not actually be limited to just meditative practice, as scholars have warned about the presence of narcissistic shamans administering CSPs to take advantage of consumers (Homan 2016). Mabit in (2014) has noted that some consumers drinking ayahuasca exacerbates their narcissism. Theoretically, as to why some consumers may reduce maladaptive narcissistic structures, while some worsen, this is unknown, but may be attributed to the experience of available care and supervision, i.e., set and setting (Byock 2018).

H5c: Meditation moderates the relationship between collapsed narcissism and conspicuous asceticism.

## **Forgiveness**

Research on forgiveness and its applications are scant (Tutu et. al 2014). However, forgiveness might come as a self-realization in ongoing experiences of immersing one's self in active imagination (Gonzalez et. al 2019; Apud Pelaez 2020). Forgiveness may be a valuable insight that directly emanates from engaging in treatment modalities that serves to reconnect consumers to communal values. Consumers may look to twelve step recovery programs as a way of learning how and when to forgive (Webb and Jeter 2015; Webb, Hirsch, and Toussaint 2015). Forgiveness is the active mechanism that serves to reconnect consumers to community, and transmute the collapsed narcissistic state into Kohut's conceptualization of transformed narcissism. In forgiveness, consumers become aware of their capacity for dysfunction, but instead make sustainable choices to not act upon such dysfunction through the guidance of wisdom, empathy, kindness, connection, and asceticism, while being careful to not overidentify with pride.

H5d: Forgiveness moderates the relationship between collapsed narcissism and conspicuous asceticism.

## METHODOLOGY

## **Participants**

A total of n = 231 participants were recruited, but only n = 139 participants (73 males, and 66 females) responded to the questionnaire. Respondents were compartmentalized by different treatment modalities regarding meditation, ayahuasca, iboga, and forgiveness with those who have not participated in any treatment modality. Participants were recruited through Arkana Spiritual Center's Facebook group, and a Facebook Iboga group. Meditators were recruited through an online recovery program called Recovery 2.0. Also, participants were recruited through Instagram using #iboga as a search tool and through Reddit's ibogaine forum. Some participants on Instagram and Reddit were personally contacted and offered \$10 Amazon gift cards for their participation. The requirements for the CSP groups were that they had to participate in at least one ceremony, be of eighteen years of age, and be English speaking. Respondents included n = 90 meditators at varying degrees (ranging from daily to monthly on a six point scale), n = 33 iboga users, n = 53 ayahuasca users.

There are two exogeneous or independent variables (IVs) within the conceptual model, which are narcissism and ACEs. In the limited number of quantitative marketing studies discussing narcissism, narcissism is a moderating variable (Bang, Wojdynski, Lee 2019), but other variables such as self-congruity have moderated narcissism and brand loyalty (Fastoso,Bartikowski, and Wang 2018). Narcissism in this study is an independent variable (IV) that has moderating effects between ACEs and compulsive consumption, and between ACEs and conspicuous consumption.

## **Materials/Measures**

The questionnaire included 131 items, including demographic questions (e.g. gender, income, and education levels). The survey consisted of a narcissism scale (Raskin and Hall 1981), a compulsive consumption scale that was adopted and adapted from the Yale Food Addiction scale (Gearhardt, Corbin, Brownell 2016), and conspicuous consumption scale (Roy Chaudhuri, Mazumdar, and Ghoshal 2011), as well as conspicuous asceticism scale, which was a semantic and reverse coded inversion scale of the conspicuous consumption scale. The ACEs scale has been the most popular scale used in ACE studies (Dube, Felitti, Dong, Chapman, Giles, and Anda 2003), and was improved upon ten years later in 2013 (Finkelhor, Shattuck, and Turner 2013), and then again in 2015 (Finkelhor, Shattuck, Turner, and Hamby 2015). Collapsed narcissism was measured by using a depression scale and adapted to fit the theoretical constitution of the construct (Radloff 1977). As far as gauging the treatment modalities, respondents were asked to report the frequencies of their practice, and were asked to answer a sixteen-item forgiveness scale (Maio et. al 2008). The narcissism scale was the only multidimensional construct used in the survey, and the conspicuous asceticism scale was presented as one that has not previously demonstrated any construct validity or reliability (De Vellis 2016). The treatment modality items were not adopted nor adapted from previous research due to their nature of being a sheer reported frequency.

## **Procedure**

At the start of the survey, participants were given an informed option to consent to the study taking place via Qualtrics, and were eligible to opt out at any time given some of the sensitive and personal nature of the questions. Participants started answering questions about narcissism on a five-point scale ranging from least narcissistic at one end, and high narcissism at the other (Wink 1991). In the middle of the survey, participants were asked about their ACEs (Finkelhor, Shattuck, Turner, and Hamby 2015), which included a two-point scale, followed by questions about their consumption, consisting of a seven-point Likert scale. They were then shown questions about treatment modalities near the end of the survey, followed by demographic questions at the very end of the survey. On average, the survey's time of completion was between 20-30 minutes. At the end of the survey, participants were thanked for their participation and were informed about the different treatment modalities as possible avenues for aiding in addiction, materialism and asceticism.

## **Reliability and Validity Analysis**

Both exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses were performed to gauge the robustness of each of the scales to ensure convergent and discriminant validity for seven constructs (Hair 2009), not including treatment modalities as it is determined by frequency with the exception of the forgiveness construct. Each construct and their loadings satisfied convergent validity at a cutoff of 0.4 (Williams, Onsman, and Brown 1996). A reliability analysis was conducted on SPSS for each of the seven constructs to ensure the reliability of each of the scales used in the instrument. A reliability of 0.5 or greater is the general cutoff point for consensus (Hair 2009). A cutoff value between 0.6-0.7 for a Cronbach's alpha is considered acceptable according to Hulin, Netemeyer, and Cudeck (2001). The cutoff value used in this study, however, was a 0.7. Cronbach's alphas for each of the seven constructs was obtained as guaranteeing a strong reliability at the following values, and ranges from 0.7 < α < 1. The smallest composite reliability of the seven constructs was 0.89, and the greatest was 0.94.

The composite reliabilities of all seven factors were at least 0.70 or greater, and the AVE were greater than 0.50 (Fornell and Larcker 1981) that can be seen in detail in Table 1.2. Of the seven factors, only five of them were able to satisfy the criteria of confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), which included authority, need for admiration, exploitation, superiority, and selfsufficiency. ## **Discussion**

For the first time in the marketing literature, a more comprehensive theoretical framework empirically shows that narcissism is mediated by conspicuous consumption with respect to the IV of ACEs. Such a relationship gives marketers a chronological and etiological understanding of consumers' development with conspicuous consumption, although ACE scores do not have a direct relationship with conspicuous consumption. Moreover, substantial evidence has been shown by establishing a direct relationship between ACEs and compulsive consumption. ACEs and conspicuous consumption, while insignificant, is telling in the context that conspicuous consumption may be influenced by parental overvaluation (Fastoso et. al 2018). Parental overvaluation has been speculated as being tied to ACEs and narcissism (Kim and Shaw 2019), but not as an extension of a dimension of ACEs. Research is currently lacking in bridging the gap between parental overvaluation and ACEs. Conspicuous consumption may suggest a stronger link to parental overvaluation. Potentially, consumers in childhood who were both devalued (exposed to ACEs) and idealized (parentally overvalued), may be susceptible to both compulsive consumption and conspicuous consumption respectively.

One of the biggest research gaps this research stream faces is highlighting how measurement scales of narcissism have changed its focus from qualities of personality (Raskin and Hall 1989) toward the experiential and emotionality of consumers (Pincus et. al 2009). The earliest version of a narcissism scale is measured by vanity, grandiosity, exploitation, need for admiration, self-sufficiency, authoritarianism, and arrogance, while newer scales are measured by rage, vulnerability, fantasy, exploitation, self-sacrificing, devaluation, and shyness. Theoretically and historically, narcissism has been associated with addiction, and only recent and emerging scales such as the Pathological Narcissism Inventory (PNI-52) potentially reflect this change of the discourse of narcissism measurement scales over the span of three decades. Dimensions of the latter capture not just qualities, but the experiential and emotional, and thus making its measurement more comprehensive, and thus a PNI-52 may be able to better explain a more powerful relationship with compulsive consumption using narcissism as a predictor variable.

A direct relationship between collapsed narcissism and conspicuous asceticism may result in a choice to publicly go sober. Sobriety as a form of conspicuous asceticism may be expressed through twelve step programs, social media posts, and public lectures (Fox and Bacile 2018). Through these outlets, consumers may elicit praise as a form of narcissistic supply that contemporizes the phenomenon of conspicuous asceticism. Conspicuous asceticism may be used as an identity for consumers to establish communal values that are used to regulate self-esteem. Collapsed narcissism had only one formative factor, and as a new construct that has only been discussed at the theoretical level, its understanding can be benefited by being tested through being composed of two-factors, clinical depression and rage, which it is exigent to use a PNI-52 whose one of seven factors is demarcated by rage.

While treatment modalities do not seem to moderate the relationship between collapsed narcissism and conspicuous asceticism, more investigation needs to be conducted with respect to iboga, as it may be a possible avenue in moderating collapsed narcissism and conspicuous asceticism at p = .06, and a direct treatment for conspicuous consumption at p = .08. A sample size of n =32 is preliminary at best, and given the difficulty to be treated with iboga in ceremonial contexts (i.e. a flight to Mexico or Africa for a one week retreat/clinical visit), larger sample sizes may be promising to reject the null hypothesis of no relationship. However, it was ascertained that iboga has a direct impact on compulsive consumption in treating addiction, and as confirmed in the literature review, more so than ayahuasca at the p<.01 level (β = .219; SE = .326; t = -2.84; p = .005), while ayahuasca was significant at the p< .05 level (β = -.195; SE = .286; t = -2.33; p = .02).

Operationalizing CSP's frequency as a construct in determining its impact on compulsive consumption was insignificant at p>.05 perhaps given the high variability of the frequency where the range of use was between 0 and 100 ceremonies. However, operationalizing CSP use as a categorical variable suggests that CSP use have diminishing returns when referring to the mode and median scores of those drinking ayahuasca Mo = 7 and Md = 7; and iboga Mo = 2, Md = 3. ## **Participants**

A total of n = 67 junior and senior level undergraduate university students were recruited to participate in the study. Students were a mix of majors from business administration, health administration, psychology, marketing, finance, management, and operations & analytics. Their ages ranged from 19-22. To ensure consensual anonymity of the students, no demographic questions were asked from them to control for uncontaminated efforts in the experiment.

## **Materials/Measures**

The questionnaire included a total of 79 items, which did not include demographic questions (e.g. gender, income, and education levels) to ensure anonymity of the students. A gratitude scale (Watkins, Woodward, Stone, and Kolts 2003), narcissism scale (Raskin and Hall 1981), compulsive consumption scale (Gearhardt, Corbin, Brownell 2016), and conspicuous consumption scale (Roy Chaudhuri, Mazumdar, and Ghoshal 2011), as well as conspicuous asceticism scale, and collapsed narcissism scale that was adopted and adapted by Radloff in (1977), which was a semantic and reverse coded inversion scale of the conspicuous consumption scale. Each scale was used to measure the different relationships between each of the aforementioned constructs, then the means of each relationship between groups were compared.

The narcissism scale is compartmentalized into three dimensions of grandiosity/need for admiration (G/NAD), leadership/authoritarianism (L/A), and arrogance (A) (Raskin and Hall 1981).

## **Procedure**

At the beginning of the survey, participants were given a verbal and written caveat to consent to the study taking place via Qualtrics, and were eligible to opt out at any time given some of the sensitive and personal nature of the questions or by personal choice. Participants were given a pre-test to measure their levels of gratitude, compulsive consumption, conspicuous consumption, narcissism collapsed narcissism, and conspicuous asceticism before they began the experiment that took 15-20 minutes to complete. They were required to enter electronic entries for two weeks as recommended by Chaplin, John, Rindfleisch and Froh in (2019). The entirety of the experiment began on a Monday, and even included participating on two weekends in a row. They were to report their completed entries throughout the day for the duration of the two weeks. At the end of the experiment, students were then to fill out the post-test questionnaire for another 15-20 minutes, and then were thanked and notified about the therapeutic nature of gratitude on well-being backed by existing literature via participation guidelines at the end of the questionnaire.

## **Manipulation Check**

Each student was required to check in everyday to report their completed entries through email or to be dropped from the experiment. Because of the nature of the sensitive questions, they were promised anonymity throughout the survey but were required to report completed entries. Through the two weeks, they were required to write three sentences they were grateful for per day. The variability of gratitude, as a manipulation check, can cause differences in the outcome of the dependent variables, which are in this case, narcissism, compulsive consumption, conspicuous consumption, and conspicuous asceticism, but as according to the extant literature, a minimum of two weeks is required to produce meaningful results.

## **Discussion**

A possible theory to explain the relationship between conspicuous asceticism, conspicuous consumption and narcissism is the concept of splitting (i.e. fluctuate between idealization (love) and devaluation (hate). Narcissistic consumers are infamously recognized for their black or white thinking, leaving no constructive grey area to approach consumer conflict (Watson and Biderman 1993). As confirmed by Fournier in (1998), consumers view brands (object-relations) similarly to the way they interact with other consumers (subject-relations). Narcissistic consumers, in the context of subject-relations, tend to split their perceived feelings toward their social communities, and therefore, theoretically this same dynamic can be applied to consumer-brand relationships.

Brand love has been a recently investigated construct (Carroll and Ahuvia 2006; Batra, Ahuvia, and Bagozzi 2012), as well as brand hate (Sampedro 2017), let alone the fluctuation between the two constructs, thus, the emergence of brand splitting may be an appropriate theoretical explanation of conspicuous asceticism. Consumers may utilize brands to elicit narcissistic supply from their peers, but when they do not receive the admiration from their consumer choices, they devalue brands, and may shift to public non-consumption as a way to elicit narcissistic supply. Curiously, the construct of brand forgiveness may then become a relevant topic as brand forgiveness may alleviate consumers' act of devaluation to either then become neutral or idealized (Fetscherin and Sampedro 2019).

Cultivating the positive aspects of narcissism (Campbell 2001; Papageorgiou, Denovan, and Dagnall 2019), specifically superiority, may actually provide a mediating effect via conspicuous consumption, where compulsive consumption (hard addiction) may be switched with conspicuous consumption (soft addiction) as a way of eliciting narcissistic supply from their peers. As previously confirmed in study 1, a transformation in narcissism may utilize conspicuous asceticism in a communal fashion, whereby feelings of gratitude do not render consumers as feeling lack, and therefore, when in a public setting, by default may consciously choose to not participate in rituals of consumption.

Cultivating superiority through tactics such as affirmations and keeping a gratitude journal can decrease compulsive consumption afflicting consumers. Lastly, brand splitting may inform compulsive consumption as consumers make a choice to devalue their addictions, which is agentic in the sense that consumers elevate the act of consumption over human relations, and thus make a conscious choice to elicit praise, admiration, and a sense of self-worth from communal contexts. "There is more wisdom in the body, than in your deepest philosophy." – Friedrich Nietzsche

An antithetical view of placing the wisdom in the body versus consumers' cultural deepest philosophy in context helps us further understand this proverb. Human remains in the Initial Magdalenian period reveal that at the time humans lived on a pre-dominantly meat-based diet with seldom plants and mycelium (Power, Salazar-Garcia, Straus, Morales, and Henry 2015). Bryant and Williams-Dean (1975) documented a coprolite analysis in which human stools dating back between 50,000-300,000 years revealed that zero plant matter was contained in the coprolites, thus consistent with the dietary intakes of those in the Magdalenian period. Plant foods contained less carbohydrates in pre-agriculture, and have evolved over time through human intervention to contain more carbohydrates (Wasserman 2019). Another indication pointing toward appropriate ancestral diets for consumers is humans' stomach acid pH being similar to carrions, which suggest humans initially evolved as meat-based scavengers (Fujimori 2020). Regarding addiction, processed food activates the same neural pathways as consumers in addiction thus signaling higher insulin responses compared to foods low in carbohydrates (Bikman 2020). Understanding the mechanistic role of insulin resistance (wisdom of the body), may help consumers emancipate from this vicious cycle of presumed modern cultural dietary choices (deepest philosophy), if the historical context of human diet is to be considered.

In this vein, the discussion of radical low-carbohydrate diets may strongly support an historical revelation of what constitutes wisdom in the body, and could be considered a viable treatment toward the unmanageability of narcissistic consumption (i.e. compulsive consumption and conspicuous consumption). This was first shown in empirical trial and error by anthropological documentations of Native Americans in their treatment of psychopathologies of tribal members (Rohrl 1970). Narcissism is often tied to chronic pain (Blazer 1981), and often, chronic pain is tied to insulin resistance (Zhai et. al 2016). Endocrinologists have shown that there is a relationship between an indicator of insulin resistance, polycystic ovarian syndrome (POCS), and cluster-B psychopathologies in women, such as exhibitionism, masochism, and sadism that explained 42.8% of the variance in their study (Scaruffi, Gambineri, Cattaneo, Turra, Vettor, and Mioni 2014).

Carbohydrates discharge six times more insulin than animal fat, coconut, olive, and avocado oil (Skrabanja et. al 2001; Yahia and Carillo-Lopez 2019), such that, diets high in certain fats keep insulin levels as low as possible for consumers and would thus minimize chronic pain, and hence mitigate consumer characteristics such as narcissistic rage—which is often a response to pain (Kohut 1970). Therefore, consumers may seek relief from pathological consumer behaviors that mire them in moot points with no relief in sight.

Before colonization, it was documented that Native American communities such as the Ojibwa and Powhatan tribes fed their tribe members copious amounts of animal fat such as bear or duck fat whom they felt were afflicted with narcissism (Rohrl 1970). Rohrl's (1970) article also mentions that specific animal fat relative to consumer tribes' geography may be appropriate for treating psychopathology. Interestingly, animal foods have been considered the most important food source for early humans in the paleolithic era (Chiles and Fitzgerald 2018). Flash forward to the 20th century, the Standard American Diet's institution as per Food and Drug Administration's recommendations, a radical shift in consumers' dietary choices from a high fat diet in the 1970s toward a diet high in carbohydrates, specifically, a diet high in grains and processed sugars was met with much commercial success that is still prevalent in today's consumer culture (Taubes 2013). As metabolic diseases, such as heart disease, cancer, and diabetes has been on the rise with one out of eight Americans qualifying for diabetes, (Taubes 2013), narcissism has also been on the rise (Twenge and Campbell 2013). Hippocrates once said, "all diseases begin in the gut," if narcissism constitutes disease of the mind, its mechanisms may also lie in the gut.

Dairy contains 10% of the strength of prescription morphine (Hazum, Sabatka, Chang, Brent, Findlay, and Cuatrecasas 1981), and also contributes to insulin resistance because of its carbohydrate content (Pereira, Jacobs, Van Horn, Slattery, Kartashov, and Ludwig 2002). Since gluten contains lectins (Gundry and Solimene 2017), and lectins are present in all legumes, including peanuts, legumes may have some adverse effects on the health of consumers, which can result in consumers seeking pain relief.

A postmodern analysis reveals that fruit and vegetables do not dualistically oppose processed food in their sugar composition (i.e. whole foods versus processed foods), but rather functions like a spectrum—from low carbohydrates to high carbohydrates. It has been demonstrated that the body cannot tell the difference between natural sugars and added sugar (Gearing and Mc Ardel 2015). Metabolic tests have shown that sugar affects the brain similar to drug addiction, and its process behaves like alcohol, however, since sugar cannot break the blood-brain barrier, its effects cannot simulate a feeling of drunkenness (Lustig 2013). Bikman (2019) has revealed that 85% of Americans are insulin resistant, and attributes the vicious cycle of metabolic disease to compulsive consumption of carbohydrates as propagated by powerful institutions such as the FDA and American Heart Association.

Insulin-resistant foods contain drug-like compounds and stimulate the brain that may stimulate addiction that could possibly manifest through compulsive consumption or through eliciting esteem via conspicuous consumption.

What has not necessarily been examined is that caffeine in isolation is not typically consumed on its own, but rather is concocted with carbohydrates such as dairy and sugar (Cherniske 2008). These powerhouse combinations can influence conspicuous consumption, as sugar and dairy activate the same neural pathways in the brain as caffeine (Cherniske 2008). Under the influence of multiple substances derived from consumers' dietary choices, this may influence consumers' propensity to conspicuously consume.

One component of dislocation theory emphasizes dietary needs, and given that consumers feel a sense of connection and wholeness (i.e. antithetical symbols to addiction), reconciling consumers with their diets may lay the foundation toward constructing a consumer segment transformed of narcissistic characteristics, and thus conducive to conspicuous asceticism.

## METHOD

## **Participants**

A total of n = 135 participants were recruited, but only n = 77 participants (41 no to low carbohydrates, and 36 Standard American Diet) responded to the questionnaire. Respondents were then split into two independent groups (with distinct elements in the experimental group) depending on their dietary choices. Participants were consistent in their eating regiments over the course of at least six months. Respondents were recruited through Amazon's Mechanical Turk for the Standard American Diet (SAD) group using criteria of employing those who ate processed food for at least six months, and participants eating a no to low carbohydrate diet were recruited through an online health community called Revero, whose members practice a predominantly animal-based protein and fat diet, including and limited to, dairy and coffee. Criteria for recruitment included non-meat eaters as long as processed foods were consumed by consumers for the SAD group of which there were n = 3 vegetarians participating in the study. Conversely, the ketogenic community included limited carbohydrate intake between 5-20 grams of carbohydrates, which were at n = 9. Extensively, the no carbohydrate group, were a total of n = 33 participants (e.g. 19 dairy included, 14 no dairy included. Together in the ketogenic group, there are n = 41 participants. In the control, with the three vegetarians and those following a common American diet, there were n = 36 participants. Each group had to adhere for eating these particular regiments for at least six months as answered on the questionnaire.

## **Materials/Measures**

The questionnaire included a total of 72 items, which included demographic questions (e.g. gender, income, and education levels). A narcissism scale (Raskin and Hall 1981), compulsive consumption scale (Gearhardt, Corbin, Brownell 2016), and conspicuous consumption scale (Roy Chaudhuri, Mazumdar, and Ghoshal 2011), as well as a conspicuous asceticism scale, which was a semantic and reverse coded inversion scale of the conspicuous consumption scale. Each scale was used to measure the different relationships between each of the aforementioned constructs, then the means of each relationship between groups were compared. The narcissism scale is compartmentalized into three dimensions of grandiosity/need for admiration (G/NAD), leadership/authoritarianism (L/A), and arrogance (A) (Raskin and Hall 1981).

## **Procedure**

Before participation, respondents were screened to confirm that they had at least been following the survey criteria for at least six months. According to the screening, most ate a combination of beef, pork, and lamb in that respective order or just beef, seldom any chicken with an emphasis on fatty cuts of meat. N = 32 participants were exclusively animal-based, eating the same combinations of fatty cuts of meat. At n= 9, some were allowed to participate while eating one or two plant-foods either avocado and/or sauerkraut into their regiment, known as ketovore (mostly animal based but with some plants), but remained pre-dominantly meatbased. Moreover, they were also asked if they have been partaking in any active addiction of harmful substances, in which they could reply "yes" or "no." All replied "no."

The Standard American Diet (SAD) group was screened through written criteria on MTurk if they had been eating processed foods and whole grains for at least six months. At the start of the survey, participants were given an informed option to consent to the study taking place via Qualtrics, and were eligible to opt out at any time given some of the sensitive and personal nature of the questions. Participants started the survey by answering questions about narcissism on a five-point scale ranging from least narcissistic to healthy narcissism (Wink 1991). They were then asked about their dietary choices including coffee consumption. On average, the survey's time of completion was between 15-20 minutes. At the end of the survey, participants were thanked for their participation and were informed about the nature of the study regarding speculation on diet's effects on addiction and asceticism in relation to insulin resistance.

## **Discussion**

The relationship between consumer dietary choices and narcissism affirms dislocation theory's dimension of disconnection from adequate nutrition requirements. More research is exigent for bridging the gap of narcissism as a consequence of consumer dislocation. Moreover, dislocation theory has been used as a prevalent explanation for describing the manifestation of compulsive consumption outside of the marketing literature (Mate 2009; Alexander 2000), of which this study confirms reconnecting consumers to ancestral dietary requirements that addresses the issues of dislocation.

Proper human nutrition may also be mediated by narcissism and thus have an indirect effect on conspicuous consumption. While this was a relatively small study at n = 77, with a subsample of n = 41 animal-based consumers over the course of six months, replications are crucial with larger sample sizes, especially given the 88 items, which would call for an adequate sample size of n = 352 (i.e. per every one item requires four respondents) (Hinkin 1995). Fortunately, animal-based diets are becoming increasingly popular, despite this nascent consumer culture, requirements of longer practice may be more sufficient toward the eventual generalizability of this treatment modality on the mediation effects of narcissism and compulsive consumption.

Qualitative inquiries may reveal further insight on the dynamics of how animal-based nutrition may alleviate narcissism and compulsive consumption in consumers by highlighting the effects of well-being. As this consumer culture grows, variations of animal-based respondents can be more effectively compartmentalized, as the majority of the current respondents consumed small amounts of dairy and coffee. A present research gap and key theoretical issue involves eventually investigate comparing strict animal-based consumers (i.e. meat, salt, and water) with current variations such as small amounts of dairy, small volumes of low-carbohydrate vegetables, and coffee. It is important to note that the compulsive consumption scale does not measure active addiction out of ethical concerns, but rather consumers' awareness with compulsive decision-making along with its dynamic.

Unfortunately, while the construct of conspicuous asceticism was validated and found to be reliable in this study, no pathways were found linking it to dietary choices. A minimalist way of eating does not necessarily lead to delineating conspicuous ascetic consumer contexts. Marketing scholars are more than welcome to offer ideas or explore other avenues with conspicuous asceticism as a dependent variable.

## CHAPTER VIII

## LIMITATIONS AND MANAGERIAL IMPLICATIONS

In all three studies, conspicuous asceticism emerged as a reliable and valid construct and was theoretically demonstrated to converge through two theoretical models as a dependent variable in studies 1 and 2. One avenue presented the emergence of conspicuous asceticism through compulsive consumption, while the other conspicuous consumption. Both onsets of conspicuous asceticism do not require any healing modalities to arrive there, but are phenomena that arise out of a sense of consumer choice whose modus operandi is to ultimately achieve narcissistic supply through the dynamic of brand splitting.

Three healing modalities including animal-based diets, meditation, and ayahuasca show promise for treating narcissistic consumption. However, given the small-scale samples of these studies, larger studies need to be conducted with perhaps more strict requirements such as longer dietary restrictions (e.g. years instead of months), and meditators who have not used CSPs or CSP users with no experience with meditation, or whom have not used multiple types of CSPs. It is useful to note that scholars at Harvard did a large scale study of over 2,000 participants and found significant healing for metabolic disease including 96% reporting improvements in mental health (Lennerz et. al 2021), while this small-scale study complements and extends theoretical significance for treating mental health. A caveat must be mentioned that this study's current sample size included roughly 50% dairy consumers, which may have deterred the alleviation of narcissism. Further studies might benefit from requiring longer dietary adherences and restrictions of coffee and dairy. Regarding other modalities for treating compulsive consumption, gratitude and affirmation campaigns may be useful tools for marketers whose practices can trickle down via social media (Fox et. al 2018). It may be incredibly beneficial to researchers in Transformative Consumer Research (TCR) to become diligently informed regarding different treatment modalities that have yet to be explored in marketing studies, such as this one.

Unfortunately, the narcissistic personality inventory has often been criticized for failing to capture the complexity of narcissism along with tests of validity and failure to capture distress and self-esteem (Miller and Campbell 2011), among the quality of relationships between friends and family. Failure to capture a unifying concept is due to the heterogeneity of concepts that do not seem to converge into a research stream (Miller and Campbell 2011, p. 401). Perhaps using alternative scales that measure the complexity and comprehensive nature of the vulnerable and grandiose narcissist could reflect better validity in the future.

The moderating effect of forgiveness at p<.01 was limited to forgiveness of consumers' parents. A more comprehensive forgiveness scale such as forgiveness of self, peers, and perhaps a more general and broader act of forgiveness may generate an interaction effect at potentially the p<.001 level (Seear and Fraser 2014). Consumers' may utilize their ACEs as parts of their identity (Berman, Montgomery, and Ratner 2020), and through collapsed narcissism may be coerced by CSPs to let go of adverse childhood memories. As previously mentioned, the act of forgiveness is often misunderstood and seldom researched (Tutu et. al 2014), and esoteric rituals of forgiveness require further investigation. It is through collapse that transformed narcissism takes place and consumers via ceremonious constraints, given the intensity and inescapable nature of CSPs that can last hours or days, are to emancipate from beliefs that mire consumers in their compulsive consumer behaviors. Addiction to negative thinking encompasses addiction to victimhood, and consumers must then take responsibility to enact forgiveness.

Under the influence of CSPs qualify gnostic insight, and through taking responsibility to forgive, consumers must surrender narcissistic grudge holding (Hamilton 1978). By undergoing the process of active imagination, gnosis reveals to consumers the harm they have caused themselves and their parents by holding grudges thus instigating the state of collapsed narcissism as revelations of the shadow render consumers "apprehensive" (Jung 1939). It is by acceptance that consumers are able to successfully integrate the act of forgiveness (Sanderson and Linehan 1999; Siassi 2007) and shift from compulsive consumption to conspicuous asceticism through the adoption of healthier lifestyle choices that foster conspicuous sobriety via public consumer contexts. Acceptance and subsequently forgiveness transmute the collapsed state into a facilitation of transformed narcissism.

While it is meaningful for marketing scholars to construct theories on deterring consumption for sustainability, environmental and transformative purposes, postmodern scholars could argue that elevating consumption is just another difference in the melting pot of consumer culture. That is, narcissistic consumption should not be viewed as inferior with respect to production in the frame of a modernist interpretation, but rather narcissistic consumption should be one that is elevated and encouraged until narcissistic consumers are ready via a collapsed state, in order to transcend their consumption patterns, given the immense suffering of these experiences, and which is temporarily minimized by conspicuous or compulsive consumption. To quote Gabor Mate, "We should not ask what is wrong with the addiction, but what is right with the addiction?," (Mate 2009).

Consumers, like a phoenix rising out of ash (Levy 2013), can become the new influencers to alter consumer culture discourse and can create social media selfie-campaigns (Taylor 2020; Fox, Bacile, Nakhata, and Weible 2018), at a time of rising levels of narcissism (Twenge, Konrath, Campbell, and Bushman 2008). This transformed narcissism can be conspicuously portrayed in social-media campaigns with ascetic messages that convey messages of well-being, gratitude, empathy, humor, and wisdom (Kohut 2013; Kohut 2009). It is through the reenchantment of meaning, in which consumers are able to recover aspects of themselves that had become fragmented as a result of trauma that they are now able to rise above the disenchantment of trauma-inducing infrastructure whose constitution is scientific materialism, capitalism, and individualism through ceremonious active imagination that then balances and eventually encroaches the organizing principle of pathological forms of consumption.

The treatment modalities proposed in this paper could potentially answer suggestions and concerns to Ozanne et. al's paper in (2011) regarding addiction and materialism, namely conspicuous consumption. Other treatment modalities such as eye movement desensitization and reprocessing (EMDR), other psychedelic treatments, hypnotherapy, and cold exposure are just a few treatment modalities worth exploring for scholars advocating transformative practice with regards to consumer well-being. Furthermore, to connect narcissism with compulsive consumption remains vague, as there is theoretical support but not empirical support. Newer scales as mentioned previously compared to the original NPI may show promise in finding a link. To quote Nietzsche, "I thought a man's shadow is his vanity," vanity being a factor of narcissism, and the shadow being the metaphorical constitution that drives both narcissism and addiction.

Knowingly, this is the first study of its kind to demonstrate analogous effects of ayahuasca and iboga on compulsive consumption, as well as their alleviation of narcissism. One study suggested that ayahuasca does not necessarily cure consumers, but rather through the process of active imagination, consumers are exposed to subliminal discourse that provides insight in which they implement practices to heal themselves (Loizaga-Velder et. al 2014), as confirmed in a study by Kohek et. al in (2022) citing that those who have used ayahuasca tend to make conscious dietary choices and exercise, and as confirmed by the data in study 1, they still battled with compulsive consumption although they drank less alcohol. The act of forgiveness seemingly reintegrates, locates, or grounds consumers back into their family units, and adopting a high-fat animal based diets adds to the act of location, given the constituents of dislocation theory noted in study 3. Attenuating dislocation with respect to pathological consumption reveals that the consumer is perhaps most authentic when they adhere to a strong connection with the natural world.

## **Managerial Implications**

Policymakers may want to review evidence-based practice with regards to CSPs and open programs that facilitate the recovery of destitute consumers rather than subject them to traditional rehabilitation programs whose failure rate of recovery often exceeds the 90th percentile (Banks and Gottfredson 2003). Moreover, transformative consumer researchers may want to look into endorsing meditation paraphernalia (e.g. meditation cushions and singing bowls) to mitigate consumers' narcissism to make an indirect impact on conspicuous consumption. One more product to push via advertisement are the use of gratitude journals as a daily tool in consumers' recovery that may be used in adjunct to a twelve-step program. Another suggestion is to begin campaigns that encourage consumers to attend seminars on forgiveness with an emphasis on harm reduction. Finally, social media marketers with an interest in TCR should employ consumers in recovery to share posts that inspire affirmations of the self along with messages of gratitude, in which simultaneously translate the message of discouraging pathological forms of consumption through conspicuously ascetic contexts.
