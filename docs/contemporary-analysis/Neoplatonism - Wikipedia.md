# **Neoplatonism**

**Neoplatonism** is a version of [Platonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonism) [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy) that emerged in the 3rd century AD against the background of [Hellenistic philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_philosophy) and [religion.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_religion) [\[1\]](#page-22-0)[\[note 1\]](#page-21-0)[\[note 2\]](#page-21-1) The term does not encapsulate a set of ideas as much as a series of thinkers. Among the common ideas it maintains is [monism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monism) the doctrine that all of reality can be derived from a single principle, "the One"[.\[2\]](#page-22-1)

Neoplatonism began with [Ammonius Saccas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonius_Saccas) and his student [Plotinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plotinus) (c. 204/5 – 271 AD) and stretched to the sixth century. [\[3\]](#page-22-2) After Plotinus there were three distinct periods in the history of neoplatonism: the work of his student [Porphyry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porphyry_(philosopher)) (third to early fourth century); that of [Iamblichus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iamblichus) (third to fourth century); and the period in the fifth and sixth centuries, when the academies in [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria) and [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens) flourished[.\[4\]](#page-23-0)

Neoplatonism had an enduring influence on the subsequent history of Western philosophy and religion. In the [Middle Ages,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages) Neoplatonic ideas were studied and discussed by [Christian,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_philosophy) [Jewish,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_philosophy) and [Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_philosophy) thinkers[.\[5\]](#page-23-1) In the Islamic cultural sphere, Neoplatonic texts were available in Arabic and Persian translations, and notable philosophers such as [al-Farabi,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Farabi) [Solomon ibn Gabirol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomon_ibn_Gabirol) (Avicebron), [Avicenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avicenna) (Ibn Sina), and [Maimonides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maimonides) incorporated Neoplatonic elements into their own thinking[.\[6\]](#page-23-2)

Christian philosopher and theologian [Thomas Aquinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas) (1225– 1274) had direct access to the works of [Proclus,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proclus) [Simplicius of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplicius_of_Cilicia) [Cilicia,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplicius_of_Cilicia) and [Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-Dionysius_the_Areopagite) and he knew about other neoplatonists, such as Plotinus and Porphyry, through second-hand sources[.\[7\]](#page-23-3) The German mystic [Meister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meister_Eckhart) [Eckhart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meister_Eckhart) (c.1260 – c.1328) was also influenced by neoplatonism, propagating a contemplative way of life which points to the Godhead beyond the nameable God. Neoplatonism also had a strong influence on the [perennial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perennial_philosophy) [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perennial_philosophy) of the [Italian Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Renaissance) thinkers [Marsilio Ficino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsilio_Ficino) and [Giovanni Pico della Mirandola,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Pico_della_Mirandola) and continues through 19th-century [Universalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universality_(philosophy)) and modern-day [spirituality.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirituality)

# **Origins of the term**

# [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Neoplatonism&action=edit§ion=1)

Neoplatonism is a modern term[.\[note 1\]](#page-21-0) The term neoplatonism has a double function as a historical category. On the one hand, it differentiates the philosophical doctrines of Plotinus and his successors from those of the historical [Plato.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato) On the other, the term makes an assumption about the novelty of Plotinus's interpretation of Plato. In the nearly six centuries from Plato's time to Plotinus', there had been an uninterrupted tradition of interpreting Plato which had begun with [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle) and with the immediate successors of [Plato's Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato%27s_Academy) and continued on through a period of [Platonism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonism) which is now referred to as [middle Platonism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Platonism). The term neoplatonism implies that Plotinus' interpretation of Plato was so distinct from those of his predecessors that it should be thought to introduce a new period in the history of Platonism. Some contemporary scholars, however, have taken issue with this assumption and have doubted that neoplatonism constitutes a useful label. They claim that merely marginal differences separate Plotinus' teachings from those of his immediate predecessors. As a pupil of philosopher [Ammonius Saccas,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonius_Saccas) Plotinus used the knowledge of his teacher and predecessors in order to inspire the next generation[.\[10\]](#page-23-4)

Whether neoplatonism is a meaningful or useful historical category is itself a central question concerning the history of the interpretation of Plato. For much of the history of Platonism, it was commonly accepted that the doctrines of the neoplatonists were essentially the same as those of Plato. The Renaissance neoplatonist [Marsilio Ficino,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsilio_Ficino) for instance, thought that the neoplatonic interpretation of Plato was an authentic and accurate representation of Plato's philosophy.[\[11\]](#page-23-5) Although it is unclear precisely when scholars began to disassociate the philosophy of the historical Plato from the philosophy of his neoplatonic interpreters, they had clearly begun to do so at least as early as the first decade of the nineteenth century. Contemporary scholars often identify the German theologian [Friedrich Schleiermacher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Schleiermacher) as an early thinker who took Plato's philosophy to be separate from that of his neoplatonic interpreters. However, others have argued that the differentiation of Plato from neoplatonism was the result of a protracted historical development that preceded Schleiermacher's scholarly work on Plato[.\[12\]](#page-24-0)

# **Origins and history of classical Neoplatonism**

## [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Neoplatonism&action=edit§ion=2)

Neoplatonism started with [Plotinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plotinus) in the 3rd century AD[.\[1\]](#page-22-0) [\[note 2\]](#page-21-1) Three distinct phases in classical neoplatonism after Plotinus can be distinguished: the work of his student [Porphyry;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porphyry_(philosopher)) that of [Iamblichus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iamblichus) and his school in Syria; and the period in the 5th and 6th centuries, when the Academies in [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria) and [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens) flourished[.\[4\]](#page-23-0)

Neoplatonism synthesized ideas from various philosophical and religious cultural spheres. The most important forerunners from Greek philosophy were the [Middle Platonists,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Platonism) such as [Plutarch,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutarch) and the [Neopythagoreans,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neopythagoreanism) especially [Numenius of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numenius_of_Apamea) [Apamea.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numenius_of_Apamea) [Philo,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philo) a Hellenized Jew, translated [Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaism) into terms of [Stoic,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoicism) Platonic, and Neopythagorean elements, and held that God is "supra rational" and can be reached only through "ecstasy". Philo also held that the [oracles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oracle) of God supply the material of moral and religious knowledge. The earliest [Christian philosophers,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_philosophy) such as [Justin Martyr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justin_Martyr) and [Athenagoras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athenagoras_of_Athens) [of Athens,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athenagoras_of_Athens) who attempted to connect Christianity with Platonism, and the Christian [Gnostics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnostics) of [Alexandria,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria) especially [Valentinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valentinus_(Gnostic)) and the followers of [Basilides,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilides) also mirrored elements of Neoplatonism[.\[14\]](#page-24-1)

[Ammonius Saccas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonius_Saccas) (died c.240–245 AD) was a teacher of Plotinus. Through Ammonius Saccas, Plotinus may have been influenced by Indian thought. The similarities between Neoplatonism and [Indian philosophy,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_philosophy) particularly [Samkhya,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samkhya) have led several authors to suggest an Indian influence in its founding, particularly on Ammonius Saccas[.\[15\]](#page-24-2)[\[16\]](#page-24-3)[\[17\]](#page-24-4)

Both Christians (see [Eusebius,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eusebius) [Jerome,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerome) and [Origen\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origen) and Pagans (see Porphyry and Plotinus) claimed him a teacher and founder of the neoplatonic system[.\[18\]](#page-24-5) Porphyry stated in On the One School of Plato and Aristotle, that Ammonius' view was that the philosophies of Plato and Aristotle were in harmony. Eusebius and Jerome claimed him as a Christian until his death, whereas Porphyry claimed he had renounced Christianity and embraced pagan philosophy.

## ![](_page_4_Picture_4.jpeg)

Presumed depiction of [Plotinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plotinus) and [his disciples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disciples_of_Plotinus) on a [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Roman_sarcophagi) [sarcophagus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Roman_sarcophagi) in the Museo Gregoriano Profano, [Vatican Museums,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vatican_Museums) Rome

[Plotinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plotinus) (c.205 – c.270) is widely considered the father of Neoplatonism. Much of our biographical information about him comes from Porphyry's preface to his edition of Plotinus' [Enneads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Enneads). While he was himself influenced by the teachings of classical [Greek,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_philosophy) [Persian,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_philosophy) and [Indian philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_philosophy) and [Egyptian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egyptian_religion) [theology,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egyptian_religion) [\[19\]](#page-24-6) his metaphysical writings later inspired numerous [Pagan,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paganism) [Jewish,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_philosophy) [Christian,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_philosophy) [Gnostic,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnosticism) and [Islamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Islamic_philosophy) metaphysicians and [mystics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mysticism) over the centuries.

Plotinus taught that there is a supreme, totally transcendent "One", containing no division, multiplicity, nor distinction; likewise, it is beyond all categories of being and non-being. The concept of "being" is derived by us from the objects of human experience and is an attribute of such objects, but the infinite, transcendent One is beyond all such objects and, therefore, is beyond the concepts which we can derive from them. The One "cannot be any existing thing" and cannot be merely the sum of all such things (compare the Stoic doctrine of disbelief in non-material existence) but "is prior to all existents".

[Porphyry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porphyry_(philosopher)) (c. 233 – c. 309) wrote widely on astrology, religion, philosophy, and musical theory. He produced a biography of his teacher, Plotinus. He is important in the history of mathematics because of his commentary on [Euclid's Elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid%27s_Elements), which Pappus used when he wrote his own commentary. Porphyry is also known as an opponent of Christianity and as a defender of [paganism;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paganism) of his Adversus Christianos ([Against the Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Against_the_Christians)) in 15 books, only fragments remain. He famously said, "The gods have proclaimed Christ to have been most pious, but the Christians are a confused and vicious sect."

[Iamblichus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iamblichus) (c.245 – c.325) influenced the direction taken by later neoplatonic philosophy. He is perhaps best known for the compendium The Life of Pythagoras, his commentary on Pythagorean philosophy, and his De Mysteriis. In Iamblichus' system, the realm of divinities stretched from the original One down to material nature itself, where soul, in fact, descended into matter and became "embodied" as human beings. The world is thus peopled by a crowd of superhuman beings influencing natural events and possessing and communicating knowledge of the future, and who are all accessible to prayers and offerings. Iamblichus had salvation as his final goal (see [henosis\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henosis). The embodied soul was to return to divinity by performing certain rites, or [theurgy,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theurgy) literally, 'divine-working'.

After Plotinus (around 205–270) and his student Porphyry (around 232–309), Aristotle's (non-biological) works entered the [curriculum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curriculum) of [Platonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato) thought. Porphyry's introduction ([Isagoge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isagoge)) to Aristotle's [Categoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Categories_(Aristotle)) was important as an introduction to [logic,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic) and the study of Aristotle became an introduction to the study of Plato in the late Platonism of [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens) and [Alexandria.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria) The commentaries of this group seek to harmonise Plato, Aristotle, and, often, the [Stoics.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoicism) [\[20\]](#page-25-0) Some works of neoplatonism were attributed to Plato or Aristotle. [De](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Universe) [Mundo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Universe), for instance, is thought not to be the work of a '[pseudo-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-Aristotle)[Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-Aristotle)' though this remains debatable[.\[21\]](#page-25-1)

[Hypatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypatia) (c.360 – 415) was a Greek philosopher and mathematician who served as head of the Platonist school in Alexandria, Egypt, where she taught philosophy, mathematics and astronomy. She was murdered in a church by a fanatical mob of [Coptic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copts) [Parabalani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabalani) [monks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coptic_monasticism) because she had been advising the prefect of Egypt [Orestes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orestes_(prefect)) during his feud with [Cyril,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyril_of_Alexandria) Alexandria's dynastic archbishop[.\[22\]](#page-25-2) The extent of Cyril's personal involvement in her murder remains a matter of scholarly debate.

[Proclus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proclus) Lycaeus (February 8, 412 – April 17, 485) was a Greek neoplatonist philosopher, one of the last major Greek philosophers (see [Damascius\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damascius). He set forth one of the most elaborate, complex, and fully developed neoplatonic systems, providing also an allegorical way of reading the dialogues of Plato. The particular characteristic of Proclus' system is his insertion of a level of individual ones, called henads, between the One itself and the divine Intellect, which is the second principle. The henads are beyond being, like the One itself, but they stand at the head of chains of causation (seirai or taxeis) and in some manner give to these chains their particular character. They are also identified with the traditional Greek gods, so one henad might be [Apollo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo) and be the cause of all things apollonian, while another might be [Helios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helios) and be the cause of all sunny things. The henads serve both to protect the One itself from any hint of multiplicity and to draw up the rest of the universe towards the One, by being a connecting, intermediate stage between absolute unity and determinate multiplicity. In the Middle Ages most Plotinus' insights will be presented as authored by Proclus.

The [Enneads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enneads) of Plotinus are the primary and classical document of neoplatonism. As a form of [mysticism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mysticism) it contains theoretical and practical parts. The theoretical parts deal with the high origin of the human [soul,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul) showing how it has departed from its first estate. The practical parts show the way by which the soul may again return to the Eternal and Supreme.[\[14\]](#page-24-1) The system can be divided between the invisible world and the phenomenal world, the former containing the [transcendent,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendence_(philosophy)) [absolute One](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_(philosophy)) from which [emanates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emanationism) an eternal, perfect, essence ([nous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nous), or intellect), which, in turn, produces the [world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anima_mundi)[soul.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anima_mundi)

For Plotinus, the first principle of reality is "the One", an utterly simple, ineffable, beyond being and non-being, unknowable subsistence which is both the creative source of the [Universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe)[\[23\]](#page-25-3) and the [teleological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teleology) end of all existing things. Although, properly speaking, there is no name appropriate for the first principle, the most adequate names are "the One" or "the Good". The One is so simple that it cannot even be said to exist or to be a being. Rather, the creative principle of all things is beyond being, a notion which is derived from Book VI of the [Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_(Plato)), [\[24\]](#page-25-4) when, in the course of his famous [analogy of the](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analogy_of_the_Sun) [Sun,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analogy_of_the_Sun) Plato says that the Good is beyond being (ἐπέκεινα τῆς οὐσίας) in power and dignity[.\[25\]](#page-26-0) In Plotinus' model of reality, the One is the cause of the rest of reality, which takes the form of two subsequent ["hypostases"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypostasis_(philosophy_and_religion)) or substances: [Nous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nous) and Soul [\(psyché\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul). Although neoplatonists after Plotinus adhered to his cosmological scheme in its most general outline, later developments in the tradition also departed substantively from Plotinus' teachings in regards to significant philosophical issues, such as the nature of evil.

From the One [emanated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emanationism) different levels of lesser realities known as "Hypostases." At the highest level of reality exists "the One" from which emanates the Nous or the mind. It is the first principle after the One and contains all knowledge in a unified form. It is both the knower, the known, and the act of knowing, embodying a complete unity. The Platonic realm of the Forms is contained within the Nous and acts as the archetype of the sensible world. From the Nous emanates a lesser reality known as the Soul, which receives information from the Nous and actualizes it. This act of "actualization" is the same as the creation of the sensible world, the realm of multiplicity, time, and space. This sensible realm is an imperfect copy of the Nous and the Platonic realm of the Forms. The process of Emanation is beyond temporality as time does not exist in the One, the Nous, or the Soul, but only in the sensible world. Despite their distinctions, these four realities are all part of the same unified reality unfolding within the One.

The original Being initially emanates, or throws out, the [nous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nous#Plotinus_and_Neoplatonism) (νοῦς), which is a perfect image of the One and the archetype of all existing things. It is simultaneously both being and thought, idea and ideal world. As image, the nous corresponds perfectly to the One, but as derivative, it is entirely different. What Plotinus understands by the nous is the highest sphere accessible to the human mind, [\[14\]](#page-24-1) while also being pure intellect itself. Nous is the most critical component of [idealism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idealism) Neoplatonism being a pure form of idealism[.\[note 3\]](#page-21-2) The [demiurge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demiurge) (the nous) is the energy, or ergon (does the work), which manifests or organises the material world into [perceivability.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perception)

The image and product of the motionless nous is the [world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anima_mundi)[soul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anima_mundi), which, according to Plotinus, is immaterial like the nous. Its relation to the nous is the same as that of the nous to the One. It stands between the nous and the phenomenal world, and it is permeated and illuminated by the former, but it is also in contact with the latter. The nous/spirit is indivisible; the world-soul may preserve its unity and remain in the nous, but, at the same time, it has the power of uniting with the corporeal world and thus being disintegrated. It therefore occupies an intermediate position. As a single world-soul, it belongs in essence and destination to the intelligible world; but it also embraces innumerable individual souls; and these can either allow themselves to be informed by the nous, or turn aside from the nous and choose the phenomenal world and lose themselves in the realm of the senses and the finite.[\[14\]](#page-24-1)

The soul, as a moving essence, generates the corporeal or phenomenal world. This world ought to be so pervaded by the soul that its various parts should remain in perfect harmony. Plotinus is no dualist in the sense of certain sects, such as the Gnostics; in contrast, he admires the beauty and splendour of the world. So long as idea governs matter, or the soul governs the body, the world is fair and good. It is an image – though a shadowy image – of the upper world, and the degrees of better and worse in it are essential to the harmony of the whole. But, in the actual phenomenal world, unity and harmony are replaced by strife or discord; the result is a conflict, a [becoming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Becoming_(philosophy)) and vanishing, an illusive existence. And the reason for this state of things is that bodies rest on a substratum of matter. Matter is the indeterminate: that with no qualities. If destitute of form and idea, it is evil; as capable of form, it is neutral.[\[14\]](#page-24-1) Evil here is understood as a parasite, having no-existence of its own (parahypostasis), an unavoidable outcome of the Universe, having an "other" necessity, as a harmonizing factor. #### **Celestial hierarchy**

## [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Neoplatonism&action=edit§ion=17)

Later neoplatonic philosophers, especially Iamblichus, added hundreds of intermediate beings such as [gods,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gods) [angels,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angels) [demons,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demons) and other beings as mediators between the One and humanity. The neoplatonist gods are omni-perfect beings and do not display the usual amoral behaviour associated with their representations in the myths.

- The One: God, The Good. Transcendent and ineffable.
- The Hypercosmic Gods: those that make Essence, Life, and Soul
- The [Demiurge:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demiurge) the Creator
- The Cosmic Gods: those who make Being, Nature, and Matter —including the gods known to us from classical religion.[[citation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed) [needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)]

Neoplatonists did not believe in an independent existence of [evil.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evil) They compared it to darkness, which does not exist in itself but only as the absence of light. So, too, evil is simply the absence of good. Things are good insofar as they exist; they are evil only insofar as they are imperfect, lacking some good which they should have.

Neoplatonists believed human perfection and happiness were attainable in this world, without awaiting an [afterlife.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afterlife) Perfection and happiness—seen as synonymous—could be achieved through philosophical [contemplation.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contemplation)

All people return to the One, from which they emanated.[\[29\]](#page-26-2)[\[30\]](#page-26-3) [\[31\]](#page-26-4)

The neoplatonists believed in the pre-existence, and [immortality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immortality) of the soul[.\[32\]](#page-26-5)[\[33\]](#page-26-6) The human soul consists of a lower irrational soul and a higher rational soul [\(mind\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind), both of which can be regarded as different powers of the one soul. It was widely held that the soul possesses a "vehicle" ([okhêma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okhema)), [\[34\]](#page-26-7) accounting for the human soul's immortality and allowing for its return to the One after death[.\[35\]](#page-26-8) After bodily death, the soul takes up a level in the [afterlife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afterlife) corresponding with the level at which it lived during its earthly life.[\[36\]](#page-26-9)[\[37\]](#page-26-10) The neoplatonists believed in the principle of [reincarnation.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reincarnation) Although the most pure and holy souls would dwell in the highest regions, the impure soul would undergo a purification, [\[33\]](#page-26-6) before descending again[,\[38\]](#page-27-0) to be reincarnated into a new body, perhaps into animal form[.\[39\]](#page-27-1) Plotinus believed that a soul may be reincarnated into another human or even a different sort of animal. However, Porphyry maintained, instead, that human souls were only reincarnated into other humans[.\[40\]](#page-27-2) A soul which has returned to the One achieves union with the cosmic universal sou[l\[41\]](#page-27-3) and does not descend again; at least, not in this world period[.\[38\]](#page-27-0) Certain central tenets of neoplatonism served as a philosophical interim for the [Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity) [theologian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theology) [Augustine of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo)

[Hippo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo) on his journey from [dualistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dualistic_cosmology) [Manichaeism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manichaeism) to Christianity. [\[42\]](#page-27-4) As a Manichaen, Augustine had held that evil has substantial being and that God is made of matter; when he became a neoplatonist, he changed his views on these things. As a neoplatonist, and later a Christian, Augustine believed that evil is a privation of goo[d\[43\]](#page-27-5) and that God is not material. [\[44\]](#page-27-6) When writing his treatise 'On True Religion', even several years after his baptism in 387 CE, Augustine's [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity) was still tempered by neoplatonism.

The term [logos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos) was interpreted variously in neoplatonism. Plotinus refers to [Thales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thales)[\[45\]](#page-27-7) in interpreting logos as the principle of mediation, the interrelationship between the [hypostases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypostasis_(philosophy))[\[46\]](#page-27-8) (Soul, Spirit (nous) and the 'One'). [St. John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_the_Evangelist) introduces a relation between [Logos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos_(Christianity)) and the Son, [Christ,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ) [\[47\]](#page-27-9) whereas [Paul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_the_Apostle) calls it 'Son', 'Image', and 'Form'[.\[47\]](#page-27-9)[\[48\]](#page-27-10)[\[49\]](#page-27-11) [Victorinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marius_Victorinus) subsequently differentiated the Logos interior to God from the Logos related to the world by creation and salvation[.\[47\]](#page-27-9) For Augustine, the Logos ["took on flesh"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incarnation_(Christianity)) in Christ, in whom the Logos was present as in no other man.[\[50\]](#page-27-12)[\[51\]](#page-27-13)[\[52\]](#page-28-0) He strongly influenced [early medieval Christian philosophy.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_philosophy#Early_medieval_Christian_philosophy) [\[53\]](#page-28-1)

# **Origen and Pseudo-Dionysius**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Neoplatonism&action=edit§ion=23)

Some early Christians, influenced by neoplatonism, identified the neoplatonic One, or God, with [Yahweh.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yahweh) The most influential of these would be [Origen,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origen) the pupil of [Ammonius Saccas;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonius_Saccas) and the sixth-century author known as [Pseudo-Dionysius the](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-Dionysius_the_Areopagite) [Areopagite,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-Dionysius_the_Areopagite) whose works were translated by [John Scotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Scotus_Eriugena) in the ninth century for the West. Both authors had a lasting influence on [Eastern Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox) and [Western Christianity,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Christianity) and the development of contemplative and [mystical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_mysticism) practices and theology.

Neoplatonism also had links with Gnosticism, which Plotinus rebuked in his ninth tractate of the second Enneads: "Against

Those That Affirm The Creator of The Cosmos and The Cosmos Itself to Be Evil" (generally known as "Against The Gnostics").

Because their belief was grounded in Platonic thought, the neoplatonists rejected Gnosticism's vilification of Plato's [demiurge,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demiurge) the creator of the material world or cosmos discussed in the [Timaeus.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timaeus_(dialogue)) Neoplatonism has been referred to as orthodox Platonic philosophy by scholars like [John D. Turner;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_D._Turner) this reference may be due, in part, to Plotinus' attempt to refute certain interpretations of Platonic philosophy, through his Enneads. Plotinus believed the followers of Gnosticism had corrupted the original teachings of Plato and often argued against likes of Valentinus who, according to Plotinus, had given rise to doctrines of [dogmatic theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dogmatic_theology) with ideas such as that the Spirit of Christ was brought forth by a conscious god after the fall from [Pleroma.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleroma) According to Plotinus, The One is not a conscious god with intent, nor a [godhead,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divinity) nor a conditioned existing entity of any kind, but is rather a requisite principle of totality which is also the source of ultimate wisdom. [\[54\]](#page-28-2)

#### **Byzantine education**

# [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Neoplatonism&action=edit§ion=25)

After the [Platonic Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_Academy) was destroyed in the first century BC, philosophers continued to teach [Platonism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Platonism) but it was not until the early 5th century (c. 410) that a revived academy (which had no connection with the original Academy) was established in [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens) by some leading neoplatonists[.\[55\]](#page-28-3) It persisted until 529 AD when it was finally closed by [Justinian I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justinian_I)

because of active [paganism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paganism) of its professors. Other schools continued in [Constantinople,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople) [Antioch,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antioch) [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria) and [Gaza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhetorical_School_of_Gaza) which were the centers of Justinian's empire[.\[56\]](#page-28-4)[\[57\]](#page-28-5)[\[58\]](#page-28-6)

After the closure of the neoplatonic academy, neoplatonic and/ or secular philosophical studies continued in publicly funded schools in Alexandria and Gaza. In the early seventh century, the neoplatonist [Stephanus of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephanus_of_Alexandria) brought this Alexandrian tradition to Constantinople, where it would remain influential, albeit as a form of secular education.[\[57\]](#page-28-5) The university maintained an active philosophical tradition of Platonism and [Aristotelianism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotelianism) with the former being the longest unbroken Platonic school, running for close to two millennia until the fifteenth centur[y\[57\]](#page-28-5)

[Michael Psellos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Psellos) (1018–1078), a Byzantine monk, writer, philosopher, politician and historian, wrote many philosophical treatises, such as De omnifaria doctrina. He wrote most of his philosophy during his time as a court politician at Constantinople in the 1030s and 1040s.

[Gemistos Plethon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemistos_Plethon) (c.1355 – 1452; Greek: Πλήθων Γεμιστός) remained the preeminent scholar of neoplatonic philosophy in the late Byzantine Empire. He introduced his understanding and insight into the works of neoplatonism during the failed attempt to reconcile the [East–West Schism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East%E2%80%93West_Schism) at the [Council of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Florence) [Florence.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Florence) At Florence, Plethon met [Cosimo de' Medici](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosimo_de%27_Medici) and influenced the latter's decision to found a new [Platonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_Academy_(Florence)) [Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_Academy_(Florence)) there. Cosimo subsequently appointed as head Marsilio Ficino, who proceeded to translate all Plato's works, the Enneads of Plotinus, and various other neoplatonist works into Latin.

## **Islamic neoplatonism**

### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Neoplatonism&action=edit§ion=26)

The major reason for the prominence of neoplatonic influences in the historical [Muslim world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_world) was availability of neoplatonic texts: Arabic translations and paraphrases of neoplatonic works were readily available to Islamic scholars greatly due to the availability of the Greek copies, in part, because [Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslims) conquered some of the more important centres of the Byzantine Christian civilization in Egypt and Syria.[[citation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed) [needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)]

Various Persian and Arabic scholars, including [Avicenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avicenna) (Ibn Sina), [Ibn Arabi,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Arabi) [al-Kindi,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Kindi) [al-Farabi,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Farabi) and [al-Himsi,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Himsi) adapted neoplatonism to conform to the monotheistic constraints of Islam[.\[59\]](#page-28-7) The translations of the works which extrapolate the tenets of God in neoplatonism present no major modification from their original Greek sources, showing the doctrinal shift towards [monotheism.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism) [\[60\]](#page-28-8) Islamic neoplatonism adapted the concepts of [the One](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Platonism#The_One) and the [First Principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Principle) to Islamic theology, attributing the First Principle to God[.\[61\]](#page-28-9) God is a transcendent being, omnipresent and inalterable to the effects of creation. [\[60\]](#page-28-8) Islamic philosophers used the framework of Islamic mysticism in their interpretation of Neoplatonic writings and concepts[.\[note 4\]](#page-22-3)

## **Medieval Christian Thought**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Neoplatonism&action=edit§ion=28)

This section **needs expansion**. You can help by [adding](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Neoplatonism&action=edit§ion=) [to it.](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Neoplatonism&action=edit§ion=) (September 2024)

The works of Pseudo-Dionysius were primarily instrumental in the flowering of western [medieval mysticism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mysticism) most notably the German mystic [Meister Eckhart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meister_Eckhart) (c. 1260 – c. 1328).

Neoplatonism also influenced [Latin scholasticism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholasticism) for example through the reception and translation of Neoplatonic conception by [Eriugena.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Scotus_Eriugena) Aquinas, for example, have some Neoplatonic elements in his philosophical conceptions that he adapts within an Aristotelian vocabulary.

#### **Western Renaissance**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Neoplatonism&action=edit§ion=29)

Neoplatonism ostensibly survived in the [Eastern Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Christianity) [Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Christianity) as an independent tradition and was reintroduced to the West by [Pletho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemistus_Pletho) (c.1355 – 1452/1454), an avowed pagan and opponent of the Byzantine Church, inasmuch as the latter, under Western scholastic influence, relied heavily upon Aristotelian methodology. Pletho's Platonic revival, following the Council of Florence (1438–1439), largely accounts for the renewed interest in Platonic philosophy which accompanied the [Renaissance.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance)

"Of all the students of Greek in Renaissance Italy, the bestknown are the neoplatonists who studied in and around Florence" (Hole). Neoplatonism was not just a revival of Plato's ideas, it is all based on Plotinus' created synthesis, which incorporated the works and teachings of Plato, Aristotle, Pythagoras, and other Greek philosophers.

The Renaissance in Italy was the revival of classic antiquity, and this started at the fall of the Byzantine empire, who were considered the "librarians of the world", because of their great collection of classical manuscripts and the number of humanist scholars that resided in Constantinople (Hole).

Neoplatonism in the Renaissance combined the ideas of Christianity and a new awareness of the writings of Plato.

[Marsilio Ficino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsilio_Ficino) (1433–1499) was "chiefly responsible for packaging and presenting Plato to the Renaissance" (Hole). In 1462, Cosimo I de' Medici, patron of arts, who had an interest in humanism and Platonism, provided Ficino with all 36 of Plato's dialogues in Greek for him to translate. Between 1462 and 1469, Ficino translated these works into Latin, making them widely accessible, as only a minority of people could read Greek. And, between 1484 and 1492, he translated the works of Plotinus, making them available for the first time to the

West.

[Giovanni Pico della Mirandola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Pico_della_Mirandola) (1463–1494) was another neoplatonist during the Italian Renaissance. He could speak and write Latin and Greek, and had knowledge on Hebrew and Arabic. The pope banned his works because they were viewed as heretical – unlike Ficino, who managed to stay on the right side of the church.

The efforts of Ficino and Pico to introduce neoplatonic and [Hermetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermeticism) doctrines into the teaching of the Roman Catholic Church has recently been evaluated in terms of an attempted "Hermetic Reformation"[.\[63\]](#page-29-0)

## **Cambridge Platonists (17th century)**

# [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Neoplatonism&action=edit§ion=30)

In the seventeenth century in England, neoplatonism was fundamental to the school of the [Cambridge Platonists,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_Platonists) whose luminaries included [Henry More,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_More) [Ralph Cudworth,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Cudworth) [Benjamin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Whichcote) [Whichcote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Whichcote) and [John Smith,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Smith_(Platonist)) all graduates of the [University of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge) [Cambridge.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge) [Coleridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Taylor_Coleridge) claimed that they were not really Platonists, but "more truly Plotinists": "divine Plotinus", as More called him.

Later, [Thomas Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Taylor_(neoplatonist)) (not a Cambridge Platonist) was the first to translate Plotinus' works into English[.\[64\]](#page-29-1)[\[65\]](#page-29-2)

#### **Modern neoplatonism**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Neoplatonism&action=edit§ion=31)

Notable modern neoplatonists include [Thomas Taylor,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Taylor_(neoplatonist)) "the

English Platonist", who wrote extensively on Platonism and translated almost the entire Platonic and Plotinian corpora into English, and the Belgian writer [Suzanne Lilar.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzanne_Lilar)

The science fiction writer [Philip K. Dick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_K._Dick) identified as a neoplatonist and explored related mystical experiences and religious concepts in his theoretical work, compiled in [The](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Exegesis_of_Philip_K._Dick) [Exegesis of Philip K. Dick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Exegesis_of_Philip_K._Dick). [\[66\]](#page-29-3)

[Julius Evola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Evola) incorporated Neoplatonic metaphysics into his vision of [Roman pagan revival,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconstructionist_Roman_religion) aligning with his [Traditionalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditionalism_(perennialism)) critique of modernity. [Arturo Reghini,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arturo_Reghini) an Italian esotericist and collaborator of Evola, also promoted Neoplatonic ideas in his efforts to revive [ancient Roman religion.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_ancient_Rome) [\[67\]](#page-29-4)
