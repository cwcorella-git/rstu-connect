#### **Korea to c. 1400**

### **The dawn of history**

Archaeological, linguistic, and legendary sources support the view that the Korean Peninsula was settled by [Tungusic](https://www.britannica.com/place/Arctic/North-central-and-northeastern-Siberian-groups#ref57865)speaking peoples who migrated in waves from [Manchuria](https://www.britannica.com/place/Manchuria) and [Siberia.](https://www.britannica.com/place/Siberia) They settled along the coasts and moved up the river valleys. These peoples formed the dominant ethnic foundation of the Korean people and developed the [Korean language.](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Korean-language) There was a close relationship between Korean [culture](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/culture) and that of neighboring peoples during the [Neolithic Period](https://www.britannica.com/event/Neolithic) (New Stone Age) and the [Bronze Age.](https://www.britannica.com/event/Bronze-Age) For example, Korean [comb](https://www.britannica.com/art/comb-pottery) [pottery,](https://www.britannica.com/art/comb-pottery) widely used in the Neolithic Period, is commonly found in northeastern Asia; Korean bronze daggers, belt hooks, and knobbed mirrors also display the traits of bronze tools unearthed elsewhere in the region.

## **The Stone Age**

Stone [artifacts](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/artifacts) of the [Paleolithic Period](https://www.britannica.com/event/Paleolithic-Period) have been unearthed at Kulp'o-ri in North Hamgyŏng province (North Korea) and at Seokjang in [South Chungcheong](https://www.britannica.com/place/South-Chungcheong) province (South Korea). Of 13 stratified Paleolithic sites, each cultural stratum produced chipped-stone tools of different shapes. Dwelling sites with round fireplaces were discovered there along with carved pebbles.

The Neolithic Period was well established by 3000 bce. A major characteristic was the use of comb pottery, chiefly found at seashore and river-basin sites, where inhabited places and shell mounds also have been discovered. In addition, [stone](https://www.britannica.com/topic/stone-tool-industry) spears and flint arrowheads have been found, as well as bone hooks and stone weights used for fishing. Remains of the Late Neolithic Period include stone plows and sickles, which indicate the beginning of farming. People lived in [dugouts,](https://www.britannica.com/technology/house-dwelling) mostly shallow round or rectangular hollows with fireplaces in the center that may have been covered with thatched roofs. These shelters were huddled together in groups. The size of such villages is yet to be determined, but [legends](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/legends) indicate the family members lived together, forming clan [communities.](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/communities)

## **The use of metals and the emergence of tribal states**

Bronze ware was probably first used about the 8th century bce, though some scholars surmise that it predates the 10th century. As the [Bronze Age](https://www.britannica.com/event/Bronze-Age) started, the design of pottery changed to undecorated earthenware. The uncovering of such pottery indicates that Bronze Age Korean people lived on hillsides, in dugouts built slightly aboveground. Half-moonshaped stone reaping knives and grooved stone axes used for hoeing show that rice farming was practiced, and bronze daggers and bronze arrowheads indicate participation in wars of conquest. [Dolmens,](https://www.britannica.com/topic/dolmen) used as tombs, which were discovered in southern Manchuria and the Korean Peninsula, show the boundary of ancient Korean culture. Since only prominent persons were buried in dolmens, their number and location indicate the existence of many small Bronze Age tribal states that were probably established by powerful people.

The most advanced state and first known kingdom was [Gojoseon](https://www.britannica.com/place/Choson) (Old Joseon), established in the [Taedong River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Taedong-River) basin, in the northern part of the peninsula. According to [legend,](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/legend) the son of heaven, Hwanung, descended to earth and married a bear-turned-woman, who bore a son, [Tangun,](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Tangun) the founder of Gojoseon. Perhaps Tangun and his descendants ruled a tribal state in which rituals and politics were not separated.

Gojoseon developed into a league of tribes in the area of the Taedong and [Liao](https://www.britannica.com/place/Liao-River) rivers (c. 4th century bce). About this time ironware came to be used. Iron plows and sickles indicate the use of animals in farming and more efficient harvesting methods. Wooden houses were built on the ground, and ondol, a floor-heating device, was developed. The appearance of iron weapons, horse equipment, and coaches indicates that horses and chariots were employed in wars. [Wiman](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Wiman) (Wei Man in Chinese), said to have defected from [China,](https://www.britannica.com/place/China) became ruler of Gojoseon about 194 bce. More likely, he was [indigenous](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/indigenous) to Gojoseon. Wiman's Gojoseon was overthrown by the [Han](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Han-dynasty) [empire](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Han-dynasty) of China and replaced by four Chinese colonies in 108 bce.

#### **The development of ancient states**

#### **The** [Three Kingdoms](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Three-Kingdoms-period)

Apart from Gojoseon, the region of Korea developed into tribal states. To the north, Buyeo rose in the [Sungari River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Sungari-River) basin of Manchuria (now northeastern China). Jin, which had emerged south of the Han River in the 2nd century bce, was split into three tribal states—Mahan, Jinhan, and Byeonhan. These states formed leagues, or tribal federations, centered on a leading state. The tribal leagues stretched across a wide area from the Sungari [basin](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/basin) to the southern Korean Peninsula. They evolved into three rival kingdoms[—Goguryeo,](https://www.britannica.com/place/Goguryeo) [Baekje,](https://www.britannica.com/place/Baekje) and [Silla.](https://www.britannica.com/place/Silla) According to legends, Goguryeo was founded by Chumo in 37 bce, Baekje by Onjo in 18 bce, and Silla by [Bak](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Pak-Hyokkose) [Hyeokgeose](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Pak-Hyokkose) in 57 bce. The actual task of [state building,](https://www.britannica.com/topic/state-building) however, was begun for Goguryeo by King [Taejo](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Taejo) (reigned 53– 146 ce), for Baekje by King Koi (reigned 234–286), and for Silla by King Naemul (reigned 356–402).

The [Three Kingdoms](https://www.britannica.com/event/Three-Kingdoms-ancient-kingdoms-China) shared several common characteristics. They evolved into statehood through frequent wars of expansion, centralized military systems were organized, and training institutions (kyŏngdang in Goguryeo, [hwarangdo](https://www.britannica.com/topic/hwarangdo) in Silla) were developed. The power of the king in each state was strengthened, and hereditary monarchies evolved.

Another common characteristic was the appearance of powerful [aristocracies](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/aristocracies) composed of tribal chiefs who moved to the capital. The aristocrats were divided into several social classes with certain privileges as they advanced socially and politically. Silla's [kolp'um](https://www.britannica.com/topic/kolpum) ("bone-rank") system, in which the families of rulers customarily monopolized political power, was typical. Silla had a state deliberative body, the Council of Nobles (Hwabaek), which made important decisions. The council's membership consisted of men of chin'gol ("truebone") class, who were of the high [aristocracy.](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/aristocracy)

The kingdoms all achieved a centralization of power. Each one was divided into administrative units—the largest called pu in Goguryeo, pang in Baekje, chu in Silla—that controlled many castles. To these provincial units the central government sent officials who made sure that the people, as royal subjects, provided taxes and unpaid labor.

The Three Kingdoms developed highly sophisticated [cultures.](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/cultures) Each compiled its own history, apparently to consolidate the authority of the state. Also noteworthy was the introduction of [Buddhism,](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Buddhism) which was regarded at the time as the state religion for the protection and welfare of the state.
