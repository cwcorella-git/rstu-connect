From the inspired commentary of Story's interlocutors and her own illuminating self-relection, it becomes immediately evident that *he Prison in Twelve Landscapes* is no ordinary prison ilm and no ordinary documentary. he ostensible object of the ilm, the prison, never appears as such in it. Yet it can be amply discerned, if only by way of the dialectical concept of the prison that is produced in Story's innovative aesthetic and political intervention. Indeed, the central accomplishment of *he Prison in Twelve Landscapes* may well be the production of a counter-commonsensical concept of the prison, one that situates it in its totality of socio-spatial relations. We seem to lack at least for now an adequate and convenient descriptive term for that totality, one that would encompass, among other things, the dynamics of the carceral state, neoliberal space, and racial capitalism, as well as the everyday lives of the scenes surveyed in the 12 distinct yet related landscapes that constitute this ilm on the prison without the prison. he method with which Story produces the concept of the prison, however, has a name: the dialectical method.

How could the prison in the United States today be properly represented in a documentary ilm? Story's aesthetic and political choices in *he Prison in Twelve Landscapes* hinge on her spirited engagement with this methodological and even epistemological question. he problem of representation has been intensely debated through the full spectrum of arts, especially in the context of theorizing modernism, postmodernism, and late capitalism, and Story's own strategic thinking on this issue builds on an inluential strand of those contentious discussions. hat vital methodological and philosophical thread runs roughly from Marx, through Georg Lukács and Guy Debord, to Fredric Jameson, the leading US cultural theorist of postmodernity, and the challenge of "cognitive mapping." he pivotal concept Story has taken from that tradition of historical materialism with regard to her task of representing the prison is *reiication*.

his idea of reiication originates in Marx's critique of the commodity form in the irst volume of *Capital*, before being powerfully extended in Lukács's *History and Class Consciousness* and centrally featured in Debord's *Society of the Spectacle*. It can be translated from the original German term *Verdinglichung* into colloquial English as "thingiication." Organically linked to the broader problematics of alienation and abstraction in capitalist modernity, the concept of reiication points speciically to the nefarious consequences of social relations and human subjectivity assuming the "objective" and "natural" appearance and form of "things." According to Story's compelling diagnostic, the US prison under contemporary socio-spatial conditions has become precisely such a "thing," and her documentary method therefore goes very much against the grain of thingiication or reiication, in order to construct a dialectical concept of the prison as an indispensable part of a broader socio-spatial totality.

In so doing, Story ofers a provocative rationale for rigorously eschewing the liberal-humanist temptation to show the prison "as it really is," as it were. Such an approach, even if successful in bringing about a measure of prison reform by virtue of some humanitarian appeal before the entrenched powers, would reinforce a reiied view of the prison that sees its visible infrastructure and its attendant social rationale as inevitable and natural, especially in a world primed for law and order. herefore, Story draws her aesthetic references from more dialectical and less conventionally realist resources, such as Bertolt Brecht's dramatic strategy of de-familiarizing the familiar and Allan Sekula's photographic art of rendering the abstract concrete. As such, her ilm demands not mere contemplation but intellectual labor and participation from the audience; for her political objective, ultimately, is not to reveal everything that there is to be known about the prison but to get rid of it. his is necessarily a collective and radical project, and the dialectical method of *he Prison in Twelve Landscapes* lays bare its bold premise: he call to abolish the prison is also a call to change the world that cannot do without it.
