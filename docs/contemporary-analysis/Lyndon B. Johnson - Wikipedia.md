# **Lyndon B. Johnson**

**Lyndon Baines Johnson** [\(/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English)[ˈ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English)[l](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English)[ɪ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English)[nd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English)[ə](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English)[n](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English) [ˈ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English)[be](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English)ɪ[nz/;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English) August 27, 1908 – January 22, 1973), also known as **LBJ**, was the 36th [president of the United States,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_United_States) serving from 1963 to 1969. He became president after [the assassination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assassination_of_John_F._Kennedy) of [John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_F._Kennedy) [F. Kennedy,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_F._Kennedy) under whom he had served as the 37th [vice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vice_President_of_the_United_States) [president](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vice_President_of_the_United_States) from 1961 to 1963. A [Southern Democrat,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Democrat) Johnson previously represented Texas in [Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Congress) for over 23 years, first as a [U. S. representative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U. S._representative) from 1937 to 1949, and then as a [U. S. senator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U. S._senator_from_Texas) from 1949 to 1961.

Born in [Stonewall, Texas,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stonewall,_Texas) Johnson worked as a teacher and a congressional aide before winning election to the U. S. House of Representatives in 1937. In 1948, he was controversially declared the winner in the Democratic primary for the [U. S. Senate election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1948_United_States_Senate_election_in_Texas) in Texas before winning the general election.[\[1\]](#page-33-1) He became [Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Party_leaders_of_the_United_States_Senate) [majority whip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Party_leaders_of_the_United_States_Senate) in 1951, Senate Democratic leader in 1953 and majority leader in 1954. Senator Kennedy bested Johnson and his other rivals for the [1960](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1960_Democratic_Party_presidential_primaries) Democratic presidential nomination before surprising many by offering to make Johnson his vice presidential running mate. The Kennedy–Johnson ticket won [the general](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1960_United_States_presidential_election) [election.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1960_United_States_presidential_election) Vice President Johnson assumed the presidency in 1963, after President Kennedy was assassinated. The following year, Johnson was [elected to the presidency in a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_United_States_presidential_election) [landslide,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_United_States_presidential_election) winning the largest share of the popular vote for the Democratic Party in history, and the highest for any candidate since the advent of widespread popular elections in the 1820s.

Lyndon Johnson's [Great Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Society) was aimed at expanding [civil rights,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_rights_movement) public broadcasting, access to health care, aid to education and the arts, urban and rural development, consumer protection, environmentalism, and public services. His administration passed many major laws that made substantial changes in civil rights, health care, welfare, and education. Conversely, Johnson is heavily criticized for his foreign policy, namely escalating American involvement in the Vietnam War[.\[3\]](#page-33-3)[\[4\]](#page-33-4)

Johnson's paternal grandfather, [Samuel Ealy Johnson Sr.,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Ealy_Johnson_Sr.) was raised Baptist and for a time was a member of the

[Christian Church \(Disciples of Christ\).](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Church_(Disciples_of_Christ)) In his later years, Samuel Sr. became a [Christadelphian;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christadelphian) Samuel Jr. also joined the Christadelphian Church toward the end of his life[.\[12\]](#page-34-5) Johnson was influenced in his positive attitude toward Jews by the religious beliefs that [his family,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_of_Lyndon_B._Johnson) especially his grandfather, had shared with him. [\[13\]](#page-34-6)

Johnson grew up poor, with his father losing a great deal of money.[\[14\]](#page-34-7) Biographer [Robert Caro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Caro) described him as being raised "in a land without electricity, where the soil was so rocky that it was hard to earn a living from it.["\[14\]](#page-34-7)

In school, Johnson was a talkative youth who was elected president of his 11th-grade class. He graduated in 1924 from [Johnson City High School,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnson_City_High_School) where he participated in [public speaking,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_speaking) [debate,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debate) and [baseball.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baseball)[\[15\]](#page-34-8)[\[16\]](#page-34-9) At 15, Johnson was the youngest in his class. Pressured by his parents to attend college, he enrolled at a "sub college" of [Southwest Texas State Teachers College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southwest_Texas_State_Teachers_College) (SWTSTC) in the summer of 1924, where students from unaccredited high schools could take the 12th-grade courses needed for admission to college. He left the school just weeks after his arrival and decided to move to California. He worked at his cousin's legal practice and in odd jobs before returning to Texas, where he worked as a day laborer.[\[17\]](#page-34-10)

In 1926, Johnson enrolled at SWTSTC. He worked his way through school, participated in debate and campus politics, and edited the school newspaper, *[The College Star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_Star)*. [\[18\]](#page-34-11) The college years refined his skills of persuasion and political organization. For nine months, from 1928 to 1929, Johnson paused his studies to teach [Mexican–American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Americans) children at the segregated Welhausen School in [Cotulla, Texas,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotulla,_Texas) 90 miles (140 km) south of [San Antonio.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Antonio) The job helped him to save money to complete his education, and he graduated in 1930 with a Bachelor of Science in history and his certificate of qualification as a high school teacher.[\[19\]](#page-34-12)[\[20\]](#page-34-13) He briefly taught at Pearsall High School in [Pearsall, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearsall,_Texas) before taking a position teaching public speaking at [Sam Houston High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Houston_High_School_(Houston,_Texas)) in Houston[.\[21\]](#page-34-14)

When he returned to San Marcos in 1965, after signing the [Higher Education Act of 1965,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_Education_Act_of_1965) Johnson reminisced:

I shall never forget the faces of the boys and the girls in that little Welhausen Mexican School, and I remember even yet the pain of realizing and knowing then that college was closed to practically every one of those children because they were too poor. And I think it was then that I made up my mind that this nation could never rest while the door to knowledge remained closed to any American.[\[22\]](#page-35-0)

### **Entry into politics**

After [Richard M. Kleberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_M._Kleberg) won a 1931 special election to represent Texas in the [United States House of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_House_of_Representatives) [Representatives,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_House_of_Representatives) he appointed Johnson as his legislative secretary. This marked Johnson's formal introduction to politics. Johnson secured the position on the recommendation of his father and that of state senator Welly Hopkins, for whom Johnson had campaigned in 1930.[\[23\]](#page-35-1) Kleberg had little interest in the day-to-day duties of a Congressman, instead delegating them to Johnson[.\[24\]](#page-35-2) After [Franklin D. Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt) won the [1932 U. S.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1932_U. S._presidential_election) [presidential election,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1932_U. S._presidential_election) Johnson became a lifelong supporter of Roosevelt's [New Deal.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Deal)[\[25\]](#page-35-3) Johnson was elected speaker of the "Little Congress", a group of Congressional aides, where he cultivated Congressmen, newspapermen, and lobbyists. Johnson's friends soon included aides to President Roosevelt as well as fellow Texans such as vice president [John Nance Garner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Nance_Garner) and congressman [Sam Rayburn.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Rayburn)[\[26\]](#page-35-4)

In 1935, Johnson was hired as Texas state administrator of the [National Youth Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Youth_Administration) (NYA). The job allowed him to travel around Texas seeking financial sponsors for NYA [construction projects.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Construction_project) Within six months, 18,000 young Texans were working on roads, parks, schools, and other public buildings[.\[27\]](#page-35-5) He resigned two years later to run for Congress. A notoriously tough boss, Johnson often demanded long workdays and work on weekends.[\[28\]](#page-35-6) He was described by friends, fellow politicians, and historians as motivated by lust for power and control. As Caro observes, "Johnson's ambition was uncommon – in the degree to which it was unencumbered by even the slightest excess weight of ideology, of philosophy, of principles, of beliefs."[\[29\]](#page-35-7)

### **U. S. House of Representatives (1937–1949)**

In 1937, after the death of 13-term congressman [James P. Buchanan,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_P._Buchanan) Johnson successfully campaigned in a special election for [Texas's 10th congressional district,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas%27s_10th_congressional_district) which included [Austin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austin,_Texas) and the surrounding [Texas Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Hill_Country) [Country.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Hill_Country) He ran on a [New Deal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Deal) platform and was effectively aided by his wife[.\[31\]](#page-35-8) He served as a U. S. Representative from April 10, 1937, to January 3, 1949.[\[32\]](#page-35-9) President Roosevelt found Johnson to be a political ally[\[33\]](#page-35-10) and conduit for information, particularly regarding the internal politics of Texas and the machinations of Vice President [John Nance Garner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Nance_Garner) and [House Speaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speaker_of_the_U. S._House_of_Representatives) [Sam Rayburn.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Rayburn)[\[34\]](#page-35-11) As a member of the [House Naval A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_House_Committee_on_Naval_Affairs)ffairs Committee, Johnson helped plan a huge [naval air training base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_Air_Station_Corpus_Christi) at [Corpus Christi,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corpus_Christi,_Texas) [Texas.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corpus_Christi,_Texas) He also helped establish [shipbuilding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shipbuilding) sites at [Houston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Houston) and [Orange, Texas;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange,_Texas) and a [Navy Reserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Navy_Reserve) station in [Dallas.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dallas)[\[35\]](#page-35-12)

Johnson successfully sponsored many federal projects for his home district. One was a program that provided cheap electricity for farmers under the new Rural Electrifi[cation Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rural_Electrification_Administration) (REA), and he secured approval to complete the hydroelectric Mansfi[eld Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mansfield_Dam) on the [Colorado River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorado_River) near Austin.[\[36\]](#page-35-14)[\[37\]](#page-35-15) He also sponsored projects that gave his Texas district [soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_conservation) [conservation,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_conservation) [public housing,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_housing) lower [railroad freight rates,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freight_rate) and expanded credit for loans to farmers[.\[38\]](#page-35-16) He steered the projects towards contractors he knew, such as [Herman and George](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kellogg,_Brown_and_Root#Brown_&_Root) [Brown,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kellogg,_Brown_and_Root#Brown_&_Root) who financed much of Johnson's future career.[\[39\]](#page-35-17)[\[40\]](#page-35-18)

During this time Johnson maintained a hostile position towards [civil rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_rights) legislation like almost all other Southern Democrat legislators; voting against anti[-lynching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynching_in_the_United_States) legislation, anti[-poll tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poll_taxes_in_the_United_States) legislation and the [Fair Employment Practice Committee.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fair_Employment_Practice_Committee)[\[41\]](#page-35-19)[\[42\]](#page-35-20)

#### **1941 U. S. Senate election**

In April 1941, [U. S. Senator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U. S._Senator) [Morris Sheppard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morris_Sheppard) of Texas died, triggering a [special election.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1941_United_States_Senate_special_election_in_Texas) Texas law allowed Johnson to run without giving up his House seat. The election had no primaries or runoff, forcing Johnson to compete against multiple Democrats, including Governor [W. Lee "Pappy" O'Daniel.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._Lee_O%27Daniel)[\[43\]](#page-35-21)

Johnson campaigned aggressively, emphasizing his ties to President Roosevelt, and initially appeared to be leading. However, after late returns came in, O'Daniel narrowly won by 1,311 votes.[\[43\]](#page-35-21) In *[The Path to Power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Years_of_Lyndon_Johnson#The_Path_to_Power)*, Johnson biographer [Robert Caro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Caro) argues that the election results were manipulated in O'Daniel's favor by lobbyists from Texas' [alcohol industry.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcohol_industry) O'Daniel was a staunch [prohibitionist,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prohibition_in_the_United_States) and as Governor, he had proposed a bill preventing the sale of alcohol within ten miles of a military base; Caro notes how business interests feared the passing of this bill, as preparations for [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) had brought thousands of young soldiers into the region. To prevent the passing of this bill, lobbyists sought to elect O'Daniel to the Senate to place the "wet" lieutenant governor [Coke Stevenson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coke_Stevenson) in the governorship, and hence rigged ballots in East Texas, swinging the election in O'Daniel's favor.[\[44\]](#page-36-0) Johnson ultimately lost by just 0.23% of the vote[.\[45\]](#page-36-1)

Though defeated, Johnson retained his House seat and political network, including support from South Texas political boss [George Berham Parr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Berham_Parr)[.\[46\]](#page-36-2) When O'Daniel declined to seek re-election in 1948[,\[47\]](#page-36-3) Johnson prepared for another Senate run, this time carefully managing vote tallies.[\[46\]](#page-36-2) He would go on to win the 1948 Democratic primary by just 87 votes[.\[48\]](#page-36-4)

#### **Active military duty (1941–1942)**

Johnson was a member of the [U. S. Naval Reserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U. S._Naval_Reserve) when [Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan) [attacked Pearl Harbor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attacked_Pearl_Harbor) in December 1941. Two days after the attack, he requested an indefinite leave of absence and applied for active duty, making him one of the first members of Congress to volunteer for an active military role.[\[49\]](#page-36-5)

Johnson was first ordered to the Office of the Chief of Naval Operations to assess the production and manpower problems that were slowing the manufacture of ships and planes. In May 1942, he was appointed

President Roosevelt's personal representative to the Navy and was sent on a survey mission of the Pacifi[c theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_War) of combat. Stationed in [Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australia) and [New Zealand,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand) it was his duty to find the under-reported problems facing U. S. troops in the Pacific and report this firsthand information to Roosevelt[.\[50\]](#page-36-6)

On June 9, 1942, Johnson received the [Silver Star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_Star) from General [Douglas Mac Arthur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Mac Arthur) for gallantry in action during an aerial combat mission over hostile positions in [New Guinea.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Guinea)[\[51\]](#page-36-7) Though accounts differ, Johnson's aircraft, a B-26 bomber, reportedly turned back before reaching the target due to mechanical trouble[.\[52\]](#page-36-8)[\[53\]](#page-36-9) Johnson's biographer Robert Caro was quoted as saying "I think that the weight of the evidence at this moment is that the plane was attacked by [Zeroes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi_A6M_Zero) and that he was cool under fire",[\[52\]](#page-36-8) but also "The fact is, LBJ never got within sight of Japanese forces. His combat experience was a myth."[\[54\]](#page-36-10)

## **U. S. Senate (1949–1961)**

### **1948 U. S. Senate election**

In [1948,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1948_United_States_Senate_election_in_Texas) Johnson again ran for the U. S. Senate and won the general election after being declared winner in a highly controversial Democratic Party [primary election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partisan_primary) against the well-known former governor [Coke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coke_Stevenson) [Stevenson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coke_Stevenson)[.\[59\]](#page-37-0) Johnson drew crowds to fairgrounds with his rented [Sikorsky S-51](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikorsky_S-51) helicopter, dubbed "The Johnson City Windmill".[\[60\]](#page-37-1) He raised money to flood the state with campaign circulars and won over conservatives by casting doubts on Stevenson's support for the [Taft–Hartley Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taft%E2%80%93Hartley_Act) (curbing union power).[\[61\]](#page-37-2) Stevenson came in first in the primary but lacked a majority, so a runoff election was held[;\[59\]](#page-37-0)[\[62\]](#page-37-3) Johnson campaigned harder, while Stevenson's efforts slumped due to a lack of funds.[\[63\]](#page-37-4)

The runoff vote count, handled by the Democratic State Central Committee, took a week. Johnson was announced the winner by 87 votes out of 988,295, an extremely narrow margin. However, Johnson's victory was based on 200 "patently fraudulent["\[64\]:](#page-37-5)608 ballots reported six days after the election from [Box 13](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Box_13_scandal) in [Jim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Wells_County,_Texas) [Wells County,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Wells_County,_Texas) in an area dominated by political boss [George Parr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Berham_Parr) The added names were in alphabetical order and written with the same pen and handwriting, at the end of the list of voters. Some on this part of the list insisted that they had not voted that day[.\[65\]](#page-37-6) Election judge Luis Salas said in 1977 that he had certified 202 fraudulent ballots, 200 for Johnson, and two for Stevenson[.\[66\]](#page-37-7) [Robert Caro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Caro) made the case in his 1990 book that Johnson had stolen the election in Jim Wells County, and that there were thousands of fraudulent votes in other counties as well, including 10,000 votes switched in [San Antonio.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Antonio)[\[67\]](#page-37-8) The Democratic State Central Committee voted to certify Johnson's nomination by a majority of one (29– 28). The state Democratic convention upheld Johnson. Stevenson went to court, eventually taking his case before the [U. S. Supreme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_the_United_States) [Court,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_the_United_States) but with timely help from his friend and future U. S. Supreme Court Justice [Abe Fortas,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abe_Fortas) Johnson prevailed on the basis that jurisdiction over naming a nominee rested with the party, not the federal government. Johnson soundly defeated [Republican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republican_Party_(United_States)) [Jack Porter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homa_J._Porter) in the general election in November and went to Washington, permanently dubbed "Landslide Lyndon". Johnson, dismissive of his critics, happily adopted the nickname. [\[68\]](#page-37-9)

#### **Freshman senator to majority whip**

During his two terms in the Senate, Johnson drifted rightward. [\[69\]](#page-37-10) He felt he had to tread carefully lest he offend politically powerful conservative [oil and gas interests in Texas,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_oil_boom)[\[70\]](#page-37-11) and in part to curry favor with the chamber's powerful southern chairmen, most notably Senator [Richard Russell,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Russell_Jr.) Democrat from Georgia and leader of the [Southern Caucus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Caucus)[\[71\]](#page-37-12) within the [Conservative coalition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_coalition) that dominated the Senate[.\[72\]](#page-37-13) With Russell's support, Johnson won election as Democratic whip in 1951, serving in this capacity until 1953.[\[32\]](#page-35-9) While serving as whip, Johnson increased his ability to persuade people to reach agreement.[\[73\]](#page-37-14)

As a member of the [Senate Committee on Interstate and Foreign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senate_Committee_on_Interstate_and_Foreign_Commerce) [Commerce,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senate_Committee_on_Interstate_and_Foreign_Commerce) Johnson was chairman of the Senate subcommittee that [refused](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leland_Olds_hearing) the re-nomination of [Leland Olds—](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leland_Olds)who was opposed by Texas oil and gas interest[s\[74\]—](#page-37-15)as chairman of the [Federal Power Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Power_Commission) (FPC) on the grounds that he had been sympathetic towards communism.[\[75\]](#page-37-16) As a member of the FPC, Olds had played a key role in the development of federal regulation of the [natural gas industry.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gas_industry)[\[76\]](#page-37-17)

President Truman favored federal control of [tidelands oil deposits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offshore_oil_and_gas_in_the_United_States) off the Texas coast. Privately, Johnson told friends that he favored federal control, but in public he staunchly defended [states' rights.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States%27_rights) In 1951–52, Johnson helped pass bills granting 47 states control over 3 miles of offshore lands and Texas, 10.5 miles. Truman vetoed the bills, calling them "robbery in broad daylight".[\[77\]](#page-37-18)

#### **Senate Majority Leader**

President Johnson giving "The Treatment" to U. S. Senator [Richard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Russell_Jr.) [Russell Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Russell_Jr.) in 1963

Historians Caro and Dallek consider Johnson the most effective Senate majority leader ever.[\[87\]](#page-38-1)[\[88\]](#page-38-2) He was unusually proficient at gathering information.[\[89\]](#page-38-3) One biographer suggests he was "the greatest intelligence gatherer Washington has ever known", discovering exactly where every senator stood on issues, his philosophy and prejudices, his strengths and weaknesses, and what it took to get his vote[.\[90\]](#page-38-4) [Bobby Baker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobby_Baker) claimed that Johnson would occasionally send senators on [NATO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NATO) trips so they were absent and unable to cast dissenting votes.[\[91\]](#page-38-5) Central to Johnson's control was "The Treatment"[,\[92\]](#page-38-6) described by two journalists:

The Treatment could last ten minutes or four hours. It came, enveloping its target, at the Johnson Ranch swimming pool, in one of Johnson's offices, in the Senate cloakroom, on the floor of the Senate itself – wherever Johnson might find a fellow Senator within his reach. Its tone could be supplication, accusation, cajolery, exuberance, scorn, tears, complaint, and the hint of threat. It was all of these together. It ran the gamut of human emotions. Its velocity was breathtaking and it was all in one direction. Interjections from the target were rare. Johnson anticipated them before they could be spoken. He moved in close, his face a scant millimeter from his target, his eyes widening and narrowing, his eyebrows rising and falling. From his pockets poured clippings, memos, statistics. Mimicry, humor, and the genius of analogy made The Treatment an almost hypnotic experience and rendered the target stunned and helpless[.\[93\]](#page-38-7)

In 1956, during the [Suez Crisis,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suez_Crisis) Johnson tried to prevent the U. S. government from criticizing [Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel) for its invasion of the [Sinai Peninsula.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinai_Peninsula)[\[94\]](#page-38-8) Along with much of the rest of the nation, Johnson was appalled by the threat of possible Soviet domination of [space exploration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_exploration) implied by the launch of *[Sputnik 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sputnik_1)*, the first artificial Earth [satellite,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite) and used his influence to ensure passage of the [National Aeronautics and Space Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Aeronautics_and_Space_Act) [of 1958,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Aeronautics_and_Space_Act) which established [NASA.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA)[\[57\]](#page-36-13) Johnson helped establish the [Senate Aeronautical and Space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Senate_Committee_on_Aeronautical_and_Space_Sciences) [Committee,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Senate_Committee_on_Aeronautical_and_Space_Sciences) and made himself its first chairman[.\[95\]](#page-38-9)

During his tenure as Majority Leader, Johnson did not sign the 1956 [Southern Manifesto,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Manifesto)[\[96\]](#page-38-10)[\[97\]](#page-38-11) and shepherded the [Civil Rights Acts of 1957](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Rights_Act_of_1957) and [1960](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Rights_Act_of_1960) to passage — the first civil rights bills to pass Congress since the [Enforcement Acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enforcement_Acts) and the [Civil Rights Act of 1875](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Rights_Act_of_1875) during [Reconstruction.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconstruction_era)[\[103\]](#page-39-0) Johnson negotiated a middle course between Northern liberal senators and the Southern bloc of senators who had opposed such legislation by removing key enforcement provisions[,\[104\]](#page-39-1)[\[105\]](#page-39-2) such as Title III, which authorized the attorney general to initiate civil action for preventive relief in a wide range of civil rights matters.[\[106\]](#page-39-3) Being a Southerner was seen as an impossible barrier for a presidential candidate[\[107\]](#page-39-4) and towards the end of his Senate career as well as not signing the Southern Manifesto, he distanced himself further from the Southern Caucus in 1959 by joining the Democrat's Western regional conference[.\[108\]](#page-39-5)

#### **Vice presidential nomination**

After much discussion with party leaders and others, Kennedy offered Johnson the vice presidential nomination at the [Los Angeles Biltmore Hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millennium_Biltmore_Hotel) on July 14, the morning after Kennedy was nominated, and Johnson accepted. From that point to the actual nomination that evening, several facts are in dispute, including whether convention chairman [Le Roy Collins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le Roy_Collins)' had the two-thirds majority required to begin the convention's proceedings.[\[114\]](#page-39-11) Kennedy's choice of Johnson as his running mate was intended to attract Southern votes.[\[115\]](#page-39-12) Kennedy was a liberal [Bostonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston) and a [Roman Catholic.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic) Johnson was more conservative, a [Southerner,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_United_States) and a member of the [Disciples of Christ.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disciples_of_Christ)[\[116\]](#page-39-13)[\[117\]](#page-39-14) Nevertheless, labor leaders were unanimous in their opposition to Johnson. [AFL-CIO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AFL-CIO) president [George Meany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Meany) called Johnson "the arch-foe of labor", and Illinois AFL-CIO president [Reuben Soderstrom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reuben_Soderstrom) asserted Kennedy had "made chumps out of leaders of the American labor movement".[\[118\]](#page-39-15)[\[119\]](#page-39-16)

#### **Re-election to U. S. Senate**

At the same time as his vice presidential run, Johnson also sought a third term in the U. S. Senate. According to Robert Caro:

Johnson won an election for both the vice presidency of the United States, on the Kennedy– Johnson ticket, and for a third term as senator (he had Texas law changed to allow him to run for both offices). When he won the vice presidency, he made arrangements to resign from the Senate, as he was required to do under federal law, as soon as it convened on January 3, 1961. [\[120\]](#page-39-17)

Johnson was re-elected senator with 1,306,605 votes (58 percent) to Republican [John Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Tower)'s 927,653 (41.1 percent). Fellow Democrat [William A. Blakley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_A._Blakley) was appointed to replace Johnson, but lost a [special](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1961_United_States_Senate_special_election_in_Texas) [election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1961_United_States_Senate_special_election_in_Texas) in May 1961 to Tower.

#### **Taxation and budget**

The new president thought it advantageous to quickly pursue one of Kennedy's primary legislative goals — a [tax cut.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_cut) Johnson worked closely with Senator [Harry F. Byrd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_F._Byrd) of [Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia) to negotiate a reduction in the budget below \$100 billion in exchange for what became overwhelming Senate approval of the [Revenue Act of 1964.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revenue_Act_of_1964) The act cut individual income tax rates across the board by approximately 20 percent, cut the top marginal tax rate from 91 to 70 percent, and slightly reduced corporate tax rates[.\[150\]](#page-41-4) Passage of the long-stalled tax cut facilitated efforts to move ahead on civil rights legislation.[\[151\]](#page-41-5) Despite a period of strong economic growth, heavy spending on the Vietnam War and on domestic programs contributed to a rising budget deficit, as well as a period of infl[ation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflation) that would continue into the 1970s[.\[152\]](#page-41-6) To counter this, Johnson reluctantly signed a second tax bill, the [Revenue and Expenditure Control Act of 1968,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revenue_and_Expenditure_Control_Act_of_1968) which included a mix of tax increases and spending cuts, producing a budget surplus.[\[153\]](#page-41-7)[\[154\]](#page-41-8)

#### **Immigration**

The sweeping [Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigration_and_Nationality_Act_of_1965) reformed the country's immigration system and repealed the [National Origins Formula,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Origins_Formula) which had restricted emigration from countries outside of Western Europe and the Western Hemisphere. ### **Federal funding for education**

Johnson, whose own ticket out of poverty was a public education in Texas, believed that education was an essential component of the [American dream,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_dream) especially for minorities[.\[224\]](#page-44-9) He made education the top priority of the Great Society agenda, with an emphasis on helping poor children. Buoyed by his landslide victory in the 1964 election, in early 1965 Johnson proposed the [Elementary and Secondary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_and_Secondary_Education_Act) [Education Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_and_Secondary_Education_Act) (ESEA), which would double federal spending on education from \$4 billion to \$8 billion.[\[225\]](#page-44-10) The bill quickly passed both houses of Congress by wide margins.[\[226\]](#page-44-11) ESEA increased funding to all school districts, but directed more money going to districts that had large proportions of students from poor families.[\[227\]](#page-44-12)

Although ESEA solidified Johnson's support among K–12 teachers' unions, neither the Higher Education Act nor the new endowments mollified the college professors and students growing increasingly uneasy with the war in Vietnam[.\[228\]](#page-44-13)[\[229\]](#page-44-14) Johnson's second major education program was the [Higher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_Education_Act_of_1965) [Education Act of 1965,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_Education_Act_of_1965) which increased federal money given to universities, created scholarships, gave low-interest loans to students, and established a [Teacher Corps.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teacher_Corps)[\[230\]](#page-44-15) Johnson also established [Head Start,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head_Start_(program)) an [early education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_education) program to help prepare children from disadvantaged families for success in public schools[.\[231\]](#page-44-16) In 1967, Johnson signed the [Public](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Broadcasting_Act_of_1967) [Broadcasting Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Broadcasting_Act_of_1967) to create educational television programs to supplement broadcast networks.[\[232\]](#page-44-17)

### **1968 presidential election**

#### **Judicial appointments**

Johnson appointed Justices [Abe Fortas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abe_Fortas) (1965) and [Thurgood Marshall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thurgood_Marshall) (1967) to the [Supreme Court of the](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_the_United_States) [United States.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_the_United_States) Johnson anticipated court challenges to his legislative measures in 1965 and thought it advantageous to have a "mole" in the Supreme Court to provide him with inside information, as he was able to get from the legislative branch. Abe Fortas in particular Johnson thought could fill the bill. The opportunity arose when an opening occurred for ambassador to the UN, with Adlai Stevenson's death; Associate Justice [Arthur Goldberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Goldberg) accepted Johnson's offer to transfer to the UN position. Johnson insisted on Fortas assuming Goldberg's seat, over Fortas's wife's objection that it was too early in his career[.\[346\]](#page-50-6)

When [Earl Warren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earl_Warren) announced his retirement in 1968, Johnson nominated Fortas to succeed him as [Chief Justice of the United](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_Justice_of_the_United_States) [States,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_Justice_of_the_United_States) and nominated [Homer Thornberry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer_Thornberry) to succeed Fortas as associate justice. However, Fortas's nomination was filibustered by senators, and neither nominee was voted upon by the full Senate.

## **Post-presidency (1969–1973)**

On Inauguration Day (January 20, 1969), Johnson saw Nixon sworn in, then got on the plane to fly back to Texas. When the front door of the plane closed, Johnson pulled out a cigarette — his first cigarette he had smoked since his heart attack in 1955. One of his daughters pulled it out of his mouth and said, "Daddy, what are you doing? You're going to kill yourself." He took it back and said, "I've now raised you, girls. I've now been President. *Now it's my time!*" From that point on, he went into a very self-destructive spiral.

## **Personality and public image**

According to biographer Randall Woods, Johnson posed in many different roles:

"Johnson the Son of the Tenant Farmer, Johnson the Great Compromiser, Johnson the All-Knowing, Johnson the Humble, Johnson the Warrior, Johnson the Dove, Johnson the Romantic, Johnson the Hard-Headed Pragmatist, Johnson the Preserver of Traditions, Johnson the Crusader for Social Justice, Johnson the Magnanimous, Johnson the Vindictive or Johnson the Uncouth, LBJ the Hick, Lyndon the Satyr, and Johnson the Usurper".[\[375\]](#page-52-4)

Johnson had his particular brand of persuasion, known as "The Johnson Treatment"[.\[376\]](#page-52-5) "There was no more powerful majority leader in American history," biographer Robert Dallek writes. Dallek stated that Johnson had biographies on all the senators, knew what their ambitions, hopes, and tastes were and used it to his advantage in securing votes. Another Johnson biographer noted, "He could get up every day and learn what their fears, their desires, their wishes, their wants were and he could then manipulate, dominate, persuade and cajole them." As president, Johnson vetoed 30 bills; no other president in history vetoed so many bills and never had a single one overridden by Congress. He was often seen as an ambitious, tireless, and imposing figure who was ruthlessly effective at getting legislation passed. He typically worked 18- to 20-hour days without a break and had no regular leisure activities. He stood 6 feet 3.5 inches (1.918 m) tall[.\[377\]](#page-52-6)[\[378\]](#page-52-7)[\[379\]](#page-52-8)

Johnson's cowboy hat and boots reflected his Texas roots and love of the rural hill country. From 250 acres (100 ha) of land that he was given by an aunt in 1951, he created a 2,700-acre (1,100 ha) working ranch with 400 cattle. The [National Park Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Park_Service) keeps a herd descended from Johnson's and maintains the ranch property[.\[380\]](#page-52-9)

Biographer Randall Woods argues that [Social Gospel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_Gospel) themes Johnson learned from childhood allowed him to transform social problems into moral problems. This helps explain his longtime commitment to social justice, and explicitly inspired his foreign-policy approach to Christian internationalism and nation-building. For example, in a 1966 speech he quoted at length from the [Social Creed of the Methodist Church,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_Creed_(Methodist)) adding "It would be very hard for me to write a more perfect description of the American ideal.["\[381\]](#page-52-10)

## **Legacy**

Scholars have viewed Johnson through the lens of both his legislative achievements and his lack of success in the Vietnam War. His overall rating among historians has remained relatively steady, and his average ranking is higher than any of the eight presidents who followed him, although similar to Reagan and Clinton[.\[382\]](#page-52-11) In public polling of [presidential favorability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_rankings_of_presidents_of_the_United_States) of Johnson and the presidents who succeeded him Johnson tends to appear more toward the bottom of lists, typically excepting [Donald Trump,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Trump) [George W. Bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_W._Bush) and [Richard Nixon,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Nixon) and sometimes [Gerald Ford.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerald_Ford)

#### Historian Kent Germany explains:

The man who was elected to the White House by one of the widest margins in U. S. history and pushed through as much legislation as any other American politician now seems to be remembered best by the public for succeeding an assassinated hero, steering the country into a quagmire in Vietnam, cheating on his saintly wife, exposing his stitched-up belly, using profanity, picking up dogs by their ears, swimming naked with advisers in the White House pool, and emptying his bowels while conducting official business. Of all those issues, Johnson's reputation suffers the most from his management of the Vietnam War, something that has overshadowed his civil rights and domestic policy accomplishments and caused Johnson himself to regret his handling of "the woman I really loved — the Great Society."[\[383\]](#page-52-12)
