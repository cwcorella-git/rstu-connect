## Received July 31, 1986

Examination of 131 burials of the Old Silla Kingdom, southeastern Korea, of the mid-4th to mid-6th centuries A. D., gives the major burial types, orientation, method of construction, and relationship of tomb typology to sex of the deceased (for a subsample). From a cluster analysis of 62 graves, according to the presence and absence of 96 types of nonceramic grave goods, five elite rank groups are inferred. The results of the analyses and our interpretations are compared with expectations of Silla burials based on historical descriptions of the kolp'um castelike rank system from several centuries later, and the comparison seems to confirm that the clusters are in fact status groups within the rulers and the aristocracy. # INTRODUCTION

This study concerns the analysis of Old Silla burials found in Kyongju City, in the southeastern portion of the Republic of Korea, dating from the 4th to 6th centuries A. D. Silla appears to have become a confederacy of small states in the 3rd and 4th centuries A. D., conquering the other members in the 5th and 6th centuries, and expanding its sphere of influence across southern Korea. The term "Old Silla" is used to refer to the Silla state from its inception in the first two centuries A. D. to 668 A. D., when it conquered its rivals, Paekche and Koguryo, and unified the Korean peninsula.

Archaeological research on Old Silla began in the early part of this century and culminated in the 1970s with the discovery of several spectacular tumuli which have yielded gold objects and exotic items such as Mediterranean glass (Kim 1987, Kim and Pearson 1977). Despite the richness of these burials, however, little is known of the organization of the Silla state before the 7th century. Although architectural features, particularly from the period of Unified Silla (668–935 A. D.), are coming to light through excavation, few discoveries of structures from Old Silla, other than burials, have been recorded.

In this study, we attempt to infer the nature of social ranking from the late 4th to early 6th centuries through an analysis of burial types and a cluster analysis based on grave goods, excluding pottery. From historical sources which deal with the famous *kolp'um* system of caste-like ranks operating in the later stages of Silla, we generate expectations for the ranking of social classes during the period preceding political integration and test these with data concerning grave goods. We then compare our results with those of P. Ch'oe (1980, 1981) based on different types of burial structures.

One particular vexing question regarding the burial sample is its internal chronology. It is possible that the grouping of burials which we achieve could represent a temporal progression from simple to complex, rather than different social ranks within the same society. We briefly review the problems of chronology and test our groupings against temporal groupings created by Ito (1972) and Morimitsu (1983) showing that our groupings are not primarily chronological.

It appears that our cluster analysis has defined high status social groups which reflect a social ordering similar to what we might expect in the early stages of the formation of the *kolp'um* system. We would expect that the same general principles were followed as the Silla state grew in complexity and territorial control. However, in the one or two centuries before the rigid codification of the *kolp'um* system, we would expect to see greater flexibility in the definition of each social group. Also, our expectations are based on sumptuary laws concerning daily life, rather than burial. We cannot be entirely sure of the symbolism which would be expressed in the burial ritual, particularly in a society which was being converted to Buddhism from the native religion in the early decades of the 6th century.

# Analysis of Burials

This paper applies a number of approaches to the analysis of burials which come from different theoretical perspectives. Pioneers in this field stressed that mortuary practices must be understood in terms of the form and organization of social systems, and that differences in burial treatment were conditioned by the organization of society. Binford, for example, established that the complexity of burial treatment is conditioned by social complexity, as defined by the complexity of subsistence patterns (Binford 1971:18 cf. O'Shea 1984:7). Other writers have argued that to the extent that a mortuary population contains members of various components of a society, the mortuary population can be expected to reflect the structure of the extinct society (Tainter 1977:70). Tainter also developed the idea of energy expenditure in burial practices—that when sets of mortuary data cluster into distinctive levels of energy expenditure, this occurrence will signify distinctive levels of social involvement in the mortuary act (Tainter 1978:125). Energy expenditure includes the complexity of body treatment, the construction and placement of the interment facility, and the extent and duration of ritual mortuary behavior. Tainter points out that in complex societies it is common for the high status of an individual to be marked in a redundant fashion, in which one kind of badge after another reminds the viewer of the high status of the individual.

In contrast, many authors have questioned the assumption that burial organization equals social organization (Kirch 1980, Hodder 1982a:195–199). These stated reservations concerning the relationship between burial structure and social structure and meaning result from ethnographic research which indicated that the most we can hope for is relative social political status, not the societal rank of the participants in the now extinct social system (Kirch 1980:306) According to this view, the burial pattern is codified, and the code of expression can be manipulated in different social strategies. The excavated object can only inform on society through an adequate understanding of cultural context (Hodder 1982b:10).

On the one hand, the earlier approach leads to the quantitative comparison of counts and levels of energy expenditure, while the other tends toward a consideration of the symbolic meaning of particular objects, treatments, and arrangements. Both approaches are informative: the status of different groups may be expressed in different ways in the same society.

The relationship between burial pattern and cultural pattern was found to have several properties. M. Pearson (1982:112) states that groups may use the occasion of mortuary ritual to manipulate social relations to improve their own status. He notes that the burial preparation and ceremony involve living cultural groups who surround the deceased and that objects in the burials must be seen in terms of their mutual interaction. Thus, not all objects in the tombs "belonged" to the deceased in the same way. Some may have been personal ornaments and possessions, while others may have been collective tributes or ritual paraphernalia used in the funeral. Furthermore, recent studies have shown that the same material object may not always indicate the same status; its meaning may change in different situations. From historical Arikara burials, in which status could be checked by independent records. O'Shea found differences in the artifactual expression of chiefly rank and that of ritual offices (O'Shea 1984:271). Chiefly rank was indicated by a central position in the cemetery, an arrow-maker's kit, and other artifacts characteristic of an upper wealth level, whereas, in another case, it was indicated by burial with a stone pipe and upper wealth level objects (O'Shea 1984:271). Similarly, bird beaks, which marked the ritual office at one site, were designators at another site of a special prestige position. Arikara society, admittedly, was undergoing rapid change, but despite the changes in symbolic expression, in our opinion the relative social position of the individual is still recognizable.

The study of spatial arrangements of burials has also been useful in attempting to reconstruct prehistoric social organization. Goldstein carried out two levels of analysis, the formality and spatial separation of the disposal area as a unit and the relation of individuals to each other (Goldstein 1980:7). Goldstein concluded from an analysis of ethnographic cases from different parts of the world, that the existence of a formal, exclusive disposal area for the dead, set apart for this purpose alone, usually means that the society is likely to have corporate groups organized by lineal descent (Goldstein 1980:8). Not all corporate groups will choose to express their identity in this fashion, but in the cases in which the pattern occurs, the existence of such corporate groups is usually the case. It is also most likely that such corporate groups will control access to critical resources.

The analysis presented below follows the general guidelines of O'Shea (1984). We first attempt to establish "culturally generated patterning in the distribution of funerary attributes" (O'Shea 1984:49), then to describe these in terms of frequency and spatial distribution. Next we classify the subsets of artifacts and/or treatments into general types of distinctions by referring to the behavior which produced the particular configurations and the model of social distinctions based on historical data.

Unfortunately, because of the methods of burial and the conditions of preservation, age and sex data are extremely rare in Silla burials. The sex of individuals has occasionally been hypothesized on the basis of particular kinds of grave goods, such as weapons, thought to be sex-specific, but there are almost no independent assessments based on osteological criteria. We were therefore unable to relate these important dimensions to observed pattern of social rank.

We use four separate dimensions for the analysis of social ranks. The first is the assessment of the amount of energy expended, with the general expectation that the higher ranks should show greater energy expenditure, as displayed through the size of the grave or chamber, the size of the earth mound, and the elaboration of the burial treatment, such as the diversity of grave goods and the presence of a wooden coffin. The second dimension is the redundancy of the expression of status, following from the assumption that higher status groups will have a wider range of grave goods to indicate status, so that the number of grave good types should be greater. The third is the hierarchy of wealth items and precious materials. the cultural preferences for certain kinds of status symbols. For this analvsis we used one kind of tomb, the stone-surrounded wooden chamber tomb. The particular pattern of rank of the Silla state, which involved sumptuary restrictions, should create a descending scale of restricted status symbols. Historical sources, introduced below, provide indications of the relative importance of gold, silver, iron, and copper alloys.

The fourth type of analysis is an examination of spatial patterning. Clustering of artifacts, and comparison of the clusters with groupings based on grave size, mound size, and special treatment, can provide evidence for the existence of social ranks, if such ranks exist. In addition to the existence of ranks, Goldstein points out that "the study of spatial patterning in conjunction with studies of artifacts and burial treatment may provide clues about the relationship of status differentiation to corporate and territorial grouping" (Goldstein 1980:10).

# THE CULTURAL CONTEXT OF SILLA BURIALS

There are no written contemporary accounts of Old Silla burials. How-

ever, some accounts of Silla society can be used to construct a series of expected burial patterns related to Silla social organization.

The historian Lee Ki-Baek2 (1984:58) states that histories were compiled in Silla and in the neighboring state of Paekche in the 4th and 6th centuries, respectively, but have not survived. Many of these early written accounts were gathered by Kim Pu-sik, who completed the Samguk Sagi (History of the Three Kingdoms) in 1145 A. D. While his work purports to give the history of the Three Kingdoms from their foundation about the beginning of the Christian era, most authors doubt that the earliest portions can be regarded as accurate factual descriptions (Gardiner 1969:45, 47). The most important part, for our purposes, is the Chi. The first volume of the Chi deals with ceremonies and rituals; the second, with the customs, conveyances, furniture, housing, etc. of the official ranks; while the seventh, eighth, and ninth, treat administrative ranks. Although these descriptions relate mostly to the period of Unified Silla (Kim 1976:15), they do shed some indirect light on the preunification period. Earlier portions of the Samguk Sagi are said to be composed of materials from the Chinese dynastic histories (Gardiner 1969:65) and are considered to present a "sinocentric" view of contemporary Korea (Y. Ch'oe 1980:9).

The Kojiki (Records of Ancient Matters) and the Nihon Shoki (Chronicles of Japan), Japanese documents written in the 8th century, contain references to Korea, but these were not found to be appropriate for the construction of a model of Silla society. Chamberlain points out specifically that the Kojiki makes no reference to the inclusion of burial offerings, and that this omission is "proof (if any be added) of the necessity of not relying exclusively on any single authority, however respectable, if the full and true picture of Japanese antiquity is to be restored" (Chamberlain 1982:1v). Surely this comment is also relevant for the Korean case. In both Japan and Korea, the primary concern in the writing of the first histories was for recording genealogical relationships accurately, since these were essential for the maintenance of the ranking systems of the elite (Philippi 1968:16; Yi 1984).

The early Korean documents provide us with an account of a rigid system of caste-like ranks termed the *kolp'um* (lit. "bone rank") system. In the late fourth century, a rather large confederated Silla kingdom had taken shape (Lee 1982); up to this time, the kingship had alternated among three clans, while after this time it was monopolized on a hereditary basis by the Kim clan. Between the late 4th and the early 6th centuries, outlying regions were incorporated into this state, and power became centralized under the Silla aristocracy. The kolp'um system originally consisted of two different systems, the kol ("bone") system, and the tup'um ("rank") system (Yi 1984:12). The former system was concerned with the royal family and the latter with aristocrats and commoners in the capital, below the rank of royalty. When the administrative laws were later promulgated under King Pophung in the 6th century (Yi 1984:12), the two systems were incorporated. The bone-rank system established hereditary ranks associated with certain privileges and regulations. The topmost rank, of the "hallowed bone" (songgol) was held by members of the Pak and Kim clans who possessed the qualification to become king. The members of the songgol were the ruling king, the king's brothers, the king's brother's children, the king's sisters, and the king's mother. When a new king was chosen, a new songgol group was formed, those members of the songgol who were excluded becoming members of the next rank. The Kim clan monopolized the kingship after 520 A. D. Shortly before unification (668 A. D.), this rank died out, after no male heirs were produced and two queens had ruled. The "true bone" (chingol) were members of the Kim royal house who were not within the circle of closest agnatic relations. In addition, according to Kim (1965:129) the chingol came to include the highest aristocrats and elders from the states which were confederated with Silla (yukch'on) and the kings of the small conquered states. The members of the chingol were the highest nobility below royalty and could become ministers, governors, or members of the king's advisory committee. After the disappearance of the songgol, people of the chingol status did become rulers. The yuktup'um, odup'um, and sadup'um comprised the general aristocracy, forming successively lower ranks below the chingol.

The most detailed extant statement of the *kolp'um* regulations is the Edict of 834 A. D. by King Hungdok, which is preserved in Section 33 of the *Samguk Sagi* (Aoyagi 1975:318-323). It forms the basis of the discussions of the *kolp'um* system by Kim (1965, 1971) (Table 1). For example, King Munmu, who was tranformed into a beneficent sea dragon, gave his son the king a ceremonial belt of black jade. In return for kindness, the son gave the sea dragon gold, precious stones, and five-colored brocade (Ilyon 1972:104–105). Talhae, the first king of the Sok clan, arrived on the shore of Silla in a large box placed on a boat. He was accompanied by the seven treasures of Buddhist lore—gold, silver, glass, the giant pearl clam of India, agate, amber, and coral.

We do not believe that the system of ranking known from the historical records of the Unified Silla existed in the Old Silla, with identical restrictions; however, we feel that the historical records can provide a background against which we can compare the groupings built up from archaeological data, to gain some idea of the degree of ranking and its material expression at the time of the Old Silla tombs.

We may expect some important social distinctions to appear in this period, based on the complex sumptuary laws which had emerged by the 9th century. From the later regulations, it can be seen that these laws restricted access to social valuables and badges. We would expect that the highest rank, subject to no restrictions, would have the greatest number of types of these precious items. Cluster analysis of the burials based on the types of grave goods present should reflect relative rank if one of the clusters contains a much wider variety of items, and if the same kinds of items (functional types such as swords or saddles) are represented in higher quality raw materials, for example, gold and silver, as opposed to gilt bronze and iron. At the same time, if different ranks had access to different government positions, we might expect to find different sets of artifacts marking functionally distinct subgroups.

It has long been noted that the most spectacular, largest tombs have yielded the most gold objects, suggesting that gold was an important marker of high status. A few mentions of gold from the Samguk Yusa seem to bear this out (see above). We might expect gold, gilt bronze, silver, and iron to form a hierarchy of relative value in the expression of rank.

In total, we are dealing with a period less than 200 years in duration. However, if the clusters reflected chronological differences within the period, rather than social strata, we should expect to find temporally distinct styles of artifacts marking the different clusters. We should also expect that the chronological subgroupings achieved by authors such as Ito (1972) and Morimitsu (1983) would be the same as those achieved in our cluster analysis. A grouping based on ranks should cut across a chronological grouping, with the members of the same status group from different chronological periods appearing in the same cluster.

# INTERRELATIONSHIPS OF OLD SILLA GRAVE GOODS

In our analysis we deal with lists of attributes or objects. The characteristics of various tombs are compared, but the total configuration of one burial, with its internal relationships, is not the primary focus of our interest. The interrelationships between artifacts in each grave would be an interesting study in itself and a necessary step toward establishing the cultural context of the burials and reconstructing the actual burial ritual. A further step, which we leave for future scholars, is the investigation of the belief system which provided the rationale for funerary behavior. The bird-like wings of the gold cap ornament may symbolize imperial power, as the eagle or falcon do in other societies. The layers of information present in the multicomponent headdresses of the highest elite may symbolize a complexity of roles present in the central court, but not in outlying areas, where crowns have fewer components.

The placement of the burial structures and offerings in the Tomb of the Heavenly Horse, the Ch'onma-ch'ong (No. 33 in our analysis), illustrates the complex interrelationships between artifacts within a single grave. and suggests the range of symbols utilized to reify imperial power. As reconstructed from the site report (Munhwajae Kwalliguk 1974:451-42), the wooden coffin was first placed at ground level. Many objects adorned the deceased; a gold crown with pendants, rings, earrings, bracelets, and a girdle, as well as a necklace of gold, silver, and colored glass beads. To the east of the coffin was a chest for mortuary offerings. The wooden gift chest, 1.8 m north-south, 1.0 m east-west, and 0.8 m high, contained two gold ornaments for a cap, one wing-shaped and the other butterflyshaped. Other objects were placed on the lid of the chest. Inside the chest, at the bottom, were placed ceramics. Above these were metal vessels and lacquer objects, and on the top, horse gear. The horse gear included four saddles and two pair of mud guards of painted birch bark. There were also two disks of birch bark decorated with mounted figures and birds. Lacquer objects and bronze or gilt bronze vessels were also found, as well as egg shells placed in a pottery vessel. A stone platform was constructed around the coffin, and many grave offerings were set on it.

A large wooden burial chamber was constructed over the coffin and the platform. It was 6.6 m east—west, 4.2 m north—south, and 2.1 m high. Over the wooden burial chamber was placed a mound of river cobbles, rectangular in shape at its base, 23.6 m east—west and 21 m south—north. A layer of mud 15 to 20 cm thick was placed over the boulders, in the area directly above the burial chamber. Finally, the earth mound was constructed. Its maximum height at the time of excavation was 12.7 m.

Around the edge of the tomb was placed a row of boundary stones, each about 20 cm in diameter.

Several kinds of statuses or "social personae" may be represented in the grave offerings described above. The grave goods may be divided into the personal decoration of the deceased, funerary gifts by relatives and subjects, and personal possessions collected during a lifetime, including heirlooms. Personal decoration, consisting of crowns, jewelry, girdles, earrings, swords, and shoes, was placed in the coffin, funerary gifts were buried outside the coffin, and heirlooms were placed in the accompanying boxes or side chambers. This view is expressed by Anazawa and Manome (1987:173) who postulate that the large numbers of objects which are found in the outer compartments of the tombs came from warehouses under the control of the deceased or his or her relatives. In our analysis we grouped all of these together assuming that a composite kind of status could be assessed (cf. Cordy 1981:526). Our sample sizes would have become very small if we had created subdivisions for each of these locations for grave goods.

# Burial Types, Pit Size, Mounds, Orientation, Sex, and the Mean Number of Grave Goods Types

Two types of analyses were carried out. The first was a study of tomb characteristics based on a sample of 131 burials (Table 2). These consisted of most burial excavations from Kyongju City. Our first object was to explore the general variability of the total sample of burials. Missing data from this larger sample precluded multivariate comparisons; instead, the frequencies of major characteristics were explored.

Five major burial types were analyzed, following the classifications of authors such as Kim (1983) and P. Ch'oe (1980, 1981). Sixty percent of the entire sample was composed of the stone-surrounded wooden chamber type (SSWC) known as the choksokmokkwakpun in Korean. This is the most common burial method used by the elite of Old Silla. We are dealing with royalty and the aristocracy. The coffin is part of a wooden frame structure set at ground level, or slightly below, and covered with a mound of rocks and an outer earth mound. In the later part of Old Silla, this SSWC gave way to the stone chamber tomb consisting of an open space reached by a horizontal corridor. Within the class of SSWC we did not make a distinction between the single and multichamber tombs as separate categories. Some authors have argued that the multichamber tombs were family burials used for one or two generations. However, Lee (1986:110-111) found that, in some cases at least, the multiple chambers were cut into each other, suggesting serial single interment over a long time. # Cluster Analysis of the Choksokmokkwakpun (SSWC) Based on Grave Goods Other than Ceramics

Following univariate and bivariate tabulations of grave dimensions and burial types (Hodson 1977), we chose the SSWC, a single class of tomb. for multivariate cluster analysis based on types of nonceramic artifacts. The cluster analysis we chose can be described as sequential, agglomerative, unweighted, and hierarchical. We had to weigh a number of pros and cons in choosing this type of classification. A good deal of debate focuses on the basic premises of cluster analysis—the selection of a large number of equivalent, unweighted characteristics. Christensen and Read (1977:166) reject the technique because they say that those who use it often attempt to classify raw data without consideration of the differing relevance of variables, the existence of variable clustering, or the behavioral complexity reflected in single variables. We agree that there is an enormous amount to be learned about the individual artifacts and their relationships. Nevertheless the technique has been used as at least a preliminary means of data reduction in burial studies. We follow Matson and True (1974:61) in the selection of Ward's method, in which an error sum of squares is used to recalculate the coefficients which form the basis of the clustering. Entities are joined to the clusters only if they optimize the sum of squared distances within the potential cluster. The computer program for the analysis is from SPSSX (1986:732, 776). We transformed Jaccard's coefficient to (1-Jaccard's coefficient) in order to produce a pseudodistance measure (Wood 1974).

Debate surrounds the choice of methods used in building up the clusters, particularly in burial studies. Tainter (1975) and Goldstein (1980) used a monothetic divisive procedure, which successively divides classes by a single attribute at each stage. Braun (1981), arguing for a polythetic divisive clustering procedure, reviewed research indicating "that the monothetic divisive method requires binary variables which are not logically autoassociated or redundant. That is, the presence or absence of a given attribute cannot logically determine the presence or absence of any other" (Braun 1981:406). On the basis of several studies, he states that a polythetic clustering procedure may be more appropriate when there is considerable redundancy. We expect that there is some redundancy in our list of variables, particularly in the saddle fittings; however, we are not willing to collapse all of the saddle information into a single binary variable, because we would lose a great deal of potentially valuable information (Braun 1981:406).

Our final sample for the cluster analysis was 62 burials and 96 types (Appendix 1). Within the groups of SSWC, all artifact types with less than three occurrences and all burials containing less than three artifact types, were removed.7

We predicted that a clustering which indicated rank status groups and the existence of sumptuary regulations should have a number of characteristics: (1) there should be a distribution of precious materials of descending value in the different clusters; (2) the cluster with the highest rank should have the least restrictions and therefore the largest number of different types of artifacts; and (3) artifacts of similar function, but fashioned of different materials, should appear in the different clusters. The structure of the dendrogram (Fig. 2) is characterized by one very distinctive cluster, labeled Cluster 5, and four other clusters, at the rescaled distance of approximately 20.98. Appendix 2 provides the percentage of cases of each artifact type in each cluster for the five cluster solution. We also explored a seven cluster solution (involving subdivisions of Clusters 2 and 4), but found that the five cluster solution was the most informative in the light of our expectations and other studies mentioned below.

The relative status of clusters can be seen from the quality and kinds of goods included in the burials, as well as the degree of exclusive access to these goods. Tables 5, 6, 7, and 8 show particular aspects of the distribution of artifact types in the five clusters. We discuss these general trends before turning to the interpretation of each cluster and the relationship of our analysis to the analyses of others. Table 7 shows the occurrence of different kinds of materials within tombs grouping the attributes by gold, silver, bronze, etc. In each case, Cluster 5 has the highest percentage of cases in which the material is present, with a descending frequency. Gilt bronze is notable because it is generally rarer than gold and is absent in Cluster 1. Iron is ubiquitous. Birch bark generally appears in panels on horse gear and is absent in Cluster 3, which has low quantities of horse gear of every type.

It is puzzling that there were not more jade objects in the highest status tombs, since these were mentioned in the *kolp'um* restrictions. It may be that such objects did not come into use until several centuries later; it may also be that they were too precious to be included as grave goods, being inherited as family heirlooms. # Interpretation of Individual Clusters

Cluster 5, whose members show close similarity, contains exclusively the gold cap ornament and gilt bronze buckles, as well as several vessel types. It contains the largest number of attributes and, consistently, the highest percentages of precious metals. # Subclusters within Clusters 2 and 4

Clusters 2 and 4 in the five cluster solution each display two conspicuous subclusters (Fig. 2). In the case of Cluster 2 it is interesting that among the common items, there are no gold or silver crowns or girdles. Knives and swords are common and relatively numerous. Horse gear is much more common in Subcluster 1, which is characterized by relatively few items at low frequencies. Subcluster 1 has a number of common crown ornaments and regalia, but they also occur in low frequencies. The fact that a large number of characteristics is shared, and that these occur in high frequencies, convinced us that it is better to maintain Cluster 2 as a single cluster.

On the other hand, Cluster 4 has fewer items shared by the two sub-

clusters. The most numerous are iron swords and gilt bronze earrings with thin solid rings. Subcluster 1 has much more horse gear, of bronze and iron, than Subcluster 2. Subcluster 2 is distinguished by relatively high percentages of iron spearheads and iron sickles. The spearheads and sickles may represent a lower social group than the one represented by Subcluster 1—perhaps a group which did not have easy access to equestrian gear. While there are some interesting differences in the two subclusters, they do not seem to be expressed very strongly, judging from the low frequency of items. Nor can we find very clear correlates in the historical materials from which we derived our expectations.

# COMPARISONS WITH OTHER ANALYSES

In his analysis of types of Silla tombs, P. Ch'oe (1981:28-49) created six groups, which he interpreted as belonging to Old Silla rank groups. He based his groupings on tomb construction and grave goods. Groups 1-3 consisted of mound burials. Within these, he made distinctions on the basis of artifacts. The first type of tomb, consisting of the twin and mounded tombs, yielded many gold, gilt bronze, silver, and jade artifacts, such as gold crowns, gold girdles, many iron weapons, and swords and knives decorated with gold, gilt bronze, and silver. This type also yielded gold vessels, glass vessels, and lacquer wares. The second group yielded no gold crowns, but gilt bronze crowns, silver bracelets, rings, mostly of gold, and earrings. Weapons and horse gear were reduced in number in comparison to the first group, and there were no vessels of gold, gilt bronze, silver, or glass. The first and second group of Ch'oe are composed of all of the members of our Cluster 5, our highest status group.

Below these three groups, Ch'oe placed his Collective Tomb Type II, the Collective Tomb Type I, and the Multichamber Tomb, in descending order. He believed that the collective burials were family graves; however, as we indicate above, we believe that at least some of them are single burials separated by more than a generation (see also Lee 1986:119).

Table 9 shows very close agreement between Ch'oe's analysis and ours in the interpretation of the highest group. # Comparison with Chronological Groupings of Ito (1972) and Morimitsu (1983)

Ito (1972) attempted to establish an independent chronology based on stylistic differences in earrings. He examined 120 graves, with 139 specimens. The earrings had three constituents—ring and middle portions and a pendant. Type A had a wire middle portion; Type B, a narrow metal plate; and Type C, a wider metal plate. Ito also examined the patterning which occurred in the other kinds of grave offerings when the tombs were arranged on the basis of their temporal position according to the earrings, and found the chronological arrangements compatible. Three time periods, based on the three styles, were established. A fourth, Period D, was composed of tombs which, on the grounds of other artifact styles and construction type, followed Period C. Period A covered the later 4th century; Period B, 450–520 A. D.; Period C, 520–634 A. D.; and Period D, the remaining portion of the 7th century.

Our sample, focusing on the SSWC tomb type, did not contain all of the cases in Ito's sample, but there was sufficient overlap to compare the cluster membership of more than half of the cases. Type A contained members of Clusters 1 and 2; Type B, Clusters 2, 3, 4, and 5; and Type C, Clusters 2 and 5. Type D did not contain examples of SSWC tombs, which disappeared with the advent of Buddhism in Type C times. Although we cannot show that all of the chronological groups contain all of the clusters, we can show that the clusters cross cut the chronological groups B and C. There is a possibility that some of the graves that we have assigned to a lower social rank are earlier chronologically, but this seems to apply only to Cluster 1.

Generally we found confirmation of Ch'oe's conclusion that contemporary status groups could be identified. Our selection of classes of artifacts by their "use" and their material, rather than by their stylistic variation, was intended to monitor social, instead of chronological variability. Of course, more work on temporal control within the two-century span of construction would strengthen future research. A review of a number of chronological studies, using different classes of artifacts, has been published by Pearson (1985). In one of these, Morimitsu (1983) postulated a relative ordering, from early to late, of the so-called royal tombs: (our numbers) 35, 34 gap, 57, 58, 33, and 45. All of these, except No. 58, occur in Cluster 5. An additional check of P. Ch'oe's chronology (1987) of 21 major burials (19 of which appeared in our cluster analysis) showed that all clusters except Cluster 1 were spread throughout the six periods. This seems to confirm that we are dealing with social groups rather than chronological periods. Cluster 1 as mentioned above may be more common in the early part of the sequence.

The compiling of the map, largely from individual site reports, was difficult, since many common points of reference from the time of excavation, as much as 60 years ago, have been changed. We also attempted to examine orientation and sex of the burials, but the data were incomplete. We first used a sample of 131 burials of several types. The most numerous of these was the SSWC tomb, considered to be typical of the Kyongju area. For this group, we did a cluster analysis and a spatial plotting of the members of the clusters in the five cluster solution.

From historical records we built up simple expectations about social status in the kingdom of Old Silla (Table 1). Although the burials date from the 4th to 6th centuries A. D., existing historical materials come from several centuries later. The distinctive feature of the Silla state in general is its series of caste-like ranks of the elite, which are known to have been in full force from the 7th century, after Silla unified the Korean peninsula. From this kolp'um system, we postulated that social groupings of the burials should display a number of characteristics. There should be a distribution of materials of descending value in the different ranks, the highest rank having the least restriction or the largest number of different kinds of grave goods. Artifacts of different kinds of precious material should appear in the graves of members of the different ranks.

Next we attempted to classify the subsets of the analysis into general types of distinctions, by referring to the behavior that produced the particular configurations and also by referring to our model of social distinctions based on the historical data. We chose a five cluster solution to the cluster analysis using Ward's method (Fig. 2). Our cluster analysis was based on 96 types of grave goods, based on the use of the objects and their material rather than on stylistic variations. We inferred five social ranks of the rulers and the aristocracy. The cluster analysis came close to our expectations of the nature of the patterning of the grave goods under the regulation of sumptuary laws. We examined further subclusters, but rejected them in favor of the 5 cluster solution. Our results compared favorably with those achieved by P. Ch'oe (1981) (Table 9) which inferred social ranks from burial constructions; however, our analysis brought out Cluster 3, the second ranked group, as a distinct entity, whereas in Ch'oe's analysis this group was not distinct.

In general, our clusters crosscut the chronological groupings of Ito (1972) based on earring styles and Morimitsu (1983) based on a wide spectrum of grave goods. This gives us some reassurance that we do have social groupings. We did not find any clear trends in the orientation or the sex of the burials, in relation to the clusters.

We found a general agreement between levels of energy consumption and social status inferred from the cluster analysis, showing that the more elaborate burial treatment (i.e., presence of a wooden coffin inside the wooden frame, higher average numbers of grave goods, and larger grave mounds) were associated with the higher rank groups. The Japanese Nihongi, said to be written in 720 A. D. (Aston 1956:xi), contains a passage of regulations on tomb construction for high ranking persons in Book II, Chapter XXIV, describing the reign of Emperor Kotoku, thought to have assumed the throne in 645 A. D. (Aston 1956:II:218-219, Kidder 1966:160). The latter portion of the *Nihongi*, in which this section occurs. is thought to be historically accurate (Aston 1956;ix). At this time, ostensibly under the influence of Buddhism, the inclusion of gold and silver and other precious objects as grave goods was prohibited; however, five grades of tomb construction were stipulated. Princes and persons of higher rank should have tombs five fathoms high built by 1000 laborers in 7 days; those of superior ministers, three fathoms high, completed by 500 laborers in 5 days; those of lower class ministers, 2½ fathoms high, completed by 250 laborers in 3 days; those of Dainin or Shonin rank, no mound, completed by 100 laborers in 1 day; those of Dairei to Shochi, completed by 50 laborers in 1 day. Energy expenditure seems to have been clearly regulated in this case. The Japanese elite was in close contact with the elite of Silla and other Korean states by this time period. Such regulations seem to have been common throughout East Asia at this time.

Our analysis fits our general expectations based on historical data about the *kolp'um* system. The system as it appears archaeologically is not as rigidly defined as it is in the description of the *kolp'um*, in later periods. It is to be expected that the sumptuary regulations would not be as formalized in the 5th and 6th centuries as in later times. Also, it is doubtful whether the system functioned as rigorously in reality as it did in the ideal.

During the shift from tribal to state organization, Friedman and Rowlands (1977:211-212) describe a pattern of increasing internal control over both the local labor force and its output, as well as wealth objects, used for the payment of debt, marriage, funerals, etc. The structure which is evolving is a "conical clan," in which all noble lineages owe their position to a single sacred royal line (Friedman and Rowlands 1977:218). This structure lies at the center of an "Asiatic state," in which the economic flows organized by affinal and feasting relations change to one in which surplus is absorbed in the vertical tributary relations of the conical structure (Friedman and Rowlands 1977:218). We do not know concretely the precise economic organization of the Old Silla state; however, the vertical nature of the social organization seems to be clear from the archaeological and historical record.

The royal line must control the clans which were formerly dispersed around its periphery, but which have now been conquered. In order to maintain its position it must open communication with the chiefs of these groups; this may be achieved by a council of elders, responsible for the affairs of the territory. Such a council did exist; according to Kim (1965:126), it was composed of village elders who were given ranked positions in the *kolp'um* system.

Marriage between the highest ranked lineage and subordinate lineages would produce offspring whose social position would be determined by the status of both the father and the mother, and their relation to the king or queen. However, the social position of competing lesser lineages of the mothers could lead to conflict in succession and other routes of access to power, particularly if the king had many wives; sumptuary laws would serve to preserve and enforce social distinctions in this situation (Friedman and Rowlands 1977:221). At the same time, bureaucratization would also serve to regulate the claims of the lower ranking lineages, by assigning them subordinate functions in the bureaucratic hierarchy.

We have seen that the highest ranking groups had access to particularly rich prestige goods, such as glass and lacquer. Control over long distance exchange contacts and specialized production must be sustained by these elite groups. Sumptuary regulations are an expression of this control, limiting access to and display of these items to a restricted group. The regulations and the hierarchical structure are mutually supporting.

The pattern of social ranks which is expressed in our analysis appears to fit the stage of the "royal tombs" outlined by Childe (1945:18), "when the kinship organization appropriate to barbarism was breaking down to make room for a territorial state." Childe (1945:18) says that this transformation occurs either through mainly internal economic forces such as the expansion of foreign trade or by contact with higher civilizations. With the appearance of objects such as Roman glass, and the advent of Buddhism in Silla, we seen to have both processes at work in the 5th and 6th centuries A. D. Later burial systems are less conspicuous and sumptuous, when the power of the territorial state has been consolidated.

We can speculate that within the central area of Kyongju, there may have been wards or sectors, belonging to each of the conical clans now resident in the center (Friedman and Rowlands 1977:235). Although our spatial analyses suggested only a few trends in the spatial distribution of tombs of different clusters, on the basis of this preliminary evidence we predict that the central area of Kyongju contains sectors housing the residences of clan chiefs, aristocrats, and clan members, along with the religious structures devoted to clan deities and ancestors.

If Cluster 5 represents the kings of the Kim clan, it may be possible to begin narrowing down the range of possibilities of their actual historical identity, using relative stylistic chronology and the lists of Silla kings. It may also be interesting to examine the interrelationships of various sets of artifacts, such as head gear, ornaments, saddle gear, and weapons. Our analysis has not been designed to do this. It would also be possible to compare outlying elite graves, from small states confederated with Silla and later absorbed, to see how the material culture of local rulers articulated with the material culture and society of the capital.

Our study shows close similarities in groupings of burials according to criteria of energy expenditure, such as mound size; general values of precious objects, such as gold, silver, bronze, and iron; and type of regalia, such as crowns and girdles. Cross-cultural criteria, eschewed by symbolic archaeologists, appear to be useful in analyzing burials in the Korean case. Perhaps they are applicable at some stages of human history, such as state emergence, where claims to power and legitimacy must be communicated not only to groups within one community, but also to surrounding groups with different symbolic systems.
