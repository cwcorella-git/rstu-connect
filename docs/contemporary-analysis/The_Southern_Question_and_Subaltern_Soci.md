## **The Southern Question and Subaltern Social Groups and Classes**

## **Jordan T. Camp**

*This essay reflects on Antonio Gramsci's* Subaltern Social Groups: A Critical Edition of Prison Notebook 25 *(Columbia University Press, 2021), edited and translated by the late Joseph A. Buttigieg and Marcus E. Green. While Gramsci's concept of subaltern groups has long inspired scholars of cultural studies, geography, subaltern studies, and political theory, this work has drawn on a relatively small selection of Gramsci's prison writings. This first complete English translation of Gramsci's writings on subaltern groups and classes represents a major turning point in Gramscian studies, showing how Gramsci provided important "methodological criteria" for analyzing race and class in specific conjunctures. Gramsci's insights about subaltern groups and classes as well as the southern question can be "translated" to confront the current conjuncture.*

**Key Words**: Antonio Gramsci, Political Theory, Subaltern Groups and Classes, Translation

In late 1934 and early 1935 Italian Marxist theorist Antonio Gramsci developed new notes examining the history of subaltern groups and classes. He did so in poor health and under twenty-four-hour police surveillance while confined to the Cusumano clinic in Formia. This new notebook was distinct from others he had written while incarcerated in a fascist prison in Turi di Bari, where he had been confined between 1929 and 1933. As indicated on its first page, this notebook had been printed in "Ditta Cugiini Rossi—Rome" (Gramsci 2021, 1). This special notebook, alongside all his writings that make up the prison notebooks, explores a period marked by the rise of fascism and outlines a method with which to comprehend it. Gramsci was interested in how the defeat of the working-class movement was related to its failure to win the allegiances of the peasants of the south (Rudé 1980, 9), who were depicted by members of the social elite in the north as "barbaric" and "pathological." Gramsci titled this notebook, "On the Margins of History: The History of Subaltern Groups" (Q25, §1; Gramsci 2021, 3).1

Gramsci used this "special" notebook to develop his concept of "subaltern" or subordinate groups and classes. He observes that "often, subaltern groups are originally of a different race (different religion and different culture) than the dominant groups" (Q25, §4; Gramsci 2021, 9). He uses the notebook to theorize the struggles of slaves, peasants, common people, and the proletariat, as well as the articulation between race and class, religion, and "common sense." In addition, he notes how women's history is "similar to the question of the subaltern groups," and that "'masculinism' can be compared to class domination." In doing so, he elaborates a central "research criterion" for understanding the elite construction of subaltern groups and classes as pathological (Q25, §4; Gramsci 2021, 9). For Gramsci, a rethinking of the criminalization of subaltern groups and classes was critical to the struggle against fascism.

Gramsci's writings on subaltern social groups have recently been edited and translated by the late Joseph A. Buttigieg and Marcus E. Green \(2021\) and published as *Subaltern Social Groups: A Critical Edition of Prison Notebook 25*. This important volume is the first complete English translation of the entirety of Gramsci's writings on subaltern social groups, work that has long been relevant for scholars of race and class, critical geography, social history, subaltern studies, and political and social theory. It includes the English translation of notebook 25 as well as previously unavailable or partially translated related materials.

The book represents one of the final works of Joseph Buttigieg, one of the most original and generative Gramscian scholars of his generation. He was William R. Kennan Professor of English at the University of Notre Dame, translator and editor of the complete edition of Gramsci's *Prison Notebooks* published by Columbia University Press, and also a founding member and president of the International Gramsci Society (Gramsci 1992, 1996, 1997). Throughout his distinguished career, Buttigieg (2017\) illustrated Gramsci's relevance for understanding the challenges of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries. As he argued, Gramsci is a "nondogmatic, alternative democratic thinker for our times."

*Subaltern Social Groups* features an introduction by co-editor Marcus E. Green, the political theorist, former editor of *Rethinking Marxism*, and International Gramsci Society secretary, who had long worked with Buttigieg and labored to complete the volume in the wake of his passing. Green's introduction carefully demonstrates the centrality of the concept of subaltern groups to Gramsci's analysis of race and class, religion, the southern question, and common sense or contradictory conceptions of the world. It shows how Gramsci conceived subaltern groups as a "category of analysis" to understand material conditions and politics. In doing so, it provides readers with a distinct assessment of his political-theoretical insights about subaltern groups and classes (Green 2021, xxxvii).

While Gramsci wrote extensively about subaltern groups, Green \(2021 xxii) observes, "There has been relatively little critical engagement with his integral texts, contributing to distortions." These distortions include the idea that Gramsci did not write about race and racism directly. Conversely, the publication of *Subaltern* *Social Groups* demonstrates that Gramsci wrote very clearly about the articulation of race and class. In turn, this newly translated work shows how he developed a distinct method for understanding how race and class shaped the consciousness and autonomous political mobilizations of subaltern social groups and classes. In doing so, it demonstrates how Gramsci provided important "methodological criteria" for analyzing race and class in specific historical *and* geographical conjunctures (Q25, §2; Gramsci 2021, 6; Hart 2002, 27).

In this notebook, Gramsci argues that these methodological criteria could be understood with precision through a concrete analysis of growing popular concerns over race and "criminality" in Italy during the early twentieth century (Q25, §8; Gramsci 2021, 14). He explains how presiding notions of criminality had been shaped by University of Turin criminologist Cesare Lombroso, who had depicted southern Italy as an "inferior civilization," one that was marked by a "criminality of blood." These theories circulated in scholarship as well as in *Avantil!*, the Italian Socialist Party publication. Lombroso influenced the emergent Italian school of criminology, whose key followers were socialists. These criminologists, as Peter D'Agostino \(2002, 323, 320) notes, "linked racial inferiority to southern Italian culture." Their racist theories purported to distinguish a "race" of criminals; notions that were influential in American criminology as well. Furthermore, as Avery Gordon \(2011) demonstrates, extensive experiments utilizing Lombroso's theories were conducted on prisoners in the United States.

Lombroso's racist constructions of the Italian South were an important reference for Gramsci in his theorization of the "southern question" (D'Agostino 2002, 323; Gramsci 2000\). Gramsci had long been aware of the conceits in the field of criminology, having studied philology at the University of Turin, where Lombroso had been a member of the faculty. Gramsci's attention to popular concerns over race and criminality and the "southern question" developed alongside the rise of fascism as a mass movement and state form. In 1926, Gramsci penned his important unfinished essay, "Some Aspects of the Southern Question." It argues that bourgeois ideology had depicted the peasantry of the Italian South as "biologically inferior," that their situation was not the result of capitalist development, but rather because they were "lazy," "criminal," and "barbaric." At the same time, it showed how these racist constructions justified the uneven development of capitalism in southern and northern Italy, as well as imperialism in Africa. In doing so, it argues for the necessity of building worker and peasant alliances in the struggle against racism, capitalism, and Italian fascism (Gramsci 2000, 173; Kipfer and Mallik 2022; Heatherton 2022, 94–5).

### **Historical-Geographical Context**

While incarcerated in a fascist prison, Gramsci elaborated the thesis he developed in "the Southern Question" from a more "disinterested view," as he described it. His writing on the "southern question" represented a shift from his journalism to his more focused conjunctural analysis in the *Prison Notebooks* (Gramsci 1994a, 83; Buttigieg 1992, 21–2; Green 2021, xxxi). There he deployed the method of conjunctural analysis in order to theorize fascism as a solution to a crisis of hegemony that rocked Italy after World War I (Said 1994; Antonini 2021, 150).

In notebook 4 included in *Subaltern Social Groups*, Gramsci elaborates his "methodological criteria" for conjunctural analysis (Q4, §38; Gramsci 2021, 36–47). For Gramsci, the study of decisive conjunctural moments enables one to analyze the connections between shifts in the economy and political and ideological processes. Conjunctural analysis involves an examination of the "relations of force" at "various levels," which include the economic, political, and military forces within a society. The "relation of military forces" is key (Q4, §38; Gramsci 2021, 40). It includes both a technical military dimension, and what Gramsci terms the "politicomilitary," which he defines as "a form of political action that has the virtue of provoking repercussions of a military character" (Q4, §38; Gramsci 2021, 40). The politico-military is a crucial dimension for comprehending the distinct political form of fascism in his time, and its resurgence in our own (Camp and Greenburg 2020; Camp and Heatherton 2023\).

In elaborating this research criterion, Gramsci warned about fascism's "popular appeal" (Hart 2013, 303). His conjunctural analysis revealed that certain elements of this political situation were relatively permanent, such as the role of the military and police in crushing opposition. Yet he noted that other factors fluctuate rapidly, such as the popular consent to the regime. In this way, Gramsci argued that the forces of opposition should consider the hegemonic processes that shape the common sense and political organization among subaltern groups and classes, as well as the long-term structural forces that shape what is possible in a specific conjuncture (Q4, §38; Gramsci 2021, 37).

Gramsci's conjunctural analysis enabled him to render vivid the "*specific case of political crisis*" or "*crisis of hegemony*" that led Italy to become one of the paradigmatic fascist state forms in the twentieth century (Poulantzas 1974, 60). In notebook 25, Gramsci suggests how popular narratives about race and criminality legitimated the formation of this particular state form. He was concerned with how the modern state replaced the "mechanical bloc" of social groups with the production of consent to the "hegemony of the ruling and dominant group." In turn, Gramsci suggests how this state form "abolishes certain autonomies" in the process. Significantly, he also shows these autonomous struggles "will be reborn in other forms, as parties, trade unions, cultural associations" (Q25, §4; Gramsci 2021, 9). He concludes by suggesting that "the dictatorships of our time abolish these new forms of autonomy" and work to "incorporate them into the activity of the state" as the "dominant class becomes totalitarian" (Q25, §4; Gramsci 2021, 9–10). In this way, Gramsci demonstrates how the hegemony of the ruling class was linked to the expansion of policing and surveillance to contain opposition (Antonini 2021, 169).

In his observations about the surveillance of subaltern social groups, Gramsci reworks insights from his notes on the state and civil society showing that Italian fascism was "more a police than a military system" (Q13, §27; Gramsci 1971, 222). He understood the political purpose of the police beyond simply ensuring "the repression of crime." He theorized the police as an expression of the "totality of forces organized by the State and by private individuals to safeguard the political and economic domination of the ruling class" (Q13, §27; Gramsci 1971, 221). His conception of fascism as a police system was critical to the development of his theorization of the state. In doing so, he showed how the fascist regime sought to resolve an "organic crisis" of hegemony through the expansion of police, military, and bureaucratic control over "civil society" (Antonini 2021, 166).

This conception of the state was a decisive intervention in a conjuncture where most political and social theorists defined the state as a "political society," or simply a kind of coercive apparatus. In place of these theories, Gramsci offered an alternative theory of the "integral state," which he defined as a "balance" between "political" and "civil" society. This work revealed how political theories of the state as ideal types fell short in theorizing the precise form the state took in fascist Italy. As Gramsci (1994b, 67) observed, they specifically neglected the ways in which fascism combined a bureaucratic regime of military, prisons, and police with allegedly "private" organizations in civil society such as churches, schools, and unions. As Gramsci argued, the "full significance" of his theory of subalternity and the integral state can only be grasped through concrete analyses of particular conjunctures (Q4, §38; Gramsci 2021, 37, 40; Green 2021, xlvi; Thomas 2018, 3, 9–11).2

New work in Gramscian studies has drawn attention to the significance of the Italian philological scholarship for interpreting Gramsci's texts as well the politics of the period. This scholarship provides the historical-geographical context required to understand Gramsci's political thought in his time, and our own (Ives 2004; Brennan 2006; Thomas 2009; Loftus 2019)*.* As Buttigieg has pointed out in his important 1994 essay on "Philology and Politics," Gramsci's attention to the self-organization of the subaltern classes represented a distinct alternative to most "communist political theories and practices." Gramsci was especially concerned about the dogmatic reduction of Marxist theory in the wake of the Stalinist turn in the Communist Party in the mid-1920s. According to Buttigieg, Gramsci thought that it would lead to "catastrophes, causing great damage that can never be repaired." In Gramsci's view, this dogmatism would lead to a "rift" between socialist intellectuals and leaders and the masses of people. As an alternative, Gramsci argued for the development of a "collective organism," one that joined principled political-theoretical work with the self-organized struggles of

subaltern groups and classes through a "living philology" (Buttigieg 1994, 122, 136, 137).

"The continuing significance of Gramsci for our time," Buttigieg argues, "can only be made manifest by those meticulous philologists," who can help readers understand how he transformed defeat into new ways of theorizing politics (Buttigieg 1994, 138). Inspired by these insights, Gramscian scholars have emphasized the philological method required to understand Gramsci's theory in the post– World War I conjuncture, suggesting a need to "translate" his insights to understand the specificities of our own much different conjuncture (Kipfer 2002; Kipfer and Hart 2013; Hart 2020; Kipfer and Mallick 2022; Camp 2022). Taking up this challenge, I want to conclude by suggesting that his insights about the history of subaltern groups and classes can be translated to confront the specific problems of the current conjuncture. Gramsci provides criteria for analyzing the historically and geographically specific relationship between the resurgence of neo-fascist political organizations and their "anti-immigrant common sense" and a crisis of U. S. hegemony and for global capitalism (Broder 2023; Silver and Payne 2020).

### **The Current Conjuncture**

In the current conjuncture, "neofascist common sense" combines antipathy to "globalist elites" and the "deep state" with dedication to nationalism, law and order, and the criminalization of subaltern groups and classes including migrants, the unemployed, the homeless, and LGBTQI communities, which are, of course, not mutually exclusive categories.3 This neofascist common sense has been critical to shoring up consent to an authoritarian program promoted by figures on the far right such as Giorgia Meloni in Italy, Marine Le Pen in France, Viktor Orbán in Hungary, Donald Trump in the United States, and beyond. Within an extremely nationalist and exclusionary framework, their putative opposition to "globalism" has attracted popular support (Broder 2023, 5, 13, 14).

While it may be tempting to interpret this neo-fascist common sense as part of an unbroken chain of racist nationalism under capitalism, we gain a deeper understanding through a conjunctural analysis. Gramsci's interventions derived their explanatory power from a specific conjuncture, but their implications are not fixed to his time and place (Camp and Heatherton 2023). His methods developed in fascist Italy during the 1920s and 1930s remain relevant in places as diverse as Brazil, India, Italy, the United States, and South Africa in the 2020s. They suggest the political stakes in moving between philological readings of Gramsci and translating his insights about the history of subaltern groups and classes to meet the distinct challenges of the current conjuncture (Hart 2024\). They illustrate that authoritarian resolutions are never inevitable (Denning 2021; Kipfer and Hart

2013), but are rather the product of political struggles where, as Gramsci put it, "various outcomes are possible" (Q4, §38; Gramsci 2021, 41).

It is therefore important to remember Gramsci's "open-ended" conception of politics. He demonstrates that politics is the product of the "relations of forces" operating in a specific historical moment and geographical context (Hall 1988, 169). In this way, the challenge is to remain committed to Gramsci's method, which provides a "shield against all forms of dogmatism" (Buttigieg 1990, 81). This is particularly important in the present, when subaltern groups and classes are recruited for different political projects with antagonistic interests. As Gramsci warned, subaltern groups are "always subject to the initiatives of dominant groups even when they rebel and rise up; only 'permanent' victory breaks their subordination, but not immediately" (Q25, §2; Gramsci 2021, 6).

It is our great fortune that scholars can now engage with *Subaltern Social Groups* in translating Gramsci's understandings in the present. In the wake of the COVID-19 pandemic, the worst economic crisis since the 1930s, and the resurgence of the far right, it illustrates how to analyze the ways common sense might shape the self-organized struggles of subaltern classes. It also suggests the failure to investigate these dynamics will have deleterious consequences. As Gramsci warned, economic crises produce not only spontaneous protests among subaltern classes, but also "conspiracies among the reactionary groups" who seize the moment to "*attempt coups d'état*," as was clear in the 6 January 2021 siege on the United States capitol that was self-consciously replicated in the Brazilian capitol on 8 January 2023 (Q3, §48; Gramsci 1971, 199). This situation suggests how fascist nationalisms "mediate" crises of capitalism in different places and, in doing so, produce "bizarre combinations" (Kipfer and Hart 2013, 337; Q3, §48; Gramsci 1971, 200). In this context, as Gramsci put it, "every trace of autonomous initiative by subaltern groups, then, should be of inestimable value to the integral historian" (Q25, §2; Gramsci 2021, 7).
