**Pennsylvania**, [\[b\]](#page-69-2) officially the **Commonwealth of**

**Pennsylvania**, [\[c\]](#page-69-3) is a [state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U. S._state) spanning the [Mid-Atlantic,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid-Atlantic_(United_States)) [Northeastern,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northeastern_United_States) [Appalachian,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appalachia) and [Great Lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Lakes_region) regions of the [United States.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States) It borders [Delaware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delaware) to its southeast, [Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryland) to its south, [West Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Virginia) to its southwest, [Ohio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohio) and the [Ohio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohio_River) [River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohio_River) to its west, [Lake Erie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Erie) and [New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_(state)) to its north, the [Delaware River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delaware_River) and [New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey) to its east, and the [Canadian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provinces_and_territories_of_Canada) [province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provinces_and_territories_of_Canada) of [Ontario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontario) to its northwest via [Lake Erie.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Erie) Pennsylvania's most populous city is [Philadelphia.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia)

Pennsylvania was founded in 1681 through a royal [land grant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_grant) to [William Penn,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Penn) the son of [the state's namesake.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Penn_(Royal_Navy_officer)) Before that, between 1638 and 1655, a southeast portion of the state was part of [New Sweden,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Sweden) a [Swedish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Empire) colony. Established as a haven for religious and political tolerance, the [colonial-era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_colonization_of_the_Americas) [Province of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Pennsylvania) [Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Pennsylvania) was known for its relatively peaceful relations with native tribes, [innovative government system,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_commission) and [religious pluralism.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_tolerance)

Pennsylvania later played a vital and historic role in the [American Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolution) and the ultimately successful quest for independence from the [British Empire,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire) hosting the [First](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Continental_Congress) and [Second Continental Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Continental_Congress) in Philadelphia, which formed the [Continental Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_Army) and elected [George Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington) as its commander in 1775 during the [American Revolutionary War,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War) and unanimously adopted the [Declaration of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Declaration_of_Independence) the following year[.\[8\]](#page-70-4) In 1787, following the establishment of the nation's independence, the [Constitution of the United](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_the_United_States) [States,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_the_United_States) now the world's oldest and longest-standing written and codified national constitution, was written at the [Constitutional Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_Convention_(United_States)) in Philadelphia, and was ratified in Philadelphia the following year. On December 12, 1787, Pennsylvania was the second state to ratify the U. S. Constitution[.\[9\]](#page-70-5)

In July 1863, during the [American Civil War,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War) the [Battle of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Gettysburg) [Gettysburg,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Gettysburg) fought over three days in and around [Gettysburg,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gettysburg,_Pennsylvania) was the bloodiest battle of both the Civil War and of any battle in American military history, with over 50,000 [Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_army) and [Confederate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederate_States_Army) casualties. But the success of the Union army at Gettysburg in repelling Confederate general [Robert E. Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_E._Lee)'s attempt to invade the North also proved the [Civil War's turning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turning_point_of_the_American_Civil_War)

[point,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turning_point_of_the_American_Civil_War) leading to the [Union's preservation.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conclusion_of_the_American_Civil_War) Recognizing the Union army's extraordinary sacrifice at Gettysburg, U. S. president [Abraham Lincoln](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Lincoln) visited the [Gettysburg Battlefield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gettysburg_Battlefield) later that year, where he delivered his historic 271-word [Gettysburg Address,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gettysburg_Address) widely considered one the most famous speeches in [American history.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_United_States)

In the late 19th and 20th centuries, Pennsylvania's manufacturing-based economy blossomed, contributing to the development of much of the nation's early infrastructure, including key bridges, [skyscrapers,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skyscraper) and much of the military hardware that allowed the U. S. to prevail in [World War I,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I) [World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) [War II,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) and the [Cold War.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War) In the late 1970s and early 1980s, however, the nation's heavy manufacturing base struggled as the two largest [steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel) manufacturers for most of the 20th century, both based in Pennsylvania, [U. S. Steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U. S._Steel) in [Pittsburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh) and [Bethlehem Steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bethlehem_Steel) in [Bethlehem,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bethlehem,_Pennsylvania) confronted unprecedented challenges from foreign manufacturers and other threats to their historic industry leadership; Bethlehem Steel subsequently ceased operations in 1982 and was liquidated in 2003.

[Pennsylvania's geography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_Pennsylvania) is highly diverse. The [Appalachian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appalachian_Mountains) [Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appalachian_Mountains) run through the center of the state, the [Allegheny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allegheny_Mountains) and [Pocono](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pocono_Mountains) mountains span much of [Northeastern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northeastern_Pennsylvania) [Pennsylvania,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northeastern_Pennsylvania) and close to [60% of the state is forested.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_cover_by_state_and_territory_in_the_United_States) Although it has no ocean shoreline, it has 140 miles (225 km) of waterfront along Lake Erie and the tidal Delaware River.[\[10\]](#page-70-6) Pennsylvania is the [fifth-most populous state in the United](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_U. S._states_and_territories_by_population) [States,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_U. S._states_and_territories_by_population) with over 13 million residents as of the [2020 United](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_United_States_census)

[States census,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_United_States_census) its highest decennial census count ever[.\[3\]](#page-70-0) The state is the [33rd-largest by area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_U. S._states_and_territories_by_area) and has the [ninth-highest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_states_and_territories_of_the_United_States_by_population_density) [population density among all states.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_states_and_territories_of_the_United_States_by_population_density) The largest [metropolitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_statistical_area) [statistical area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_statistical_area) is the southeastern [Delaware Valley,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delaware_Valley) including and surrounding Philadelphia, the [state's largest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_cities_in_Pennsylvania) and [nation's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_United_States_cities_by_population) [sixth-most populous city.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_United_States_cities_by_population) The second-largest metropolitan area, [Greater Pittsburgh,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Pittsburgh) is centered in and around Pittsburgh, the state's second-largest city. The state's subsequent five most populous cities are [Allentown,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allentown,_Pennsylvania) [Reading,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reading,_Pennsylvania) [Erie,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erie,_Pennsylvania) [Scranton,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scranton,_Pennsylvania) and Bethlehem[.\[11\]](#page-70-7)

### **Indigenous settlement**

### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pennsylvania&action=edit§ion=2)

Pennsylvania's history of human habitation extends thousands of years before the foundation of the colonial [Province of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Pennsylvania) [Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Pennsylvania) in 1681. [Archaeologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeology) believe the first [settlement of the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peopling_of_the_Americas) occurred at least 15,000 years ago, during the [Last Glacial Period,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_Glacial_Period) though it is unclear when humans first inhabited present-day Pennsylvania. Between 10,000 and 16,000 years ago, [Native Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_Americans_in_the_United_States) crossed the two continents, arriving in [North America.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_America) [\[12\]](#page-70-8) [Meadowcroft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meadowcroft_Rockshelter) [Rockshelter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meadowcroft_Rockshelter) in [Jefferson Township](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jefferson_Township,_Washington_County,_Pennsylvania) includes the earliest known signs of human activity in Pennsylvania and perhaps all of North America[,\[13\]](#page-71-0) including the remains of a civilization that existed over 10,000 years ago and possibly pre-dated the [Clovis culture.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clovis_culture) [\[14\]](#page-71-1)[\[13\]](#page-71-0) By 1000 AD, in contrast to their nomadic [hunter-gatherer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunter-gatherer) [ancestors,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleo-Indians) the native population of Pennsylvania had developed [agricultural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture) techniques and a mixed food economy[.\[15\]](#page-71-2)

By the time [European colonization of the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_colonization_of_the_Americas) began, at least two major Native American tribes inhabited Pennsylvania. [\[14\]](#page-71-1) The first, the [Lenape,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lenape) spoke an [Algonquian language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algonquian_languages) and inhabited the eastern region of the state, then known as [Lenapehoking.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lenapehoking) It included most of present-day [New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey) and the [Lehigh Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lehigh_Valley) and [Delaware Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delaware_Valley) regions in eastern and southeastern Pennsylvania. The Lenape's territory ended somewhere between the [Delaware River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delaware_River) in the east and the [Susquehanna River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susquehanna_River) in central Pennsylvania. The second tribe, the [Susquehannock,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susquehannock) spoke an [Iroquoian language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iroquoian_language) and were based in [Western Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Pennsylvania) from [New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_(state)) state in the north to [West Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Virginia) in the southwest, including the Susquehanna, [Allegheny,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allegheny_River) and [Monongahela](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monongahela_River) rivers near present-day [Pittsburgh.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh) [\[16\]](#page-71-3) European disease and constant warfare with several neighboring tribes and groups of Europeans weakened these two tribes, and they were grossly outpaced financially as the [Hurons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wyandot_people) and [Iroquois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iroquois) blocked them from proceeding west into [Ohio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohio) during the [Beaver Wars.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaver_Wars) As they lost numbers and land, the Hurons abandoned much of their western territory and moved closer to the Susquehanna River, and the Iroquois and Mohawk tribes moved further north. Northwest of the Allegheny River was the Iroquoian [Petun,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petun) [\[17\]](#page-71-4)[\[18\]](#page-71-5) who were fragmented into three groups during the Beaver Wars: the Petun of New York, the Wyandot of Ohio, and the Tiontatecaga of the Kanawha River in southern West Virginia. South of the Allegheny River was a nation known as Calicua[,\[19\]](#page-71-6) which may have been part of the [Monongahela](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monongahela_culture)

### [culture.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monongahela_culture)

## ![](_page_11_Picture_3.jpeg)

[William Penn,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Penn) a [Quaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quakers) and son of a [prominent admiral,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Penn_(Royal_Navy_officer)) founded the colonial [Province of Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Pennsylvania) in 1681.

In the 17th century, the [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Netherland) and the [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia_Company) each claimed both sides of the [Delaware River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delaware_River) as part of their colonial lands in America[.\[20\]](#page-71-7)[\[21\]](#page-72-0)[\[22\]](#page-72-1) The Dutch were the first to take possession[.\[22\]](#page-72-1) By June 3, 1631, the Dutch began settling the [Delmarva Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delmarva_Peninsula) by establishing the [Zwaanendael Colony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zwaanendael_Colony) on the site of present-day [Lewes, Delaware.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewes,_Delaware) [\[23\]](#page-72-2) In 1638, Sweden established the [New Sweden Colony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Sweden) in the region of [Fort Christina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Christina) on the site of present-day [Wilmington, Delaware.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilmington,_Delaware) New Sweden claimed and, for the most part, controlled the lower Delaware River region, including parts of present-day Delaware, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania, but settled few colonists there[.\[24\]](#page-72-3)[\[25\]](#page-72-4)

On March 12, 1664, [King Charles II of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_II_of_England) gave [James,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_II_of_England) [Duke of York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_II_of_England) a grant that incorporated all lands included in the original Virginia Company of Plymouth Grant and other lands. This grant was in conflict with the Dutch claim for [New](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Netherland)

[Netherland,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Netherland) which included parts of today's Pennsylvania.[\[26\]](#page-72-5)

On June 24, 1664, the Duke of York sold the portion of his large grant that included present-day [New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey) to [John Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Berkeley,_1st_Baron_Berkeley_of_Stratton) and [George Carteret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Carteret) for a proprietary colony. The land was not yet in British possession, but the sale boxed in the portion of New Netherland on the West side of the Delaware River. The British conquest of New Netherland began on August 29, 1664, when [New Amsterdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Amsterdam) was coerced to surrender while facing cannons on British ships in [New York Harbor.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Harbor) [\[27\]](#page-72-6)[\[28\]](#page-72-7) The conquest was completed in October 1664, when the British captured [Fort Casimir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Casimir) in what today is [New Castle, Delaware.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Castle,_Delaware) The [Treaty of Breda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Breda_(1667)) between England, France, and the Netherlands confirmed the English conquest on July 21, 1667, [\[29\]](#page-72-8)[\[30\]](#page-73-0) although there were temporary reversions.

On September 12, 1672, during the [Third Anglo-Dutch War,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Anglo-Dutch_War) the Dutch reconquered [New York Colony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Colony)[/New Amsterdam,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Amsterdam) establishing three County Courts, which went on to become original Counties in present-day [Delaware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delaware) and Pennsylvania. The one that later transferred to Pennsylvania was Upland.[\[31\]](#page-73-1) This was partially reversed on February 9, 1674, when the [Treaty of Westminster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Westminster_(1674)) ended the Third Anglo-Dutch War and reverted all political situations to the status quo ante bellum. The British retained the Dutch Counties with their Dutch names[.\[32\]](#page-73-2) By June 11, 1674, New York reasserted control over the outlying colonies, including Upland, and the names started to be changed to British names by November 11, 1674[.\[33\]](#page-73-3) Upland was partitioned on November 12, 1674, producing the general outline of the current border between Pennsylvania and Delaware[.\[34\]](#page-73-4)

On February 28, 1681, Charles II granted a land charter[\[35\]](#page-74-0) to [Quaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quakers) leader William Penn to repay a debt of £16,00[0\[36\]](#page-74-1) (around £2,100,000 in 2008, adjusting for retail inflation)[\[37\]](#page-74-2) owed to William's father. The transaction represents one of the largest land grants to an individual in history.[\[38\]](#page-74-3)[\[39\]](#page-74-4) Penn proposed that the land be called New Wales, but there were objections to that name, so he recommended Sylvania (from the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) silva: "forest, woods"). The King named it Pennsylvania (literally "Penn's Woods") in honor of Admiral Penn. The younger Penn was embarrassed at this name, fearing that people would think he had named it after himself, but King Charles would not rename the grant[.\[40\]](#page-74-5) Penn established a government with two innovations, the [county](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_commission) [commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_commission) and [freedom of religion,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_religion) which were subsequently replicated in many of the [Thirteen Colonies.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirteen_Colonies) [\[39\]](#page-74-4)

What had been Upland on the Pennsylvania side of the Pennsylvania-Delaware border was renamed [Chester County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chester_County,_Pennsylvania) when Pennsylvania instituted its colonial governments on March 4, 1681[.\[41\]](#page-75-0)[\[42\]](#page-75-1) Penn signed a peace treaty with [Tamanend,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamanend) leader of the Lenape, which began a long period of friendly relations between the Quakers and the Indians.[\[43\]](#page-75-2) Additional treaties between Quakers and other tribes followed. Between 1730 and when the Pennsylvania Colony was shut down by [Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_of_Great_Britain) with the [Currency Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency_Act) in 1764, the Pennsylvania Colony made its own paper money to account for the shortage of actual gold and silver. The paper money was called [Colonial Scrip.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonial_Scrip)

The Colony issued bills of credit, which were as good as gold or silver coins because of their legal tender status. Since they were issued by the government and not a banking institution, they were interest-free, largely defraying the expense of the government and therefore taxation of the people. It also promoted general employment and prosperity, since the government used discretion and did not issue excessive amounts that inflated the currency. [Benjamin Franklin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Franklin) had a hand in creating this currency, whose utility, he said, was never to be disputed. The currency also met with "cautious approval" by [Adam Smith.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Smith) [\[48\]](#page-75-7)

Pennsylvania's diverse topography produces a variety of climates, though the entire state experiences cool to cold winters and very warm, humid summers. Straddling two major zones, much of the state has a [humid continental climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humid_continental_climate) [\(Köppen climate classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification) Dfa or Dfb). The southern portion of the state has a [humid subtropical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humid_subtropical) climate. The largest city, [Philadelphia,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia) has a [humid subtropical climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humid_subtropical_climate) (Köppen Cfa).

Summers are generally hot and humid. Moving toward the mountainous interior of the state, the winter climate becomes colder, the number of cloudy days increases, and snowfall amounts are greater. Western areas of the state, particularly locations near [Lake Erie,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Erie) can receive over 100 inches (250 cm) of snowfall annually, and the entire state receives plentiful precipitation throughout the year. The state may be subject to severe weather from spring through summer into autumn. Tornadoes occur annually in the state, sometimes in large numbers, such as 30 recorded tornadoes in 2011; violent tornadoes, however, are rarer than they are in states further west[.\[87\]](#page-79-10)

## Ethnic origins of Pennsylvanians

The vast majority of Pennsylvania's population is made up of whites, blacks and Hispanics, with the latter two being minorities and having significant populations. Non-Hispanic Whites make up the majority of Pennsylvania; they are mostly descended from German, Irish, Scottish, Welsh, Italian, and English immigrants. Rural portions of South Central Pennsylvania are recognized nationally for their [Amish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amish#Population_and_distribution) [communities.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amish#Population_and_distribution) [Wyoming Valley,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wyoming_Valley) including [Scranton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scranton,_Pennsylvania) and [Wilkes-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilkes-Barre,_Pennsylvania)[Barre,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilkes-Barre,_Pennsylvania) has the highest percentage of white residents of any metropolitan area with a population of 500,000 or above in the U. S.; in Wyoming Valley, 96.2% of the population claim to be white with no Hispanic background. The state's Hispanic or Latino American population grew by 82.6% between 2000 and 2010, marking one of the largest increases in a state's Hispanic population. The significant growth of the Hispanic or Latino population is due to migration to the state mainly from [Puerto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puerto_Rico) [Rico,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puerto_Rico) a U. S. territory, and to a lesser extent immigration from countries such as the [Dominican Republic,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominican_Republic) [Mexico,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico) and various [Central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_America) and [South American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_America) nations and a wave of Hispanic and Latinos leaving [New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City) and [New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey) for safer and more affordable living.[[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)]

The majority of Hispanic or Latino Americans in Pennsylvania are of [Puerto Rican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puerto_Rican_American) descent[.\[104\]](#page-81-5)[\[105\]](#page-81-6) Most of the remaining Hispanic or Latino population is made up of [Mexicans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_American) and [Dominicans,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominican_American) and the majority of Hispanics and Latinos are concentrated in Philadelphia, the [Lehigh Valley,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lehigh_Valley) and South Central Pennsylvania[.\[106\]](#page-81-7) The Hispanic or Latino population is greatest in [Bethlehem,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bethlehem,_Pennsylvania) [Allentown,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allentown,_Pennsylvania) [Reading,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reading,_Pennsylvania) [Lancaster,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lancaster,_Pennsylvania) [York,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/York,_Pennsylvania) and around Philadelphia. As of 2010, the vast majority of Hispanics and Latino Americans in Pennsylvania, about 85%, live within a 150-mile (240 km) radius of Philadelphia, and about 20% live in the city itself.

The Asian population swelled by almost 60%, mostly Indian, Vietnamese, and Chinese immigrants, and many Asians moving to [Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia) from New York City. The rapid growth of this community has given Pennsylvania one of the largest Asian populations in the nation. The African American population grew by 13%, which was the largest increase in that population among the state's peers of [New York,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_(state)) [New Jersey,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey) [Ohio,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohio) [Illinois,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illinois) and [Michigan.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan) [\[107\]](#page-81-8) Pennsylvania has a high inmigration of black and Hispanic people from other nearby states with the eastern and south-central portions of the state seeing the bulk of the increases[.\[108\]](#page-81-9)[\[109\]](#page-82-0)

Among the state's black population, the vast majority in the state are African American. There are also a growing number of black residents of [West Indian,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Indian_American) [recent African,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_immigration_to_the_United_States) and [Hispanic or](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Hispanic) [Latino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Hispanic) origins[.\[110\]](#page-82-1) Most Blacks live in the Philadelphia area, Pittsburgh, or South Central Pennsylvania.[[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)] Additionally, in 2020, 31,052 identified as being Native American alone, and 158,112 did in combination with one or more other races[.\[111\]](#page-82-2)

Pennsylvania is divided into 67 [counties.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_(United_States)) [\[167\]](#page-87-7) Counties are further subdivided into municipalities that are either incorporated as cities, [boroughs,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borough_(Pennsylvania)) or [townships.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Township_(Pennsylvania)) [\[168\]](#page-87-8) The most populous county in Pennsylvania and [24th-most populous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_the_most_populous_counties_in_the_United_States) [county](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_the_most_populous_counties_in_the_United_States) in the United States is [Philadelphia County,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia_County,_Pennsylvania) which includes the city of [Philadelphia,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia) with a 2020 population of 1,603,797; the state's least populous county is [Cameron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cameron_County,_Pennsylvania) with a population of 4,547[.\[108\]](#page-81-9)

There are a total of 56 cities in Pennsylvania, which are classified by population as either first-class, second-class, or third-class cities[.\[167\]](#page-87-7)[\[169\]](#page-87-9) Philadelphia, the state's largest city with a population exceeding 1.6 million, is Pennsylvania's only first-class city[.\[168\]](#page-87-8) [Pittsburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh) (303,000) and [Scranton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scranton,_Pennsylvania) (76,000) are second-class and second-class 'A' cities, respectively.[\[168\]](#page-87-8) All of the state's remaining cities including [Allentown,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allentown,_Pennsylvania) the state's third-largest city, and [Reading,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reading,_Pennsylvania) its fourth-largest, to [Parker,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parker,_Pennsylvania) the state's smallest city with a population of only 820, are designated as third-class cities[.\[170\]](#page-87-10) First- and second-class cities are governed by a "strong mayor" form of [mayor–council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor%E2%80%93council_government) [government,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor%E2%80%93council_government) whereas third-class cities are governed by either a "weak mayor" form of government or a [council–manager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council%E2%80%93manager_government) [government.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council%E2%80%93manager_government) [\[168\]](#page-87-8)

Pennsylvania boroughs are generally smaller in population than the state's cities, and most of the state's cities were incorporated as boroughs prior to being designated cities.[\[168\]](#page-87-8) There are 958 boroughs in Pennsylvania, all of which are governed by the "weak mayor" form of mayor-council government[.\[167\]](#page-87-7)[\[168\]](#page-87-8) The largest borough in Pennsylvania is [State College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_College,_Pennsylvania) (40,501) and the smallest is [Centralia.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centralia,_Pennsylvania)

Townships are the third type of municipality in Pennsylvania and are classified as either first-class or second-class townships. There are 1,454 second-class townships and 93 first-class townships[.\[171\]](#page-88-0) Second-class townships can become first-class townships if they have a population density greater than 300 inhabitants per square mile (120/km2) and a [referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Referendum) is passed supporting the change[.\[171\]](#page-88-0) Pennsylvania's largest township is [Upper Darby Township](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Darby_Township,_Delaware_County,_Pennsylvania) (85,681), and the smallest is [East Keating Township.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Keating_Township,_Clinton_County,_Pennsylvania)

There is one exception to the types of municipalities in Pennsylvania: [Bloomsburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloomsburg,_Pennsylvania) was incorporated as a town in 1870 and is, officially, the only town in the state.[\[172\]](#page-88-1) In 1975, [Mc Candless Township](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mc Candless_Township,_Allegheny_County,_Pennsylvania) adopted a home-rule charter under the name of "Town of Mc Candless", but is, legally, still a first-class township[.\[173\]](#page-88-2) The state has 56 cities, 958 boroughs, 93 firstclass townships, 1,454 second-class townships, and one town (Bloomsburg) for a total of 2,562 municipalities.

Pennsylvania had the 15th-highest state and local tax burden in the nation as of 2012, according to the [Tax Foundation.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_Foundation) [\[174\]](#page-88-3) Residents paid a total of \$83.7 billion in state and local taxes with a per capita average of \$4,589 annually. Residents share 76% of the total tax burden. Many state politicians have tried to increase the share of taxes paid by out-of-state sources. Suggested revenue sources include taxing natural gas drilling as Pennsylvania is the only state without such a tax on gas drilling[.\[175\]](#page-88-4) Additional revenue prospects include trying to place tolls on interstate highways; specifically [Interstate 80,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_80_in_Pennsylvania) which is used heavily by out of state commuters with high maintenance costs[.\[176\]](#page-88-5)

[Sales taxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sales_tax) provide 39% of Pennsylvania's state revenue; [personal income taxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_income_tax) 34%; motor vehicle taxes about 12%, and taxes on [cigarettes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cigarette_tax#Taxation) and alcoholic beverages 5%[.\[177\]](#page-88-6) The personal income tax is a flat 3.07%. An individual's taxable income is based on the following eight types of income: compensation (salary); interest; dividends; net profits from the operation of a business, profession or farm; net gains or income from the dispositions of property; net gains or income from rents, royalties, patents and copyrights; income derived through estates or trusts; and gambling and [lottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lottery) winnings (other than [Pennsylvania Lottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvania_Lottery) winnings)[.\[178\]](#page-88-7)

Counties, municipalities, and [school districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School_district) levy taxes on real estate. In addition, some local bodies assess a [wage tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Income_tax) on personal income. Generally, the total wage tax rate is capped at 1% of income but some municipalities with [home rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_rule) charters may charge more than 1%. Thirty-two of Pennsylvania's sixty-seven counties levy a [personal property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Property_tax) [tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Property_tax) on stocks, bonds, and similar holdings. With the exception of the city of [Philadelphia,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia) Pennsylvania, municipalities and school districts are allowed to enact a local earned income tax within the purview of Act 32. Residents of these municipalities and school districts are required to file a local income tax return in addition to federal and state returns. This local return is filed with the local income tax collector, a private collection agency appointed by a particular county to collect the local earned income and local services tax (the latter a flat fee deducted from salaried employees working within a particular municipality or school district)[.\[179\]](#page-88-8)

[Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia) has its own local income taxation system. Philadelphia-based employers are required to withhold the Philadelphia wage tax from the salaries of their employees. Residents of Philadelphia working for an employer are not required to file a local return as long as their Philadelphia wage tax is fully withheld by their employer. If their employer does not withhold the Philadelphia wage tax, residents are required to register with the Revenue Department and file an Earnings Tax return. Residents of Philadelphia with self-employment income are required to file a Net Profits Tax (NPT) return, while those with business income from Philadelphia sources are required to obtain a Commercial Activity License (CAL) and pay the Business Income and Receipts Tax (BIRT) and the NPT. Residents with unearned income except interest from checking and savings accounts are required to file and pay the School Income-tax (SIT)[.\[180\]](#page-88-9)

The complexity of Pennsylvania's local tax filing system has been criticized by experts, who note that the outsourcing of collections to private entities is akin to [tax farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_farming) and that many new residents are caught off guard and end up facing failure to file penalties even if they did not owe any tax. Attempts to transfer local income tax collections to the state level by having a separate local section on the state income tax return, currently the method used to collect local income taxes in [New York,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_(state)) [Maryland,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryland) [Indiana,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiana) and [Iowa,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iowa) have been unsuccessful[.\[181\]](#page-88-10)

Since the latter half of the 20th century, Pennsylvania has been perceived as a powerful [swing state,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swing_state) and winning Pennsylvania has since been deemed as essential to [U. S. presidential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_United_States) [candidates.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_United_States) Only thrice between [1932](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1932_United_States_presidential_election) and [1988](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988_United_States_presidential_election) (1932, 1948, and [1968,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1968_United_States_presidential_election) with [Franklin D. Roosevelt,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt) [Harry S. Truman,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_S._Truman) and [Richard Nixon,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Nixon) respectively) has a presidential candidate been able to win the White House while losing Pennsylvania.

Between 1992 and 2016, Pennsylvania trended [Democratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Party_(United_States)) in presidential elections; [Bill Clinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Clinton) won the state twice by large margins and [Al Gore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Gore) won it by a slightly closer margin in 2000. In the 2004 presidential election, [John F. Kerry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_F._Kerry) beat President [George W. Bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_W._Bush) in Pennsylvania, 2,938,095 (51%) to 2,793,847 (48%). In the 2008 presidential election, Democrat [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama) defeated [Republican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republican_Party_(United_States)) [John Mc Cain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Mc Cain) in Pennsylvania, 3,276,363 (54%) to 2,655,885 (44%).

In the [2016 United States presidential election,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_United_States_presidential_election) however, Republican [Donald Trump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Trump) broke the Democratic streak in the state, winning by 2,970,733 (48%) votes to 2,926,441 (47%) votes[.\[183\]](#page-89-1) The state returned to the Democratic column in [2020](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_United_States_presidential_election_in_Pennsylvania) by voting for [Joe Biden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Biden) over Trump, 3,458,229 (50%) to 3,377,674 (49%). The state holds 19 [electoral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Electoral_College) votes[.\[184\]](#page-89-2)

Despite voting for the Democratic ticket for president in every election between 1992 and 2012, Pennsylvania has a history of electing Republican U. S. senators. From 2009 to 2011, the state was represented by two Democratic senators for the first time since 1947 after Republican Senator [Arlen Specter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arlen_Specter) switched party affiliation. In 2010, Republicans recaptured a U. S. Senate seat and a majority of the state's congressional seats, control of both chambers of the state legislature, and the governorship. Democrats won back the governorship, however, four years later in the [2014 election.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvania_gubernatorial_election,_2014) It was the first time since a governor became eligible for reelection that an incumbent governor had been defeated in a reelection bid.

Historically, Democratic strength was concentrated in

Philadelphia in the southeast, the Pittsburgh, and [Johnstown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnstown,_Pennsylvania) areas in the southwest, and [Scranton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scranton,_Pennsylvania) and [Wilkes-Barre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilkes-Barre,_Pennsylvania) in the northeast. Republican strength was concentrated in the Philadelphia suburbs and the more rural areas in the state's central, northeastern, and western portions, some of which have long been considered among the nation's most conservative areas. Since 1992, however, the Philadelphia suburbs have swung Democratic; the brand of Republicanism there was traditionally moderate. In the 21st century, however, Pittsburgh suburbs, which historically had been Democratic strongholds, have swung more Republican.

Democratic political consultant [James Carville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Carville) once pejoratively described Pennsylvania as "Philadelphia in the east, Pittsburgh in the west, and Alabama in the middle", suggesting that political power in the state was based in its two largest cities, which have been reliably Democratic, offset by the state's large rural power base, which has proven equally reliably Republican. Political analysts and editorials refer to central Pennsylvania as the "T" in statewide elections. The state's three valleys [\(Delaware,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delaware_Valley) [Lehigh,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lehigh_Valley) and [Wyoming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wyoming_Valley) Valleys) and [Greater Pittsburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Pittsburgh) generally vote Democratic, while the majority of the counties in the central part of the state vote Republican. As a result, maps showing the results of statewide elections invariably form a shape that resembles a "T".

Pennsylvania retains the [death penalty,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_penalty) although there is currently a gubernatorial hold on executions[.\[185\]](#page-89-3)

Primary north–south highways are [Interstate 79](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_79_in_Pennsylvania) from its terminus in Erie through [Pittsburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh) to [West Virginia,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Virginia) [Interstate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_81_in_Pennsylvania) [81](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_81_in_Pennsylvania) from [New York state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_(state)) through [Scranton, Lackawanna County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scranton,_Pennsylvania) and [Harrisburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harrisburg,_Pennsylvania) to [Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryland) and [Interstate 476,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_476) which begins 7

miles (11 km) north of the [Delaware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delaware) border, in [Chester,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chester,_Pennsylvania) [Delaware County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chester,_Pennsylvania) and travels 132 miles (212 km) to [Clarks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarks_Summit,_Pennsylvania) [Summit,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarks_Summit,_Pennsylvania) where it joins I-81. All but 20 miles (32 km) of I-476 is the Northeast Extension of the [Pennsylvania Turnpike.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvania_Turnpike) The highway south of the Pennsylvania Turnpike is officially called the "Veterans Memorial Highway", but is commonly referred to colloquially as the "Blue Route".

[High Point Raceway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Point_Raceway) is located in [Mount Morris, Pennsylvania,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Morris,_Pennsylvania) and Steel City is located in [Delmont, Pennsylvania.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delmont,_Pennsylvania)

[Horse racing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horse_racing) tracks in Pennsylvania include [The Meadows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hollywood_Casino_at_The_Meadows) in [North Strabane Township,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Strabane_Township,_Pennsylvania) [Mohegan Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohegan_Pennsylvania) in Wilkes-Barre, [Penn National](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hollywood_Casino_at_Penn_National_Race_Course) in [Grantville,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grantville,_Pennsylvania) [Presque Isle Downs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presque_Isle_Downs_%26_Casino) in [Summit Township,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summit_Township,_Erie_County,_Pennsylvania) and [Parx Racing,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parx_Casino_and_Racing) [Harrah's Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harrah%27s_Philadelphia) in Chester, which was the home course of [Smarty Jones,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smarty_Jones) winner of the [2004 Kentucky Derby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Kentucky_Derby) and [2004 Preakness Stakes.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Preakness_Stakes) Harrah's Philadelphia also hosts [harness racing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harness_racing) and Presque Isle Downs also hosts [thoroughbred racing.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thoroughbred_racing)

In [college football,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_football) three Pennsylvania universities compete in [NCAA Division I,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCAA_Division_I) the highest level of sanctioned collegiate play in the sport: [Penn State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penn_State_Nittany_Lions_football) in the [Big Ten Conference,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Ten_Conference) [Pitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh_Panthers_football) in the [Atlantic Coast Conference,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_Coast_Conference) and [Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_Owls_football) in the [American Athletic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Athletic_Conference) [Conference.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Athletic_Conference)

Over their respective college football histories, Penn State claims two [national championships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_football_national_championships_in_NCAA_Division_I_FBS) (1982 and 1986) and seven undefeated seasons (1887, 1912, 1968, 1969, 1973, 1986, and 1994) and Pitt has won nine national championships (1915, 1916, 1918, 1929, 1931, 1934, 1936, 1937, and 1976) and had eight undefeated seasons (1904, 1910, 1915, 1916, 1917, 1920, 1937, and 1976)[.\[220\]](#page-92-8) Penn State plays its home games at [Beaver Stadium,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaver_Stadium) a 106,572-capacity stadium that is the [second-largest stadium in the nation;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_North_American_stadiums_by_capacity) the team is coached by [James Franklin.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Franklin_(American_football_coach)) Pitt plays its home games at [Acrisure Stadium,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acrisure_Stadium) a 68,400-capacity stadium it shares with the [Pittsburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh_Steelers) [Steelers;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh_Steelers) the team is coached by [Pat Narduzzi.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pat_Narduzzi) Over their respective histories, four additional Pennsylvania universities and colleges have won national college football championships: [Lafayette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lafayette_Leopards_football) in [Easton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easton,_Pennsylvania) (1896), [Villanova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villanova_Wildcats_football) in [Villanova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villanova,_Pennsylvania) (2009), [Penn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penn_Quakers_football) in Philadelphia (1895, 1897, 1904, and 1908)[,\[221\]](#page-92-9) and [Washington & Jefferson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_%26_Jefferson_Presidents) in [Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington,_Pennsylvania) (1921).

In [college basketball,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_basketball) five Philadelphia and [Philadelphia-area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delaware_Valley) universities, collectively known as the [Big Five,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia_Big_5) have a tradition in NCAA Division I basketball. National titles in college basketball have been won by [La Salle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Salle_Explorers_men%27s_basketball) (1954), [Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_Owls_men%27s_basketball) (1938), [Penn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penn_Quakers_men%27s_basketball) (1920 and 1921), [Pitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh_Panthers_men%27s_basketball) (1928 and 1930), and [Villanova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villanova_Wildcats_men%27s_basketball) (1985, 2016, and 2018).

Pennsylvania has several universities and colleges known as national leaders in [college wrestling.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_wrestling) [Penn State,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penn_State_Nittany_Lions_wrestling) coached by

[Cael Sanderson,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cael_Sanderson) has won ten [NCAA Division I Wrestling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCAA_Division_I_Wrestling_Championships) [Championships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCAA_Division_I_Wrestling_Championships) in its history, second most among all universities and colleges after [Oklahoma State.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_State_Cowboys_wrestling) [Lehigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lehigh_Mountain_Hawks) in [Bethlehem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bethlehem,_Pennsylvania) has had 28 NCAA Division I individual champions over its history.

Since 1802, Pennsylvania has been known as the **Keystone State**, which remains the state's most popular and widelyused nickname[.\[222\]](#page-93-0) The nickname "Keystone State" originates with the agricultural and architectural term ["keystone"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keystone_(architecture)), and is based on the central role that Pennsylvania played geographically and functionally among the original [Thirteen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirteen_Colonies) [Colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirteen_Colonies) from which the nation was established, the important founding documents, including the Declaration of Independence and U. S. Constitution, that were signed and ratified in Pennsylvania, and the central role that Pennsylvania played in the nation's early manufacturing and agricultural development[.\[223\]](#page-93-1)[\[224\]](#page-93-2)

Less often, Pennsylvania is referred to as **the Coal State**, **the Oil State**, and **the Steel State**, in recognition of the important role these respective industries played in the state in the 19th and 20th centuries[.\[225\]](#page-93-3) **The State of Independence** appears on several current day road signs entering Pennsylvania from bordering states.

Pennsylvania residents and those of surrounding states sometimes refer to Pennsylvania by the state's abbreviation, **PA**. [\[226\]](#page-93-4)

Pennsylvania was historically referred to by the nickname

**Quaker State** during the [colonial era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Pennsylvania)[\[227\]](#page-93-5) based on the influential role that [William Penn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Penn) and other [Quakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quakers) played in establishing the [first frame of government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_of_Government_of_Pennsylvania) constitution for the Province of Pennsylvania that guaranteed [liberty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_(political)) of [conscience,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conscience) which was a reflection of Penn's knowledge of the hostility Quakers confronted when they opposed religious rituals, taking oaths, violence, war, and military service, and what they viewed as ostentatious [frippery.](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/frippery) [\[228\]](#page-93-6)[\[229\]](#page-93-7)[\[230\]](#page-93-8)
