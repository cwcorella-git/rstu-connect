# **Instant-runoff voting**

## Contributors to Wikimedia projects

57–73 minutes

For the proportional electoral system applying ranked ballots to multi-member constituencies, see [Single transferable vote.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_transferable_vote)

**Instant-runoff voting** (**IRV**; [US:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_English) **ranked-choice voting** (**RCV**), [AU:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_English) **preferential voting**, [UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_English)[/NZ:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_English) **alternative vote**) is a [single-winner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-winner) [ranked voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranked_voting) election system where [multi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequential_loser_method)[round eliminations i](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequential_loser_method)s used to simulate a series of [runoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runoff_(election)) [elections.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runoff_(election)) When no candidate has a majority of the votes in the first round of counting, in each following round, the candidate with the fewest [first-preferences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-preference_votes) (among the remaining candidates) is eliminated, and their votes transferred if possible. This continues until one candidate accumulates a majority of the votes still in play.

Instant runoff voting falls under the plurality-based voting rule family, in that under certain conditions the candidate with the least votes (by plurality) is eliminated[,\[1\]](#page-34-0) making use of secondary rankings as contingency votes. Thus it is related to the [two-round runoff system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runoff_election) and the [exhaustive ballot.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exhaustive_ballot) [\[2\]](#page-34-1)[\[3\]](#page-34-2) Unlike [first-past-the-post voting,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-past-the-post_voting) IRV is a sequential procedure. [\[4\]](#page-34-3) Unlike [contingent voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contingent_vote) (AKA supplementary voting), which has just two rounds of counting at most, IRV may entail numerous rounds of counting.

Instant-runoff voting (IRV) has been used [in national elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_and_use_of_instant-runoff_voting) [in several countries,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_and_use_of_instant-runoff_voting) predominantly in the [Anglosphere.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglosphere) It is used to elect members of the [Australian House of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_House_of_Representatives) [Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_House_of_Representatives) and the [National Parliament of Papua New](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Parliament_of_Papua_New_Guinea) [Guinea,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Parliament_of_Papua_New_Guinea) and to elect the [head of state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heads_of_state) in [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_India), [Ireland,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_Ireland) and [Sri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_Sri_Lanka) [Lanka.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_Sri_Lanka)

The rule was first studied by the [Marquis de Condorcet,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marquis_de_Condorcet) who observed it could eliminate the [majority-preferred candidate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majority-preferred_candidate) [\(Condorcet winner\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet_winner)[.\[5\]](#page-34-4)[\[6\]](#page-34-5)[\[7\]](#page-35-0)[\[8\]](#page-35-1) Since then, instant-runoff voting has been criticized for failing other criteria, including its ability to eliminate candidates for having [too much support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_responsiveness) or [too](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No-show_paradox) [many votes.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No-show_paradox) [\[9\]](#page-35-2) Instant-runoff voting may exhibit a kind of [Independence of irrelevant alternatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_of_irrelevant_alternatives) violation called a [center squeeze,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_squeeze) [\[10\]](#page-35-3)[\[11\]](#page-36-0) which causes it to favor uncompromising alternatives over more-moderate ones,[\[12\]](#page-36-1) [\[13\]](#page-36-2) which may in turn hinder a recovery from increasing polarization between the candidates and limit [free entry.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_entry) [\[14\]](#page-36-3) Advocates of instant-runoff voting often argue these properties are positive, as voting rules should encourage candidates to appeal to their [core support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Core_support) or political base rather than a broad coalition[.\[15\]](#page-36-4) They also note that in countries like the [UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom) without [primaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_election) or [runoffs,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-round_system) instant-runoff voting can prevent [spoiler effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spoiler_effect) by eliminating minor-party candidates. Unlike a plurality vote system where votes are non-transferable, instant-runoff voting also [avoids some kinds of vote-splitting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutual_majority_criterion) by

[near-identical \(clone\) candidates.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_of_clones_criterion) IRV has also been advocated for as a natural and practical generalization of the two-round system[.\[16\]](#page-37-0)

## Flowchart of instant-runoff voting

In instant-runoff voting, as with other [ranked voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranked_voting) rules, each voter orders candidates from first to last. The counting procedure is then as follows:

- 1. If there is a candidate that has a majority of the top preferences of the valid, active ballots, then that candidate is elected and the count stops. If not, go to step 2.
- 1. Eliminate the candidate with the fewest votes and transfer their votes to the next usable marked preference if any.[\[a\]](#page-33-0) It is possible for a candidate to win an instant-runoff race with support from less than half of the voters who cast votes. This occurs when some voters [do not rank all the candidates on](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exhausted_vote) [their ballots.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exhausted_vote) [\[17\]](#page-37-1)

In practice, the candidate who leads the first round usually finishes with a majority, or enough to win the seat: the IRV winner is usually the same as the first-past-the-post winner.[\[18\]](#page-37-2)

# **Wasted votes and Condorcet winners**

# [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Instant-runoff_voting&action=edit§ion=3)

Compared to a plurality voting system that rewards only the top vote-getter using non-transferable votes, instant-runoff voting mitigates the problem of [wasted votes.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wasted_vote) [\[19\]](#page-37-3) However, it does not ensure the election of a [Condorcet winner,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet_winner_criterion) which is the candidate who would win a direct election against any other candidate in the race.

Some advocates of instant-runoff voting argue that the failure to elect a Condorcet winner is positive, as it enables instantrunoff voting to pass [later-no-help](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Later-no-help) and [later-no-harm,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Later-no-harm) which together render the method immune to [burying strategy.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_voting#Common_types_of_strategic_voting) Fair Vote, in particular, has stated that they "believe [later-noharm] is necessary in the context of high-stakes, competitive elections"[.\[20\]](#page-37-4)

#### **Invalid, incomplete and exhausted ballots**

# [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Instant-runoff_voting&action=edit§ion=4)

All forms of ranked-choice voting reduce to plurality when all ballots rank only one candidate. By extension, ballots for which all candidates ranked are eliminated are equivalent to votes for any non-winner in plurality, and considered [exhausted ballots.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exhausted_ballots)

Some political scientists have found the IRV system contributes to higher rates of [spoiled votes,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spoiled_vote) [\[21\]](#page-37-5) partly because the ballot marking is more complex[.\[21\]](#page-37-5)[\[18\]](#page-37-2) Most jurisdictions with instant-runoff voting do not require complete rankings and may use columns to indicate preference instead of numbers. In

American elections with instant-runoff voting, more than 99 percent of voters typically cast a valid ballot.[\[22\]](#page-37-6)

A 2015 study of four local US elections that used instant-runoff voting found that inactive ballots occurred often enough in each of them that the winner of each election did not receive a majority of votes cast in the first round. The rate of inactive ballots in each election ranged from a low of 9.6 percent to a high of 27.1 percent[.\[23\]](#page-37-7)

#### **Resistance to strategy**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Instant-runoff_voting&action=edit§ion=5)

Instant-runoff voting has notably high resistance to [tactical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_voting) [voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_voting) but less to [strategic nomination.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_nomination)

#### **Party strategizing and strategic nomination**

# [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Instant-runoff_voting&action=edit§ion=6)

In Australia, preference deals (where one party's voters agree to place another party's voters second, in return for their doing the same) between parties are common. Parties and candidates often encourage their supporters to participate in these preference deals using [How-to-vote cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How-to-vote_card) explaining how to use their lower rankings to maximize the chances of their ballot helping to elect someone in the preference deal before it may exhaust[.\[24\]](#page-38-0)

Instant runoff may be manipulable via strategic candidate entry and exit, reducing similar candidates' chances of winning. Such manipulation does not need to be intentional, instead acting to deter candidates from running in the first place[.\[25\]](#page-38-1) Spatial model simulations indicate that instant runoff rewards strategic withdrawal by candidates[.\[26\]](#page-38-2)[\[b\]](#page-34-6)

[Gibbard's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibbard%27s_theorem) demonstrates that no (deterministic, nondictatorial) voting method can be entirely immune from tactical voting. This implies that instant-runoff voting is susceptible to tactical voting in some circumstances. In particular, when there exists a [Condorcet winner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet_winner) who instantrunoff voting fails to elect, voters who prefer the Condorcet winner to the instant-runoff voting winner have an incentive to use the [compromising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tactical_voting#Types_of_strategic_voting) strategy[.\[26\]:](#page-38-2)proposition 17 instant-runoff voting is also sometimes vulnerable to a paradoxical strategy of ranking a candidate higher to make them lose, due to instant-runoff voting failing the [monotonicity criterion.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotonicity_criterion) [\[27\]](#page-38-3)

Research suggests that instant-runoff voting is highly resistant to strategic voting. In a test of multiple methods, instant runoff was found to be the second-most-resistant to tactical voting, after a class of instant runoff[-Condorcet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet_method) hybrids[.\[28\]](#page-38-4) Instantrunoff voting is also completely immune to the burying strategy: ranking a strong opposition candidate lower can't get one's preferred candidate elected[.\[26\]:](#page-38-2)proposition 3

Tactical voting in instant-runoff voting seeks to alter the order of eliminations in early rounds, to ensure that the original winner is challenged by a stronger opponent in the final round. For example, in a three-party election where voters for both the [left](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left-wing_politics) and [right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-wing_politics) prefer the [centrist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centrism) candidate to stop the opposing candidate from winning, those voters who care more about defeating the opposition than electing their own candidate may cast a tactical first-preference vote for the centrist candidate.

Proponents of instant-runoff voting claim that instant-runoff voting eliminates the spoiler effect, since instant-runoff voting makes it safe to vote honestly for marginal parties. Under a plurality method, voters who sympathize most strongly with a marginal candidate are strongly encouraged to instead vote for a more popular candidate who shares some of the same principles, since that candidate has a much greater chance of being elected, and a vote for the marginal candidate will not result in the marginal candidate's election. Instant-runoff voting reduces this problem, since the voter can rank the marginal candidate first and the mainstream candidate second; in the likely event that the fringe candidate is eliminated, the vote is not wasted but is transferred to the second preference.

However, when the third-party candidate is more competitive, they can still act as a spoiler under instant-runoff voting,[\[29\]](#page-39-0)[\[30\]](#page-39-1) [\[31\]](#page-39-2) by taking away first-choice votes from the more mainstream candidate until that candidate is eliminated, and then that candidate's second-choice votes helping a moredisliked candidate to win. In these scenarios, it would have been better for the third party voters if their candidate had not run at all (spoiler effect), or if they had voted dishonestly, ranking their second-favorite first and their favorite second, rather than first (favorite betrayal)[.\[32\]\[](#page-39-3)better source [needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:NOTRS)] This is the same bracketing effect exploited by Robinette and

Tideman in their research on strategic campaigning, where a candidate alters their campaign to cause a change in voter honest choice, resulting in the elimination of a candidate who nevertheless remains more preferred by voters.

For example, in the [2009 Burlington, Vermont, mayoral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_Burlington_mayoral_election) [election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_Burlington_mayoral_election), if the Republican candidate who lost in the final instant runoff had not run, the Democratic candidate would have defeated the winning Progressive candidate. In that sense, the Republican candidate was a spoiler—albeit for an opposing Democrat, rather than some political ally—even though leading in first choice support[.\[31\]](#page-39-2) This also occurred in the [2022 Alaska's at-large congressional district special](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Alaska%27s_at-large_congressional_district_special_election) [election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Alaska%27s_at-large_congressional_district_special_election). If Republican [Sarah Palin,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_Palin) who lost in the final instant runoff, had not run, the more centrist Republican candidate, Nick Begich, would have defeated the winning Democratic candidate, [Mary Peltola.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Peltola) [\[33\]](#page-39-4)

The system has had a mixed reception among [political](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_science) [scientists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_science) and [social choice theorists.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_choice_theory) [\[34\]](#page-40-0)[\[35\]](#page-40-1) Some have suggested that the system does not do much to decrease the impact of [wasted votes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wasted_vote) relative to plurality[.\[36\]](#page-40-2)[\[21\]](#page-37-5)[\[37\]](#page-40-3) Research has found instant-runoff voting causes lower confidence in election[s\[38\]](#page-40-4)[\[39\]](#page-40-5)[\[40\]](#page-40-6) and does not substantially affect minority representation[,\[41\]](#page-40-7) [voter turnout,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voter_turnout) [\[34\]](#page-40-0)[\[37\]](#page-40-3) or long-run [electoral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_competition) [competition.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_competition) [\[34\]](#page-40-0)[\[41\]](#page-40-7) Opponents have also noted a high rate of repeals for the system[.\[39\]](#page-40-5)

#### **Voter confusion and legitimacy**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Instant-runoff_voting&action=edit§ion=10)

Governor [Paul Le Page](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Le Page)[\[42\]](#page-41-0) and Representative Bruce Poliqui[n\[43\]](#page-41-1) claimed, ahead of the 2018 primary elections, that instant-runoff voting would result in "one person, five votes", as opposed to ["one person, one vote"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_person,_one_vote). Federal judge Lance Walker rejected these claims, and the 1st circuit court denied Poliquin's emergency appeal[.\[44\]](#page-41-2)

#### **Similarity to plurality**

# [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Instant-runoff_voting&action=edit§ion=11)

Often instant-runoff voting elections are won by the candidate who leads in first-count vote tallies so they choose the same winner as [first-past-the-post voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-past-the-post_voting) would have. In Australia federal elections, the [1972 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1972_Australian_federal_election) had the largest number of winners who would not have won under first past the post but still only 14 out of 125 seats filled were not won by the firstcount leader[.\[45\]](#page-41-3)

The effect of instant-runoff voting on voter turnout is difficult to assess. In a 2021 report, researchers at [New America,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_America_(organization)) a [think](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Think_tank) [tank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Think_tank) based in Washington, D. C., said it may increase turnout by attracting more and more diverse candidates, but the impact would be realized most significantly by eliminating the need for primaries[.\[46\]](#page-41-4) The overall impact on diversity of candidates is difficult to detect[.\[34\]](#page-40-0)

While instant run-off voting is distinguished from its multiple winner equivalent, the [single transferable vote,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_transferable_vote) most Englishspeaking discussion of electoral systems does not differentiate them. In [Ireland,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Ireland) one of the few countries to use these systems in all elections, no distinction between the two is made, either by the general population or in legal texts. The [Constitution of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Ireland) [Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Ireland) describes the electoral system as "proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote",[\[47\]](#page-41-5) as do all other statutory authorities, when referring to either single-winner or multiple-winner elections. The acronym "PR-STV" is in general use to describe both types of elections. Examples of single-winner elections in Ireland which are described officially as "proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote" are the [election of the President](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_presidential_election) and the [election of the Ceann Comhairle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Ceann_Comhairle_election) (chairperson of [Dáil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A1il_%C3%89ireann) [Éireann\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A1il_%C3%89ireann). The lack of distinction between the systems in Ireland reflects that there is no difference in the mechanics of the process from election to election, only the number of candidates to be elected by that process. This is not always the case when discussing the systems in the abstract, as there are many variations in how such elections could be run.

Instant-runoff voting derives its name from the way the ballot count simulates a series of runoffs, similar to an [exhaustive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exhaustive_ballot) [ballot system,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exhaustive_ballot) except that voters do not need to turn out several times to vote[.\[48\]](#page-41-6) It is also known as the alternative vote, transferable vote, ranked-choice voting (RCV), singleseat ranked-choice voting, or preferential voting (but use of some of those terms may lead to misunderstanding as they also apply to single transferable vote.[\)\[49\]](#page-41-7)

Britons and New Zealanders generally call instant-runoff voting the "alternative vote" (AV)[.\[50\]](#page-41-8)[\[51\]](#page-41-9) Australians, who use instant-

runoff voting for most single winner elections, call instantrunoff voting "preferential voting"[.\[52\]](#page-42-0) While this term is widely used by Australians, it is somewhat of a [misnomer.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misnomer) Depending on how "preferential" is defined, the term would include all voting systems, apply to any system that uses ranked ballots (thus both instant-runoff voting and single transferable vote), or would exclude instant-runoff voting (instant-runoff voting fails [positive responsiveness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotonicity_criterion) because ballot markings are not interpreted as "preferences" in the traditional sense. Under instant-runoff voting (and single transferable vote), secondary preferences are used as back-up preferences/contingency votes).

Jurisdictions in the United States such as [San Francisco,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco) [Minneapolis,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minneapolis) [Maine,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maine) and [Alaska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaska) have tended to use the term "ranked-choice voting" in their laws that apply to instant-runoff voting contests. The San Francisco Department of Elections claimed the word "instant" in the term "instant-runoff voting" could confuse voters into expecting results to be immediately available[.\[53\]](#page-42-1)[\[54\]](#page-42-2) As a result of American influence, the term ranked-choice voting is often used in Canada as well.[\[55\]](#page-42-3) When discussing his promise of electoral reform, Canadian Prime Minister Justin Trudeau used the term "preferential ballot".[\[56\]](#page-42-4) American NGO [Fair Vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fair Vote) has promoted the terminology "ranked-choice voting" to refer to instant-runoff voting,[\[55\]](#page-42-3)[\[57\]](#page-42-5) a choice that has caused controversy and accusations that the organization is attempting to obscure the existence of other [ranked-choice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranked_voting) methods that are competing or could compete with instant-runoff voting.[[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)]

Instant-runoff voting is occasionally referred to as Hare's metho[d\[58\]](#page-43-0) (after [Thomas Hare\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hare_(political_scientist)) to differentiate it from other ranked-choice voting methods such as [majority-choice voting,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet_winner_criterion) [Borda,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borda_count) and [Bucklin,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bucklin_voting) which use weighted preferences or methods that allow voter's lower preference to be used against voter's most-preferred choice.

When the [single transferable vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_transferable_vote) (STV) method is applied to a single-winner election, it becomes instant-runoff voting; the government of Ireland has called instant-runoff voting "proportional representation" based on the fact that the same ballot form is used to elect its president by instant-runoff voting and parliamentary seats by proportional representation (single transferable vote), but instant-runoff voting is a nonproportional single-winner election method, while single transferable vote elects multiple winners and produces minority representation, as well as majority representation, in almost all cases[.\[59\]](#page-43-1)

State law in [South Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Carolina)[\[60\]](#page-43-2) and [Arkansas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arkansas)[\[61\]](#page-43-3) use "instant runoff" to describe the practice of having certain categories of absentee voters cast ranked-choice ballots before the first round of an election and counting those ballots in any subsequent runoff elections.

This method was first discussed by the [Marquis de Condorcet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marquis_de_Condorcet) in 1788, who quickly rejected it after showing it would often eliminate a candidate preferred by a majority of voters to any other candidate[.\[62\]](#page-43-4)[\[63\]](#page-43-5)

Instant-runoff voting was later independently reinvented by

[Thomas Hare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hare_(political_reformer)) (of England) and Carl Andrae (of Denmark) in the form of the [single transferable vote.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_transferable_vote) [Henry Richmond Droop,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Richmond_Droop) inventor of the [Droop quota,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Droop_quota) then proposed applying the system to single-winner contests[.\[64\]](#page-43-6)

After conducting a demonstration STV election in 1881, [William](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Robert_Ware) [R. Ware,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Robert_Ware) proposed that a single-winner versions (IRV) could be used to determine the winner in a byelection, thus helping push IRV as workable system. IRV was once known as "Ware's system" due to this advocacy[.\[65\]](#page-43-7)

#### **Robert's Rules of Order**

[\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Instant-runoff_voting&action=edit§ion=18)

In the United States, the sequential elimination method used by instant-runoff voting is described in [Robert's Rules of Order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert%27s_Rules_of_Order) [Newly Revised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert%27s_Rules_of_Order) as an example of [ranked-choice voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranked_voting_systems) that can be used to elect officers[.\[72\]](#page-44-4) Robert's Rules note that rankedchoice systems (including instant-runoff voting) are an improvement on [simple plurality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plurality_voting) but recommend against runoff-based rules because they often prevent the emergence of a consensus candidate with broad support. The book instead recommends repeated balloting until some candidate manages to win a majority of votes. Two other books on American parliamentary procedure, [The Standard Code of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Standard_Code_of_Parliamentary_Procedure) [Parliamentary Procedure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Standard_Code_of_Parliamentary_Procedure)[\[73\]](#page-44-5) and [Riddick's Rules of Procedure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riddick%27s_Rules_of_Procedure), [\[74\]](#page-44-6) take a similar stance.

The term instant-runoff voting is derived from the name of a class of voting methods called runoff voting. In runoff voting voters do not rank candidates on the ballot - each voter marks only one preference. When no candidate receives a majority of voters in the first round, voters cast votes in two or more subsequent rounds of voting as the field of candidates is reduced. Multi-round runoff voting methods allow voters to change their preferences in each round, incorporating the results of the prior round to influence their decision, which is not possible in instant-runoff voting.

The runoff method closest to instant-runoff voting is the [exhaustive ballot.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exhaustive_ballot) In this method—familiar to fans of the television show [American Idol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Idol)—one candidate is eliminated in each round, and many rounds of voting may be necessary. Because holding many rounds of voting on separate days is generally expensive, the exhaustive ballot is not used for largescale, public elections.

A more practical form of runoff voting is the [two-round system.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-round_system) If no candidate receives a majority of votes in the first round, all but the top-two candidates are excluded after the first round, and just one more round of voting determines the winner. Eliminations can occur with or without allowing and applying preference votes to choose the final two candidates. This method is used in Mali and France, and in Finnish and Slovenian presidential elections.

## Top-two instant-runoff voting

The [contingent vote,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contingent_vote) also known as "top-two instant-runoff voting" ("top-two IRV"), is the same as instant-runoff voting, except that if no candidate achieves a majority in the first round of counting, all but the two candidates with the most votes are eliminated, and the second preferences for those ballots are counted. As in instant-runoff voting, there is only one round of voting. Unlike instant-runoff voting, the vote count process entails only two rounds of counting, while instant-runoff voting can entail many rounds.

Under a variant of contingent voting used in [Sri Lanka,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sri_Lanka) and formerly for the elections for [Mayor of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor_of_London) in the United Kingdom, voters rank a specified maximum number of candidates. In London, the [supplementary vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contingent_vote) allowe[d\[c\]](#page-34-7) voters to express first and second preferences only. Sri Lankan voters [rank up to three candidates to elect the president of Sri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contingent_vote#contingent_vote_in_Sri_Lanka) [Lanka.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contingent_vote#contingent_vote_in_Sri_Lanka)

While similar to "sequential-elimination" instant-runoff voting, top-two can produce different results. Excluding more than one candidate after the first count might eliminate a candidate who would have won under sequential elimination instant-runoff voting. Restricting voters to a maximum number of preferences is more likely to exhaust ballots if voters do not anticipate which candidates will finish in the top two. This can encourage voters to vote more [tactically,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tactical_voting) by ranking at least one candidate they think is likely to win.

Conversely, a practical benefit of 'contingent voting' is expediency and confidence in the result with only two rounds.

# **Larger runoff process**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Instant-runoff_voting&action=edit§ion=22)

Instant-runoff voting may also be part of a larger runoff process:

- Some jurisdictions that hold [runoff elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-round_system) allow absentee (only) voters to submit ranked ballots, because the interval between rounds of balloting is too short for a second round of absentee voting. Ranked ballots enable absentee votes to count in the second (general) election round if their first choice does not make the second round runoff. [Arkansas,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arkansas) [South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Carolina) [Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Carolina) and [Springfield, Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Springfield,_Illinois) adopt this approach[.\[75\]](#page-45-0) Louisiana uses it only for members of the United States Service or who reside overseas[.\[76\]\[](#page-45-1)better source [needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:NOTRS)][[needs update](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Dates_and_numbers#Chronological_items)]

- Under some [exhaustive ballot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exhaustive_ballot) runoff systems, instant-runoff voting may be used to quickly eliminate some weak candidates in early rounds, using rules to leave a desired number of candidates for further balloting.
- Instant-runoff voting elections that require a majority of cast ballots but not that voters rank all candidates may require more than a single instant-runoff voting contest due to exhausted ballots.[[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)] (Most IRV systems require a winner to have only a majority of votes still in play, not a majority of votes cast.)
- Robert's Rules recommends [preferential voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranked_voting_systems) for elections by mail and requiring a majority of votes to elect a winner. For inperson elections, they recommend repeated balloting until one candidate receives a majority of votes; if candidates drop out when it becomes clear they will not win, this procedure will always elect a [Condorcet winner.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet_method) The use of repeated balloting allows voters to resolve [Condorcet cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet_paradox) by discussion and compromise, or by electing a consensus candidate who might have polled poorly in the initial election[.\[72\]](#page-44-4)

#### **Comparison to first-past-the-post**

[\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Instant-runoff_voting&action=edit§ion=23)

In the [Australian federal election in September 2013,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2013_Australian_federal_election) 135 out of the 150 [House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_House_of_Representatives) seats (or 90 percent) were won by the candidate who led on first preferences. The other 15 seats (10 percent) were won by the candidate who placed second on first preferences[.\[77\]](#page-45-2)

![](_page_19_Figure_3.jpeg)

Example of a full preferential ballot paper from the [Australian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_House_of_Representatives) [House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_House_of_Representatives)

A number of instant-runoff voting methods, varying as to ballot design and as to whether or not voters are obliged to rank all the candidates, are in use in different countries and local governments.

In an [optional preferential voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optional_preferential_voting) system, voters can give a preference to as many candidates as they wish. They may make only a single choice, known as ["bullet voting"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bullet_voting), and some jurisdictions accept a single box marked with an "X" (as opposed to a numeral "1") as valid for the first preference. Bullet voting may result in exhausted ballots, where all of a voter's preferences are eliminated before a candidate is elected, such that the "majority" in the final round may constitute only a minority of all ballots cast. Optional preferential voting is used for elections for the [President of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_Ireland) [Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_Ireland) as well as some elections in [New South Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_South_Wales) and [Queensland.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queensland) [\[78\]](#page-45-3)[\[79\]](#page-45-4)

In a full-preferential voting method, voters are required to mark a preference for every candidate standing[.\[80\]](#page-45-5) Ballots that do not contain a ranking of all candidates are in some jurisdictions considered [spoilt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spoilt_vote) or invalid, even if there are only two candidates standing. The full-preferential voting method can become burdensome in elections with many candidates and can lead to ["donkey voting"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donkey_voting), in which some voters simply choose candidates at random or in top-to-bottom order, or a voter may order his or her preferred candidates and then fill in the remainder on a donkey basis. Full preferential voting is used for elections to the [Australian federal parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_electoral_system) and for most [state parliaments.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_systems_of_the_Australian_states_and_territories)

Other methods only allow marking preferences for a maximum of the voter's top three favourites, a form of partial preferential voting[.\[81\]](#page-45-6) Such a system produces exhausted votes even when the voter would have been willing to rank more candidates.

A version of instant-runoff voting applying to the ranking of parties was first proposed for elections in Germany in 2013[\[82\]](#page-45-7) as [spare vote.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spare_vote) [[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)]

# **Voting method criteria**

# [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Instant-runoff_voting&action=edit§ion=25)

As shown by [Arrow,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrow%27s_impossibility_theorem) [Gibbard,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibbard%27s_theorem) and others, it's usually impossible for a method to pass all of a number of seemingly reasonable properties, or criteria, at once. Instant-runoff voting is no exception: it passes some and fails others. The following criteria are satisfied and failed by instant-runoff voting:

#### **Condorcet loser Criterion**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Instant-runoff_voting&action=edit§ion=27)

The **[Condorcet loser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet_loser_criterion)** criterion states that "if a candidate would lose a [head-to-head competition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet_method) against every other candidate, then that candidate must not win the overall election". Instant-runoff voting (like all voting methods with a final runoff round) meets this criterion, since the Condorcet loser cannot win a runoff.

#### **Independence of clones criterion**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Instant-runoff_voting&action=edit§ion=28)

The [independence of clones criterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_of_clones_criterion) states that "the election outcome remains the same even if an identical candidate who is equally preferred decides to run". Advocates have noted that instant-runoff voting meeting this criterio[n\[83\]](#page-46-0)[\[84\]](#page-46-1) greatly reduces the impact of clones compared to [FPTP.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-preference_plurality)

#### **Later-no-harm criterion**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Instant-runoff_voting&action=edit§ion=29)

The **[later-no-harm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Later-no-harm_criterion)** criterion states that "if a voter alters the order of candidates lower in his/her preference (e.g. swapping the second and third preferences), then that does not affect the chances of the most preferred candidate being elected". Instant runoff satisfies this criterion.

The **[majority criterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majority_favorite_criterion)** states that "if one candidate is preferred by an absolute majority of voters, then that candidate must win". Instant runoff also satisfies this criterion.

### **Mutual majority criterion**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Instant-runoff_voting&action=edit§ion=31)

The **[mutual majority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutual_majority_criterion)** criterion states that "if an absolute majority of voters prefer every member of a group of candidates to every candidate not in that group, then one of the preferred group must win". Note that this is satisfied because when all but one candidate that a mutual majority prefer is eliminated, the votes of the majority all flow to the remaining candidate, in contrast to [FPTP,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FPTP) where the majority would be treated as separate small groups. Instant runoff satisfies this criterion as well.

## **Resolvability criterion**

# [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Instant-runoff_voting&action=edit§ion=32)

The **[resolvability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resolvability_criterion)** criterion states that "the probability of an exact tie must diminish as more votes are cast".[[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)]

#### **Condorcet winner criterion**

### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Instant-runoff_voting&action=edit§ion=34)

The **[Condorcet winner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet_criterion)** criterion states that "if a candidate would win a [head-to-head competition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet_method) against every other candidate, then that candidate must win the overall election". It is incompatible with the later-no-harm criterion, so instantrunoff voting does not meet this criterion.

Instant-runoff voting is more likely to elect the Condorcet winner than [plurality voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plurality_voting) and [traditional runoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-round_system) elections. The California cities of Oakland, San Francisco and San Leandro in 2010 provide an example; there were a total of four elections in which the plurality-voting leader in first-choice rankings was defeated, and in each case the instant-runoff voting winner was the Condorcet winner, including a San Francisco election in which the instant-runoff voting winner was in third place in first choice rankings.

A particularly notable Condorcet failure occurred in the [2009](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_Burlington_mayoral_election) [Burlington mayoral election.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_Burlington_mayoral_election)

# **Independence of irrelevant alternatives**

# [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Instant-runoff_voting&action=edit§ion=35)

The [independence of irrelevant alternatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_of_irrelevant_alternatives) criterion states that "the election outcome remains the same even if a candidate who cannot win decides to run." Instant-runoff voting violates this. In the general case, instant-runoff voting can be susceptible to [strategic nomination:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_nomination) whether or not a candidate decides to run at all can affect the result even if the new candidate cannot themselves win. This is less likely to happen than under plurality, but much more likely than under the [Minimax Condorcet method.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimax_Condorcet_method) [\[26\]](#page-38-2)

#### **Monotonicity criterion**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Instant-runoff_voting&action=edit§ion=36)

The [monotonicity criterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotonicity_criterion) says that a voter ranking a candidate higher on their ballot should not cause that candidate to lose and conversely, that a voter ranking a candidate lower on their ballot should not help that candidate win. The exact probability of a monotonicity failure depends on the circumstances, but with 3 major candidates, the probabilities range from 15 percent under the [impartial culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impartial_culture) [model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impartial_culture)[\[85\]:](#page-46-2)Table 6 to 8.5 percent in the case of a strict [left–right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left%E2%80%93right_political_spectrum) [spectrum.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left%E2%80%93right_political_spectrum) [\[86\]](#page-46-3)

# **Participation criterion**

# [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Instant-runoff_voting&action=edit§ion=37)

The [participation criterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Participation_criterion) says that candidates should not lose as a result of having "too many voters"—a set of ballots that all rank A>B should not switch the election winner from B to A. Instant-runoff voting fails this criterion. In his 1984 study, mathematician Depankar Ray found that in elections where instant-runoff voting elects a different candidate from plurality, that there was an estimated 50 percent probability that some voters would have received a more preferable outcome if they had not participated[.\[87\]](#page-46-4)

#### **Reversal symmetry criterion**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Instant-runoff_voting&action=edit§ion=38)

The [reversal symmetry criterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reversal_symmetry) states that the first- and lastplace candidates should switch places if every ballot is reversed. In other words, it should not matter whether voters rank candidates from best-to-worst and select the best candidate, or whether they rank them worst-to-best and then select the least-bad candidate.

Instant-runoff voting fails this criterion: it is possible to construct an election where reversing the order of every ballot does not alter the final winner; that is, the first- and last-place finishers, according to instant-runoff voting, are the same candidate[.\[88\]](#page-46-5)

#### **Comparison to other voting systems**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Instant-runoff_voting&action=edit§ion=39)

Under Burlington, Vermont's second-ever instant-runoff voting mayoral election in 2009, the winner, Bob Kiss, was elected over the more-popular Andy Montroll as a result of a first-round spoiler effect.

[Fair Vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fair Vote) touted the 2009 election as one of its major success stories[,\[91\]](#page-47-2) claiming it helped the city save on costs of a traditional runof[f\[91\]](#page-47-2)[\[92\]](#page-47-3) and prevented a [spoiler effect,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spoiler_effect) [\[93\]](#page-47-4) although later analysis showed that without Wright in the election, Montroll would have defeated Kiss in a one-on-one race[.\[94\]](#page-47-5)

[Mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematicians) and [voting theorists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_theory) criticized the election results as revealing several [pathologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathological_(mathematics)) associated with instant-runoff voting, noting that Kiss was elected as a result of 750 votes [cast against him](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Participation_criterion) (ranking Kiss in last place)[.\[95\]](#page-48-0)[\[96\]](#page-48-1)

Several [electoral reform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_reform) advocates branded the election a failure after Kiss was elected, despite 54 percent of voters voting for Montroll over Kiss[,\[97\]](#page-48-2) violating the principle of [majority rule.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet_winner) [\[94\]](#page-47-5)[\[98\]](#page-48-3)[\[99\]](#page-49-0)[\[100\]](#page-49-1)
