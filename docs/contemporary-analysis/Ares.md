# **Ares**

**Ares** (/[ˈɛə](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English)riːz/; [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language): Ἄρης, *Árēs* [\[ár](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Greek)ɛːs]) is the [Greek god](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Greek_deities) of [war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_god) and courage. He is one of the [Twelve Olympians,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_Olympians) and the son of [Zeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeus) and [Hera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hera). Many Greeks were ambivalent towards him. He embodies the physical valor necessary for success in war but can also personify sheer brutality and bloodlust, in contrast to his sister [Athena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athena), whose martial functions include military strategy and generalship. An association with Ares endows places, objects, and other deities with a savage, dangerous, or militarized quality.

Although Ares's name shows his origins as [Mycenaean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycenae), his reputation for savagery was thought by some to reflect his likely origins as a Thracian deity. Some cities in Greece and several in Asia Minor held annual festivals to bind and detain him as their protector. In parts of Asia Minor, he was an oracular deity. Still further away from Greece, the [Scythians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scythians) were said to ritually kill one in a hundred prisoners of war as an offering to their equivalent of Ares. The later belief that ancient Spartans had offered human sacrifice to Ares may owe more to mythical prehistory, misunderstandings, and reputation than to reality.

Although there are many literary allusions to Ares's love affairs and children, he has a limited role in [Greek mythology.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mythology) When he does appear, he is often humiliated. In the [Trojan War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trojan_War), [Aphrodite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aphrodite), protector of Troy, persuades Ares to take the Trojans' side. The Trojans lose, while Ares's sister Athena helps the Greeks to victory. Most famously, when the craftsman-god [Hephaestus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hephaestus) discovers his wife [Aphrodite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aphrodite) is having an affair with Ares, he traps the lovers in a net and exposes them to the ridicule of the other gods.

Ares's nearest counterpart in Roman religion is [Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_(mythology)), who was given a more important and dignified place in [ancient Roman religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_ancient_Rome) as ancestral protector of the Roman people and state. During the [Hellenization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenization) of [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_literature) [literature,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_literature) the myths of Ares were [reinterpreted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpretatio_graeca) by Roman writers under the name of Mars, and in later [Western art and literature,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_culture) the mythology of the two figures became virtually indistinguishable.

### **Names**

The etymology of the name *Ares* is traditionally connected with the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) word ἀρή (*arē*), the [Ionic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionic_Greek) form of the [Doric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doric_Greek) ἀρά (*ara*), "bane, ruin, curse, imprecation".[\[2\]](#page-11-1) [Walter Burkert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Burkert) notes that "Ares is apparently an ancient abstract noun meaning throng of battle, war."[\[3\]](#page-11-2) [R. S. P. Beekes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_S._P._Beekes) has suggested a [Pre-Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Greek) origin of the name.[\[4\]](#page-11-3) The earliest attested form of the name is the [Mycenaean Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycenaean_language) , *a-re*, written in the [Linear B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_B) syllabic script.[\[5\]](#page-11-4)[\[6\]](#page-11-5)[\[7\]](#page-11-6)

The [adjectival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjective) [epithet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epithet), *Areios* ("warlike") was frequently appended to the names of other gods when they took on a warrior aspect or became involved in warfare: *Zeus Areios*, *Athena Areia*, even [Aphrodite Areia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aphrodite_Areia)

### **Cult**

In mainland Greece and the [Peloponnese,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peloponnese) only a few places are known to have had a formal temple and cult of Ares.[\[18\]](#page-12-8)\[n 1] [Pausanias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pausanias_(geographer)) (2nd century AD) notes an altar to Ares at [Olympia,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympia,_Greece) [\[19\]](#page-12-9) and the moving of a [Temple of Ares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Ares) to the [Athenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Agora_of_Athens) [agora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Agora_of_Athens) during the reign of [Augustus,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustus) essentially rededicating it (2 AD) as a [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_temple) [temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_temple) to the Augustan [Mars Ultor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_Ultor). [\[18\]](#page-12-8) The [Areopagus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Areopagus) ("mount of Ares"), a natural rock outcrop in Athens, some distance from the Acropolis, was supposedly where Ares was tried and acquitted by the gods for his revenge-killing of [Poseidon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poseidon)'s son, [Halirrhothius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halirrhothius), who had raped Ares's daughter [Alcippe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcippe_(daughter_of_Ares)). Its name was used for the court that met there, mostly to investigate and try potential cases of treason.[\[20\]](#page-12-10)

Numismatist [M. #### **Chained statues**

Gods were immortal but could be bound and restrained, both in mythic narrative and in cult practice. There was an archaic [Spartan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparta) statue of Ares in chains in the temple of [Enyalios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enyalios) (sometimes regarded as the son of Ares, sometimes as Ares himself), which Pausanias claimed meant that the spirit of war and victory was to be kept in the city. \[n 2] The Spartans are known to have ritually bound the images of other deities, including [Aphrodite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aphrodite) and Artemis (cf Ares and Aphrodite bound by Hephaestus), and in other places there were chained statues of Artemis and Dionysos.[\[29\]](#page-12-18)[\[30\]](#page-12-19)

Statues of Ares in chains are described in the instructions given by an oracle of the late Hellenistic era to various cities of [Pamphylia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pamphylia) (in Anatolia) including [Syedra,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syedra) [Lycia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycia) and [Cilicia,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cilicia) places almost perpetually under threat from pirates. Each was told to set up a statue of "bloody, man-slaying Ares" and provide it with an annual festival in which it was ritually bound with iron fetters ("by [Dike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dike_(mythology)) and Hermes") as if a supplicant for justice, put on trial and offered sacrifice. The oracle promises that "thus will he become a peaceful deity for you, once he has driven the enemy horde far from your country, and he will give rise to prosperity much prayed for". This Ares *karpodotes* ("giver of Fruits") is well attested in Lycia and Pisidia.[\[31\]](#page-12-20)

#### **Sacrifices**

Like most Greek deities, Ares was given animal sacrifice; in Sparta, after battle, he was given an ox for a victory by stratagem, or a rooster for victory through onslaught.[\[32\]\[](#page-12-21)n [3\]](#page-11-11) The usual recipient of sacrifice before battle was Athena. Reports of historic human sacrifice to Ares in an obscure rite known as the *Hekatomphonia* represent a very long-standing error, repeated through several centuries and well into the modern era.\[n 4] The *hekatomphonia* was an animal sacrifice to Zeus; it could be offered by any warrior who had personally slain one hundred of the enemy. [n [5\]](#page-11-13)[\[33\]](#page-12-22)[\[34\]](#page-12-23) Pausanias reports that in Sparta, each company of youths sacrificed a puppy to Enyalios before engaging in a hand-to-hand "fight without rules" at the Phoebaeum.[n [6\]](#page-11-14)[\[36\]](#page-12-24) The [chthonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chthonic) night-time sacrifice of a dog to Enyalios became assimilated to the cult of Ares.[\[37\]](#page-12-25) [Porphyry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porphyry_(philosopher)) claims, without detail, that Apollodorus of Athens (circa second century BC) says the [Spartans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparta) made human sacrifices to Ares, but this may be a reference to mythic pre-history. [\[38\]](#page-13-0)

#### **Thrace and Scythia**

A Thracian god identified by [Herodotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodotus) (c. 484 – c. 425 BC) as Ares, through *[interpretatio Graeca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpretatio_Graeca)*, was one of three otherwise unnamed deities that Thracian commoners were said to worship. Herodotus recognises and names the other two as "Dionysus" and "Artemis", and claims that the Thracian aristocracy exclusively worshiped "Hermes".[\[39\]](#page-13-1)[\[40\]](#page-13-2) In Herodotus's *Histories*, the [Scythians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scythian) worship an indigenous form of Greek Ares, who is otherwise unnamed, but ranked beneath [Tabiti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tabiti) (whom Herodotus claims as a form of [Hestia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hestia)), Api and Papaios in Scythia's divine hierarchy. His cult object was an iron sword. The "Scythian Ares" was offered blood-sacrifices (or ritual killings) of cattle, horses and "one in every hundred human war-captives", whose blood was used to douse the sword. Statues, and complex platform-altars made of heaped brushwood were devoted to him. This sword-cult, or one very similar, is said to have persisted among the [Alans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alans). [\[41\]](#page-13-3) Some have posited that the "[Sword of Mars"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sword_of_Mars) in later European history alludes to the [Huns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huns) having adopted Ares.[\[42\]](#page-13-4)

#### **Asia Minor**

In some parts of Asia Minor, Ares was a prominent [oracular deity,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oracle) something not found in any Hellennic cult to Ares or Roman cult to Mars. Ares was linked in some regions or polities with a local god or cultic hero, and recognised as a higher, more prestigious deity than in mainland Greece. His cults in southern Asia Minor are attested from the 5th century BC and well into the later Roman Imperial era, at 29 different sites, and on over 70 local coin issues.[\[43\]](#page-13-5) He is sometimes represented on coinage of the region by the "Helmet of Ares" or carrying a spear and a shield, or as a fully armed warrior, sometimes accompanied by a female deity. In what is now western Turkey, the Hellenistic city of [Metropolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolis_(Anatolia)) built a monumental temple to Ares as the city's protector, not before the 3rd century BC. It is now lost, but the names of some of its priests and priestesses survive, along with the temple's likely depictions on coins of the province.[\[44\]](#page-13-6)

### **Crete**

A sanctuary of Aphrodite was established at [Sta Lenika](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Sta_Lenika&action=edit&redlink=1), on [Crete,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crete) between the cities of [Lato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lato) and [Olus,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olus) possibly during the [Geometric period.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_period) It was rebuilt in the late 2nd century BC as a double-sanctuary to Ares and Aphrodite.[\[45\]](#page-13-7) Inscriptions record disputes over the ownership of the sanctuary. The names of Ares and Aphrodite appear as witness to sworn oaths, and there is a Victory thanks-offering to Aphrodite, whom Millington believes had capacity as a "warrior-protector acting in the realm of Ares". There were cultic links between the Sta Lenika sanctuary, Knossos and other Cretan states, and perhaps with [Argos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argos,_Peloponnese) on the mainland.[\[46\]](#page-13-8) While the Greek literary and artistic record from both the Archaic and Classical eras connects Ares and Aphrodite as complementary companions and ideal though adulterous lovers, their cult pairing and Aphrodite as warrior-protector is localised to Crete.[\[47\]](#page-13-9)[\[48\]](#page-13-10)

#### **Aksum**

In Africa, Maḥ[rem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ma%E1%B8%A5rem), the principal god of the [kings of Aksum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kings_of_Aksum) prior to the 4th century AD, was invoked as Ares in Greek inscriptions. The anonymous king who commissioned the [Monumentum Adulitanum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monumentum_Adulitanum) in the late 2nd or early 3rd century refers to "my greatest god, Ares, who also begat me, through whom I brought under my sway [various peoples]". The monumental throne celebrating the king's conquests was itself dedicated to Ares.[\[49\]](#page-13-11) In the early 4th century, the last pagan king of Aksum, [Ezana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ezana), referred to "the one who brought me forth, the invincible Ares".[\[50\]](#page-13-12)

### **Characterisation**

Ares was one of the [Twelve Olympians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_Olympians) in the archaic tradition represented by the *[Iliad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iliad)* and *[Odyssey.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odyssey)* In [Greek literature,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_literature) Ares often represents the physical or violent and untamed aspect of war and is the personification of sheer brutality and bloodlust ("overwhelming, insatiable in battle, destructive, and man-slaughtering", as Burkert puts it), in contrast to his sister, the armored [Athena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athena), whose functions as a [goddess of intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge_deity) include military strategy and generalship.[\[51\]](#page-13-13) An association with Ares endows places and objects with a savage, dangerous, or militarized quality;[\[52\]](#page-13-14) but when Ares does appear in myths, he typically faces humiliation.[\[53\]](#page-13-15)

In the *Iliad*, Zeus expresses a recurring Greek revulsion toward the god when Ares returns wounded and complaining from the [battlefield at Troy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trojan_War):

Then looking at him darkly Zeus who gathers the clouds spoke to him:

"Do not sit beside me and whine, you double-faced liar.

To me you are the most hateful of all gods who hold Olympus.

Forever quarrelling is dear to your heart, wars and battles.

...

And yet I will not long endure to see you in pain, since you are my child, and it was to me that your mother bore you. But were you born of some other god and proved so ruinous long since you would have been dropped beneath the gods of the bright sky."[\[54\]](#page-13-16)

### **Mythology**

### **Birth**

He is one of the [Twelve Olympians,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_Olympians) and the son of [Zeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeus) and [Hera.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hera) [\[57\]](#page-13-19)

#### **Argonautica**

In the *[Argonautica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argonautica)*, the *Golden Fleece* hangs in a grove sacred to Ares, until its theft by [Jason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jason_(mythology)). The Birds of Ares (*Ornithes Areioi*) drop feather darts in defense of the [Amazons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazons)' shrine to Ares, as father of their queen, on a coastal island in the [Black Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Sea). [\[58\]](#page-13-20)

#### **Founding of Thebes**

Ares plays a central role in the [founding myth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Founding_myth) of [Thebes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thebes,_Greece), as the progenitor of the water-dragon slain by [Cadmus.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cadmus) The dragon's teeth were sown into the ground as if a crop and sprang up as the fully armored [autochthonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples) [Spartoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spartoi). Cadmus placed himself in the god's service for eight years to atone for killing the dragon.[\[26\]](#page-12-16) To further propitiate Ares, Cadmus married [Harmonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonia), a daughter of Ares's union with Aphrodite. In this way, Cadmus harmonized all strife and founded the city of Thebes.[\[3\]](#page-11-2) In reality, Thebes came to dominate [Boeotia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeotia)'s great and fertile plain, which in both history and myth was a battleground for competing polities.[\[59\]](#page-13-21) According to Plutarch, the plain was anciently described as "The dancing-floor of Ares".[\[60\]](#page-13-22)

#### **Aphrodite**

In Homer's *[Odyssey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odyssey)*, in the tale sung by the bard in the hall of [Alcinous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcinous), [\[61\]](#page-13-23) the Sun-god [Helios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helios) once spied Ares and Aphrodite having sex secretly in the hall of [Hephaestus,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hephaestus) her husband.[\[62\]](#page-13-24) Helios reported the incident to Hephaestus. Contriving to catch the illicit couple in the act, Hephaestus fashioned a finely-knitted and nearly invisible net with which to snare them. At the appropriate time, this net was sprung, and trapped Ares and Aphrodite locked in very private embrace.\[n 8]

But Hephaestus was not satisfied with his revenge, so he invited the Olympian gods and goddesses to view the unfortunate pair. For the sake of modesty, the goddesses demurred, but the male gods went to witness the sight. Some commented on the beauty of Aphrodite, others remarked that they would eagerly trade places with Ares, but all who were present mocked the two. Once the couple was released, the embarrassed Ares returned to his homeland, Thrace, and Aphrodite went to Paphos.[n [8\]](#page-11-16)[\[53\]](#page-13-15) Afterwards, Hephaestus divorced Aphrodite, and she and Ares married.[\[63\]](#page-13-25)

In a much later interpolated detail, Ares put the young soldier [Alectryon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alectryon_(mythology)), who was Ares companion in drinking and even love-making, by his door to warn them of Helios's arrival as Helios would tell Hephaestus of Aphrodite's infidelity if the two were discovered, but Alectryon fell asleep on guard duty. [\[64\]](#page-13-26) Helios discovered the two and alerted Hephaestus. The furious Ares turned the sleepy Alectryon into a [rooster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rooster) which now always announces the arrival of the sun in the morning, as a way of apologizing to Ares.[\[65\]](#page-14-0)

The Chorus of [Aeschylus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeschylus)'s *[Suppliants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Suppliants_(Aeschylus))* (written 463 BC) refers to Ares as Aphrodite's "mortal-destroying bedfellow". In the *Illiad*, Ares helps the Trojans because of his affection for their divine protector, Aphrodite; she thus redirects his innate destructive savagery to her own purposes.[\[47\]](#page-13-9)[\[48\]](#page-13-10)

#### **Giants**

In one archaic myth, related only in the *Iliad* by the goddess [Dione](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dione_(Titaness)) to her daughter Aphrodite, two chthonic giants, the [Aloadae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aloadae), named Otus and Ephialtes, bound Ares in chains and imprisoned him in a bronze urn, where he remained for thirteen months, a [lunar year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_year). "And that would have been the end of Ares and his appetite for war, if the beautiful Eriboea, the young giants' stepmother, had not told [Hermes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermes) what they had done," she related.[\[66\]](#page-14-1) In this, [Burkert] suspects "a festival of licence which is unleashed in the thirteenth month".[\[67\]](#page-14-2)[\[68\]](#page-14-3) Ares was held screaming and howling in the urn until Hermes rescued him, and [Artemis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis) tricked the Aloadae into slaying each other.

In [Nonnus'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonnus)s *[Dionysiaca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dionysiaca)*, in the war between [Cronus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cronus) and Zeus, Ares killed an unnamed giant son of [Echidna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Echidna_(mythology)) who was allied with Cronus, and described as spitting "horrible poison" and having "snaky" feet.[\[69\]](#page-14-4)

In some versions of the [Gigantomachy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gigantomachy), Ares was the god who killed the giant [Mimas.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mimas_(Giant)) [\[70\]](#page-14-5)

In the 2nd century AD *Metamorphoses* of [Antoninus Liberalis,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoninus_Liberalis) when the monstrous [Typhon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typhon) attacked Olympus the gods transformed into animals and fled to Egypt; Ares changed into a fish, the Lepidotus (sacred to the Egyptian war-god [Anhur\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anhur). Liberalis's [koine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koine) Greek text is a "completely inartistic" epitome of [Nicander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicander)'s now lost *Heteroeumena* (2nd century BC).[\[71\]](#page-14-6)[\[72\]](#page-14-7)

#### *Iliad*

In [Homer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer)'s *[Iliad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iliad)*, Ares has no fixed allegiance. He promises Athena and Hera that he will fight for the [Achaeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaeans_(Homer)) but [Aphrodite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aphrodite) persuades him to side with the [Trojans.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troy) During the war, [Diomedes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diomedes) fights [Hector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hector) and sees Ares fighting on the Trojans' side. Diomedes calls for his soldiers to withdraw. [\[73\]](#page-14-8) Zeus grants Athena permission to drive Ares from the battlefield. Encouraged by Hera and Athena, Diomedes thrusts with his spear at Ares. Athena drives the spear home, and all sides tremble at Ares's cries. Ares flees to [Mount Olympus,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Olympus) forcing the Trojans to fall back.[\[74\]](#page-14-9) Ares overhears that his son Ascalaphus has been killed and wants to change sides again, rejoining the Achaeans for vengeance, disregarding Zeus's order that no Olympian should join the battle. Athena stops him. Later, when Zeus allows the gods to fight in the war again, Ares attacks Athena to avenge his previous injury. Athena overpowers him by striking him with a boulder. [\[75\]](#page-14-10)

#### **Attendants**

[Deimos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deimos_(mythology)) ("Terror" or "Dread") and [Phobos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phobos_(mythology)) ("Fear") are Ares's companions in war, [\[76\]](#page-14-11) and according to [Hesiod,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hesiod) are also his children by [Aphrodite.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aphrodite) [\[77\]](#page-14-12) [Eris,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eris_(mythology)) the goddess of discord, or [Enyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enyo), the goddess of war, bloodshed, and violence, was considered the sister and companion of the violent Ares.[\[78\]](#page-14-13) In at least one tradition, Enyalius, rather than another name for Ares, was his son by Enyo.[\[79\]](#page-14-14)

Ares may also be accompanied by [Kydoimos,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kydoimos) the daemon of the din of battle; the [Makhai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makhai) ("Battles"); the "Hysminai" ("Acts of manslaughter"); [Polemos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polemos), a minor spirit of war, or only an epithet of Ares, since it has no specific dominion; and Polemos's daughter, [Alala,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alala) the [goddess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goddess) or [personification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personification) of the Greek war-cry, whose name Ares uses as his own war-cry. Ares's sister [Hebe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebe_(mythology)) ("Youth") also draws baths for him.

According to [Pausanias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pausanias_(geographer)), local inhabitants of [Therapne,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Therapne) [Sparta,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparta) recognized [Thero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thero_(Greek_mythology)), "feral, savage", as a nurse of Ares.[\[80\]](#page-14-15)

#### **Offspring and affairs**

Though Ares plays a relatively limited role in [Greek mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mythology) as represented in literary narratives, his numerous love affairs and abundant offspring are often [alluded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allusion) to.[\[81\]](#page-14-16) The union of Ares and Aphrodite created the gods [Eros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eros), [Anteros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anteros), [Phobos,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phobos_(mythology)) [Deimos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deimos_(mythology)), and [Harmonia.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonia) Other versions include [Alcippe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcippe_(mythology)) as one of their daughters.

Ares had a romantic liaison with [Eos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eos), the [goddess of the dawn.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dawn_deities) Aphrodite discovered them, and in anger she cursed Eos with insatiable lust for men.[\[82\]](#page-14-17)

[Cycnus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycnus_(son_of_Ares)) (Κύκνος) of [Macedonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonia_(region)) was a mortal son of Ares who tried to build a temple to his father with the skulls and bones of guests and travellers. [Heracles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heracles) fought him and, in one account, killed him. In another account, Ares fought his son's killer but Zeus parted the combatants with a thunderbolt.[\[83\]](#page-14-18)

## **Mars**

The nearest counterpart of Ares among the [Roman gods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Roman_deities) is [Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_(mythology)), a son of [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter_(mythology)) and [Juno,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juno_(mythology)) pre-eminent among the [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_the_Roman_military) [army's military gods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_the_Roman_military) but originally an agricultural deity. [\[142\]](#page-16-25) As a father of [Romulus,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romulus) Rome's legendary founder, Mars was given an important and dignified place in [ancient Roman religion,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_ancient_Rome) as a [guardian deity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tutelary_deity) of the entire Roman state and its

## ![](_page_9_Picture_0.jpeg)

Wall-painting in [Pompeii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompeii), c. 20 BC – 50s AD, showing Mars and Venus. The Roman god of war is depicted as youthful and beardless, reflecting the influence of the Greek Ares.

people. Under the influence of Greek culture, Mars was identified with Ares,[143] but the character and dignity of the two deities differed fundamentally. [144][145] Mars was represented as a means to secure peace, and he was a father *(pater)* of the Roman people.[146] In one tradition, he fathered Romulus and Remus through his rape of Rhea Silvia. In another, his lover, the goddess Venus, gave birth to Aeneas, the Trojan prince and refugee who "founded" Rome several generations before Romulus.

In the [Hellenization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenization) of [Latin literature,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_literature) the myths of Ares were [reinterpreted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpretatio_graeca) by Roman writers under the name of Mars. Greek writers under [Roman rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire) also recorded [cult practices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cult_(religious_practice)) and beliefs pertaining to Mars under the name of Ares. Thus in the [classical tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_mythology) of later [Western art and literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_culture), the mythology of the two figures later became virtually indistinguishable.[\[147\]](#page-16-26)

# Renaissance and later depictions

In <u>Renaissance</u> and <u>Neoclassical</u> works of art, Ares's symbols are a spear and helmet, his animal is a dog, and his bird is the <u>vulture</u>. In literary works of these eras, Ares is replaced by the Roman <u>Mars</u>, a romantic emblem of manly valor rather than the cruel and blood-thirsty god of Greek mythology.
