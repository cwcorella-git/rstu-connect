# **Criticism of the Quran**

## Contributors to Wikimedia projects

132–167 minutes

The [Quran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quran) is viewed to be the scriptural foundation of Islam and is believed by Muslims to have been sent down by God [\(Arabic:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language) هللا , [romanized:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Arabic)Allah) and revealed to [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad) by the angel [Jibrael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C5%AB%E1%B8%A5#As_interpreted_to_refer_to_the_Archangel_Gabriel) [\(Gabriel\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriel). The **Quran** has been subject to **criticism** both in the sense of being the subject of an interdisciplinary field of study where secular, (mostly) Western scholars set aside doctrines of its divinity, perfection, unchangeability, etc. accepted by Muslim Islamic scholars;[\[1\]](#page-68-0) but also in the sense of being found fault with by those including Christian missionaries and other skeptics hoping to convert Muslims — who argue it is not divine, not perfect, and/ or not particularly morally elevated.

In critical-historical study scholars (such as [John Wansbrough,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wansbrough) [Joseph Schacht,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Schacht) [Patricia Crone,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patricia_Crone) [Michael Cook\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Cook_(historian)) seek to investigate and verify the Quran's origin, text, composition, and history[,\[1\]](#page-68-0) examining questions, puzzles, difficult text, etc. as they would non-sacred ancient texts[.\[2\]](#page-68-1) The most common criticisms concern various pre-existing sources that the Quran relies upon, internal consistency, clarity and ethical teachings.

According to Toby Lester, many Muslims find not only the religious fault-finding but also Western scholarly investigation of textual evidence "disturbing and offensive".[\[1\]](#page-68-0)

## **Historical authenticity**

## [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Criticism_of_the_Quran&action=edit§ion=1)

According to Islamic tradition, which criticism may question or contradict, the Quran followed a passage from heaven down to the angel [Gabriel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriel#Islam) (Jabreel) who revealed it in the seventh century CE over 23 years to a [Hejazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hejaz) [Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabs) [trader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merchant) and [shepherd,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shepherd) [Muhammad,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad) who became one of the Prophets of Islam[.\[3\]](#page-68-2)\[Note [1\]](#page-64-0) Muhammad shared these revelations – which brought uncompromising monotheism to humanity – with his [companions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Companions_of_the_Prophet) who wrote them down and/or memorized them[.\[5\]](#page-68-3) From these memories and scraps, a standard edition was carefully compiled and edited under the supervision of [Caliph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uthman#Compilation_of_the_Quran) [Uthman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uthman#Compilation_of_the_Quran) not long after Muhammad's death[.\[6\]](#page-68-4) Copies of this [codex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codex) or "[Mus'haf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mus%27haf)" were sent to the major centers of what was by this time a rapidly expanding empire, and all other incomplete or "imperfect" variants of the Quranic revelation were ordered by Uthman to be destroyed[.\[7\]](#page-68-5) In the next few centuries, the religion and empire of Islam solidified, and an enormous body of religious literature and laws were developed, including commentaries/exegeses ([Tafsir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tafsir)) to explain the Quran.

Thus, according to Islamic teaching, it was ensured that the wording of the Quranic text available today corresponds exactly to the literal, infallible[,\[8\]](#page-69-0) "perfect, timeless", "absolute["\[1\]](#page-68-0) unadulterated [word of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_language) revealed to Muhammad[.\[5\]](#page-68-3) That revelation in turn is identical to an eternal "mother of the book["\[Note 2\]](#page-64-1) the archetyp[e\[9\]/](#page-69-1)prototyp[e\[10\]](#page-69-2) of the Quran. In their religious and theological view, this was not created/written by God, but an attribute of Him, co-eternal and kept with Him in [heaven.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavenly_Quran) [\[11\]](#page-69-3)[\[Note 3\]](#page-65-0)

### **Muslim views of criticism**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Criticism_of_the_Quran&action=edit§ion=3)

For Muslims the contents of the Quran have been "a source of doctrine, law, poetic and spiritual inspiration, solace, zeal, knowledge, and mystical experience.["\[12\]](#page-69-4) "Millions and millions" of whom "refer to the Koran daily to explain their actions and to justify their aspirations"[,\[Note 4\]](#page-65-1) and in recent years many consider it the source of scientific knowledge.[\[13\]](#page-69-5) [\[14\]](#page-69-6) Revered by pious Muslims as "the holy of holies"[,\[15\]](#page-69-7) whose sound moves some to "tears and ecstasy"[,\[16\]](#page-69-8) it is the physical symbol of the faith[,\[12\]](#page-69-4) the text often used as a char[m\[17\]](#page-69-9) on occasions of birth, death, marriage. The traditional Muslim understanding of the Quran is not that it is simply divinely inspired, but the literal word of God[;\[18\]](#page-69-10) the last and complete message from God, from his final messenger (Muhammad)[\[19\]](#page-69-11) superseding the Old and New Testament and purified of "accretions of Judaism and Christianity"[.\[20\]](#page-69-12)[\[21\]](#page-69-13)

Muslims have developed their own Quranic studies or "Quranic sciences" ('ulum al Qur'an[\)\[22\]](#page-70-0) over the centuries[,\[23\]](#page-70-1) following the Quranic encouragement "Will they not contemplate the Quran?"[\(4:82\)](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D4%3Averse%3D82)[.\[24\]](#page-70-2) There are two types of [exegesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exegesis) to explain and interpret the Quran: [tafsir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tafsir) (literal interpretation) and [ta'wil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esoteric_interpretation_of_the_Quran) (allegorical interpretation). Other issues studied are kalimat dakhila (the investigation of the foreign origin of some Quranic terms)[;\[25\]](#page-70-3) [naskh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naskh_(tafsir)) (studying contradictory verse[s\[Note 5\]](#page-65-2) to determine which should be abrogated in favor of the other), study of "occasions of revelation" (connecting Quranic verses with "episodes of Muhammad's career based on hadith and biographies of him -- which are known as [sira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophetic_biography)), chronology of revelation[,\[22\]](#page-70-0) the division of quranic chapters [\(surahs\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surah) into ["Meccan surah"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meccan_surah) (those believed to have been revealed in [Mecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecca) before the [hijra\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hijrah) and ["Medinan surah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medinan_surah) (revealed afterward in the city of [Medina\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medina)[.\[26\]](#page-70-4) According to Seyyed Hossein Nasr, these traditional religious sciences

"provide all the answers to questions posed by modern western orientalists about the structure and text of the Koran, except, of course, those questions that issue from the rejection of the Divine Origin of the Koran and its reduction to a work by the prophet. Once the revealed nature of the Koran is rejected, then problems arise. But these are problems of orientalist that arise not from scholarship but from a certain theological and philosophical position that is usually hidden under the guise of rationality and objective scholarship. For Muslims there has never been the need to address these 'problems' ..."[\[23\]](#page-70-1)

In contrast, many of the original non-Muslim scholars of the Quran worked "in the context of an openly declared hostility" between Christianity and Islam, with an eye to debunking Islam or proselytizing against it[.\[1\]](#page-68-0) The nineteenth-century orientalist and colonial administrator [William Muir,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Muir) wrote that the Quran was one of "the most stubborn enemies of Civilisation, Liberty, and the Truth which the world has yet known.["\[27\]](#page-70-5) In the twentieth century, scholars of the early [Soviet Union\[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union)[who?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Words_to_watch#Unsupported_attributions)] working in the context of [dialectical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialectical_materialism) [materialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialectical_materialism) and fighting the ["opium of the people"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opium_of_the_people) argued that Muhammad and the first Caliphs were "mythical figures" and that "the motive force" of early Islam was "the mercantile bourgeoisie of Mecca and Medina" and "slave-owning" Arab society[.\[28\]](#page-70-6)

At least in part in reaction, some Muslim opposition to "The Orientalist enterprise of Qur'anic studies" has been intense.[\[1\]](#page-68-0) In 1987 Muslim critic S. Parvez Manzoor, denounced it as conceived in "the polemical marshes of medieval Christianity".

At the greatest hour of his worldly-triumph, the Western man, coordinating the powers of the State, Church and Academia, launched his most determined assault on the citadel of Muslim faith. All the aberrant streaks of his arrogant personality—its reckless rationalism, its world-domineering phantasy and its sectarian fanaticism—joined in an unholy conspiracy to dislodge the Muslim Scripture from its firmly entrenched position as the epitome of historic authenticity and moral unassailability[.\[29\]](#page-70-7)

In the twenty-first century, some Muslim Islamic scholars have warned against lending "legitimacy to non-Muslim scholars'

understanding about Islam" by engaging with them, and that even a rigorously scholarly academic work on Islam such as the [Brill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brill_Publishers) [Encyclopedia of Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclopedia_of_Islam) "is filled with insults and disparaging remarks about the Qur'an"[.\[30\]](#page-70-8)

Textual criticism of the Quran, the structure and style of the [surahs,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surah) has been opposed on grounds that it questions the divine origin of the Quran[.\[3\]](#page-68-2) Seyyed [Hossein Nasr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hossein_Nasr) has denounced the "rationalist and agnostic methods of higher criticism" as similar to dissecting and subjecting [Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus) to "modern medical techniques" to determine whether he was born miraculously or was the son of [Joseph.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Joseph) [\[31\]](#page-70-9)[\[13\]](#page-69-5)[\[1\]](#page-68-0) In his influential [Orientalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientalism_(book)), [Edward Said](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Said) declared Western study of the Middle East — including the religion of Islam — inextricably tied to Western [Imperialism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperialism) making the study inherently political and servile to [power.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_(social_and_political)) [\[32\]](#page-70-10)

These complaints have been compared to those of other religious conservatives (Christian) against textual historical criticism of their own sacred text (the Bible)[.\[Note 6\]](#page-65-3) Non-Muslim scholar [Patricia Crone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patricia_Crone) acknowledges the call for humility towards the sacred of other cultures — "who are you to tamper with their legacy?" — but defends the challenging of orthodox views of Islamic history, saying "we Islamicists are not trying to destroy anyone's faith.["\[1\]](#page-68-0)

Not all Muslims oppose criticism; Roslan Abdul-Rahim writes that critical study of the Quran "will not hurt the Muslims; it will only help them" because "no amount of criticism can change that fact" that the "Quran is truly a divine piece of work as the

Muslim theology stipulates and as the Muslims have so strongly defended"[.\[33\]](#page-71-0) Some scholars have suffered for attempting to apply literary or philological techniques to the Quran, such as Egyptian "Dean of Arabic Literature" [Taha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taha_Husain) [Husain,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taha_Husain) who lost his post at Cairo University in 1931,[\[Note 7\]](#page-65-4) Egyptian professor Mohammad Ahmad Khalafallah, whose dissertation was rejected[,\[37\]](#page-71-1)[\[38\]](#page-71-2) a non-Muslim German professor [Günter Lüling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%BCnter_L%C3%BCling) (dismissed)[,\[39\]](#page-71-3)[\[38\]](#page-71-2) and Egyptian professor [Nasr Abu Zaid,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasr_Abu_Zaid) who was forced to seek exile in Europe after being declared an [apostate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostasy_in_Islam#Execution) and [threatened with](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasr_Abu_Zayd#Death_threats) [death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasr_Abu_Zayd#Death_threats) for violating a "right of God"[.\[Note 8\]](#page-65-5)

Not all non-Muslim scholars of Islam are interested in critical examination/analysis. Patricia Crone and Ibn Rawandi argue that Western scholarship lost its critical attitude to the sources of the origins of Islam around the time of the First World War." Andrew Rippin has expressed surprise that for students acquainted with approaches such as [source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Source_criticism) [criticism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Source_criticism) [oral-formulaic composition,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral-formulaic_composition) literary analysis and [structuralism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structuralism) all quite commonly employed in the study of Judaism and Christianity, such naive historical study seems to suggest that Islam is being approached with less than academic candor[.\[42\]](#page-71-4)

Scholars have complained about "'dogmatic Islamophilia' of most Arabists" (Karl Binswanger)[;\[43\]](#page-71-5) that in one western country (France as of 1983) "it is no longer acceptable to criticize Islam or the Arab countries" (Jacque Ellul);[\[44\]](#page-71-6) that among some historians ("like Norman Daniel") understanding of Islam "has given way to apologetics pure and simple" [\(Maxime Rodinson\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxime_Rodinson)[.\[45\]](#page-72-0)[\[46\]](#page-72-1)

However, in the 1970s, what has been described as a "wave of skeptical scholars" challenged a great deal of the received wisdom in Islamic studies[.\[47\]:](#page-72-2)23 They argued that the Islamic historical tradition had been greatly corrupted in transmission, that there was a lack of supporting evidence consistent with the traditional narrative, such as the lack of archaeological evidence, and discrepancies with non-Muslim literary sources. [\[48\]](#page-72-3) They tried to correct or reconstruct the early history of Islam from other, presumably more reliable, sources such as coins, inscriptions, and non-Islamic sources.

Although there is some disagreement[,\[Note 9\]](#page-65-6) the collection of verses for the compilation of a written Quran is said to have begun under Caliph [Abu Bakr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Bakr) [\[Note 10\]](#page-66-0) The last recensions to make an official and uniform Quran in a single dialect were effected under [Caliph Uthman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uthman#Reign_as_Caliph_(644%E2%80%93656)) (644–656) starting some twelve years after the Prophet's death and finishing twenty-four years after the effort began, with all other existing personal and individual copies and dialects of the Quran being burned:

When they had copied the sheets, Uthman sent a copy to each of the main centres of the empire with the command that all other Qur'an materials, whether in single sheet form, or in whole volumes, were to be burned[.\[52\]](#page-72-4)

It is traditionally believed the earliest writings had the advantage of being checked by people who already knew the text by heart, for they had learned it at the time of the revelation itself and had subsequently recited it constantly. Since the official compilation was completed two decades after Muhammad's death, the Uthman text has been scrupulously preserved. Bucaille believed that this did not give rise to any problems of the Quran's authenticity[.\[53\]](#page-73-0)

Evolution of early [Arabic script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_script) (9th–11th century), with the [Basmala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basmala) as an example, from [kufic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kufic) [Qur'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qur%27an)ān manuscripts: (1) Early 9th century, script with no dots or diacritic marks;(2) and (3) 9th–10th century under the Abbasid dynasty, [Abu al-Aswad's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_al-Aswad_al-Du%27ali) system established red dots with each arrangement or position indicating a different short vowel; later, a second black-dot system was used to differentiate between letters like fā' and qāf; (4) 11th century, in [al-Far](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Khalil_ibn_Ahmad_al-Farahidi)āhidi's system (system used today) dots were changed into shapes resembling the letters to transcribe the corresponding long vowels.

Despite [caliph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliph) [Uthman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uthman)'s reported work to standardize the Quran, and the belief by many Muslims that it "exists exactly as it had been revealed to the [Prophet;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad) not a word - nay, not a dot of it - has been changed" [\(Abul A'la Maududi\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abul_A%27la_Maududi)[,\[54\]](#page-73-1) there are not one but ten different recognized versions of recitations of the Quran, known as qira'at (meaning 'recitations or readings'). [\[Note 11\]](#page-66-1) These exist because the Quran was originally spread and passed down orally, and though there was a written text, it did not include most vowels or distinguish between many consonants[.\[55\]](#page-73-2) [\[Note 12\]](#page-66-2) Consequently, although the differences between the Qira'at are slight and only one version of the ten is in wide use[,\[Note 13\]](#page-66-3) the differences between the "readings" go beyond pronunciation into consonants and meaning[.\[55\]](#page-73-2)

In addition to the Qira'at there are also [Ahruf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahruf)—both being readings of the Quran with "unbroken chain(s) of transmission going back to the Prophet"[,\[58\]](#page-73-3) but all but one ahruf allegedly being forgotten after Uthman standardized the Quran[.\[59\]](#page-73-4) There are multiple views on the nature of the ahruf and how they relate to the qira'at, the general view being that [caliph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliph) [Uthman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uthman) eliminated all of the ahruf except one during the 7th century CE[.\[60\]](#page-73-5) The ten qira'at were canonized by Islamic scholars in early centuries of Islam[.\[61\]](#page-73-6) Prior to this period, there is evidence that the unpointed text could be read in different ways, with different meanings.

Even after centuries of Islamic scholarship, the variants of the Qira'at have been said to continue "to astound and puzzle" Islamic scholars (Ammar Khatib and Nazir Khan),[\[58\]](#page-73-3) and make up "the most difficult topics" in Quranic studies (according to [Abu Ammaar Yasir Qadhi\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Ammaar_Yasir_Qadhi)[.\[62\]](#page-74-0) While in theory Qira'at include differences in consonantal diacritics (i'jā[m](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_diacritics#I)), vowel marks (ḥ[arak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E1%B8%A4arak%C4%81t)āt), but not the consonantal skeleton ([rasm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rasm)) which should be uniform in all Qira'at, there are differences in ([rasm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rasm)), [\[63\]](#page-74-1) resulting in materially different readings (see [examples\)](about:reader?url=https%3A%2F%2Fen.wikipedia.org%2Fwiki%2FCriticism_of_the_Quran#Examples_of_differences_between_readings).

#### **Extant copies prior to Uthman version**

### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Criticism_of_the_Quran&action=edit§ion=7)

## ![](_page_12_Picture_5.jpeg)

[Manuscripts found in Sana'a.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sana%27a_manuscripts) The "subtexts" revealed using UV light are very different from today's Qur'an. [Gerd R. Puin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerd_R._Puin) believed this to mean an '**evolving'** text[.\[72\]](#page-75-6) A similar phrase is used by [Lawrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Conrad) [Conrad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Conrad) for [biography of Muhammad.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biography_of_Muhammad) Because, according to his studies, Islamic scientific view on the date of birth of the Prophet until the second century A. H. had exhibited a diversity of 85 years.[\[73\]](#page-75-7)

In 1972, a cache of 12,000 ancient Quranic parchment fragments was discovered in a mosque in [Sana'a,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sana%27a) Yemen – commonly known as the [Sana'a manuscripts.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sana%27a_manuscripts) Of the fragments, all except 1500–2000 were assigned to 926 distinct Quranic manuscripts as of 1997.

The manuscript is a [palimpsest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palimpsest) and comprises two layers of text, both of which are written in the [Hijazi script.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hijazi_script) The upper text largely conforms to the standard ['Uthmanic' Quran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdullah_ibn_Masud#Uthman) in text and in the standard order of chapters ([suwar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suwar), singular sū[rah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C5%ABrah)), whereas the lower text (the original text that was erased and written over by the upper text, but can still be read with the help of ultraviolet light and computer processing) contains many variations from the standard Uthmanic text, and the sequence of its chapters corresponds to no known Quranic order.

For example, in sura 2, verse 87, the lower text has waqaffaynā 'alā āthārihi whereas the standard text has waqaffaynā min ba'dihi. The Sana'a manuscript has exactly the same verses and the same order of verses as the standard Quran[.\[74\]](#page-75-8) The order of the suras in the Sana'a codex is different from the order in the standard Quran[.\[75\]](#page-75-9) Such variants are similar to the ones reported for the Quran codices of Companions such as [Ibn Masud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Masud) and [Ubay ibn Ka'b.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ubay_ibn_Ka%27b) However, variants occur much more frequently in the Sana'a codex, which contains "by a rough estimate perhaps twenty-five times as many [as Ibn Mas'ud's reported variants]".[\[76\]](#page-76-0)

On the basis of studies of the trove of Quranic manuscripts discovered in Sana'a, [Gerd R. Puin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerd_R._Puin) concluded that the Quran as we have it is a 'cocktail of texts', some perhaps preceding Muhammad's day, and that the text as we have it evolved.[\[77\]](#page-76-1) However, other scholars, such as Asma Hilali presumed that the San'aa palimpsest seems to be written down by a [learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanaa_manuscript#Hilali) [scribe as a form of "exercise"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanaa_manuscript#Hilali) in the context of a "school exercise", which explains a potential reason of variations in this text from the standard Quran Mushafs available today.[\[78\]](#page-76-2) Another way to explain these variations is that San'aa manuscript may have been part of [a surviving copy of Quranic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanaa_manuscript#Relation_of_the_lower_text_to_other_non-) [Mus'haf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mus%27haf) which escaped the 3rd caliph [Uthman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uthman)'s attempt to destroy all the dialects [\(Ahruf\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahruf) of Quran except the Quraishi one (in order to unite the Muslims of that time).

#### **Birmingham/Paris manuscript**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Criticism_of_the_Quran&action=edit§ion=9)

The early Arabic script transcribed 28 consonants, of which only 6 can be readily distinguished, the remaining 22 having formal similarities which means that what specific consonant is intended can only be determined by context. It was only with the introduction of [Arabic diacritics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_diacritics) some centuries later, that an authorized vocalization of the text, and how it was to be read, was established and became canonical.[\[79\]](#page-76-3) In 2015, the [University of Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Birmingham) disclosed that scientific tests may show [a Quran manuscript in its collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_Quran_manuscript) as one of the oldest known and believe it was written close to the time of Muhammad. The findings in 2015 of the Birmingham Manuscripts lead Joseph E. B. Lumbard, Assistant Professor of Classical Islam, Brandeis University, to comment:[\[80\]](#page-76-4)

These recent empirical findings are of fundamental importance. They establish that as regards the broad outlines of the history of the compilation and codification of the Quranic text, the classical Islamic sources are far more reliable than had hitherto been assumed. Such findings thus render the vast majority of Western revisionist theories regarding the historical origins of the Quran untenable.

Tests by the Oxford Radiocarbon Accelerator Unit indicated with a probability of more than 94 percent that the parchment dated from 568 to 645[.\[81\]](#page-76-5) Dr Saud al-Sarhan, Director of [Center for Research and Islamic Studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Faisal_Foundation#King_Faisal_Center_for_Research_and_Islamic_Studies) in [Riyadh,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riyadh) questions whether the parchment might have been reused as a [palimpsest,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palimpsest) and also noted that the writing had chapter separators and dotted verse endings – features in [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_script) [scripts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_script) which are believed not to have been introduced to the Quran until later[.\[81\]](#page-76-5) Al-Sarhan's criticisms were supported by several Saudi-based experts in Quranic history, who said that the Birmingham/Paris Quran could not have been written during the lifetime of Muhammad. They said that while Muhammad was alive, Quranic texts were written without chapter decoration, marked verse endings or use of coloured inks; and did not follow any standard sequence of surahs. They said that those features were introduced into Quranic practice in the time of the Caliph Uthman, and so the Birmingham leaves could have been written later, but not earlier.[\[82\]](#page-76-6)

Professor Süleyman Berk of the faculty of Islamic studies at [Yalova University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yalova_University) said there is a strong similarity between the script of the Birmingham leaves and those of a number of Hijazi Qurans in the [Turkish and Islamic Arts Museum,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_and_Islamic_Arts_Museum) which were brought to Istanbul from the [Great Mosque of Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_Mosque) following a fire in 1893. Berk said that these manuscripts had been intensively researched in association with an exhibition on the history of the Quran, The Quran in its 1,400th Year held in Istanbul in 2010, and the findings published by François Déroche as Qur'ans of the Umayyads in 2013[.\[83\]](#page-76-7) In that study, the Paris Quran, [BnF Arabe 328\(c\),](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BnF_Arabe_328(c)) is compared with Qurans in Istanbul, and concluded as having been written "around the end of the seventh century and the beginning of the eighth century.["\[84\]](#page-76-8)

In December 2015 [François Déroche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois_D%C3%A9roche) of the [Collège de France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coll%C3%A8ge_de_France) said the two Birmingham leaves were those of the Paris Qur'an BnF Arabe 328(c), as had been proposed by Alba Fedeli. Deroche expressed reservations about the reliability of the radiocarbon dates proposed for the Birmingham leaves, noting instances elsewhere in which radiocarbon dating had proved inaccurate in testing Qurans with an explicit endowment date; and also that none of the counterpart Paris leaves had yet been carbon-dated. [Jamal bin Huwaireb,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamal_bin_Huwaireb) managing director of the Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum Foundation, has proposed that, were the radiocarbon dates to be confirmed, the Birmingham/Paris Qur'an might be identified with the text known to have been assembled by the first [Caliph,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliph) [Abu Bakr,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Bakr) between 632 and 634 CE[.\[85\]](#page-76-9)

#### **Further research and findings**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Criticism_of_the_Quran&action=edit§ion=10)

Critical research of historic events and timeliness of eyewitness accounts reveal the effort of later traditionalists to consciously promote, for nationalistic purposes, the centrist concept of Mecca and prophetic descent from Ismail, in order to grant a [Hijazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hejaz) orientation to the emerging religious identity of Islam:

For, our attempt to date the relevant traditional material confirms on the whole the conclusions which [Schacht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Schacht) arrived at from another field, specifically the tendency of [isnads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadith_studies#Sanad_and_matn) to grow backwards[.\[86\]](#page-76-10)

In their book 1977 [Hagarism: The Making of the Islamic World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hagarism:_The_Making_of_the_Islamic_World), written before [more recent discoveries of early Quranic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Quranic_manuscripts) [material,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Quranic_manuscripts) [Patricia Crone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patricia_Crone) and [Michael Cook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Cook_(historian)) challenge the traditional account of how the Quran was compiled, writing that "there is no hard evidence for the existence of the Koran in any form before the last decade of the seventh century."[\[87\]](#page-77-0)[\[77\]](#page-76-1) Crone, Wansbrough, and Nevo argued, that all the primary sources which exist are from 150 to 300 years after the events which they describe, and thus are chronologically far removed from those events[.\[88\]](#page-77-1)[\[89\]](#page-77-2)[\[90\]](#page-77-3)

![](_page_17_Picture_3.jpeg)

Quran from the 9th century. It was alleged to be a 7th-century original from [Uthman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uthman) era.

It is generally acknowledged that the work of Crone and Cook was a fresh approach in its reconstruction of early [Islamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_history) [history,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_history) but the theory has been almost universally rejected. [\[91\]](#page-77-4) Van Ess has dismissed it stating that "a refutation is perhaps unnecessary since the authors make no effort to prove it in detail ... Where they are only giving a new interpretation of well-known facts, this is not decisive. But where the accepted facts are consciously put upside down, their approach is disastrous.["\[92\]](#page-77-5) R. B. Serjeant states that "[Crone and Cook's thesis]... is not only bitterly anti-Islamic in tone, but anti-Arabian. Its superficial fancies are so ridiculous that at first one wonders if it is just a 'leg pull', pure 'spoof'."[\[93\]](#page-77-6) [Francis Edward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Edward_Peters) [Peters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Edward_Peters) states that "Few have failed to be convinced that what is in our copy of the Quran is, in fact, what Muhammad taught, and is expressed in his own words"[.\[94\]](#page-77-7)

In 2006, legal scholar Liaquat Ali Khan claimed that Crone and Cook later explicitly disavowed their earlier book.[\[95\]](#page-77-8)[\[96\]](#page-77-9) Patricia Crone in an article published in 2006 provided an update on the evolution of her conceptions since the printing of the thesis in 1976. In the article she acknowledges that Muhammad existed as a historical figure and that the Quran represents "utterances" of his that he believed to be revelations. However she states that the Quran may not be the complete record of the revelations. She also accepts that oral histories and Muslim historical accounts cannot be totally discounted, but remains skeptical about the traditional account of the [Hijrah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hijra_(Islam)) and the standard view that Muhammad and his tribe were based in Mecca. She describes the difficulty in the handling of the [hadith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadith) because of their "amorphous nature" and purpose as documentary evidence for deriving religious law rather than as historical narratives[.\[97\]](#page-77-10)

The author of the [Apology of al-Kindy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apology_of_al-Kindy) Abd al-Masih ibn Ishaq al-Kindi (not the famed philosopher [al-Kindi\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Kindi) claimed that the narratives in the Quran were "all jumbled together and intermingled" and that this was "an evidence that many different hands have been at work therein, and caused discrepancies, adding or cutting out whatever they liked or disliked"[.\[98\]](#page-78-0) Bell and Watt suggested that the variation in writing style throughout the Quran, which sometimes involves the use of rhyming, may have indicated revisions to the text during its compilation. They claimed that there were "abrupt changes in the length of verses; sudden changes of the dramatic situation, with changes of pronoun from singular to plural, from second to third person, and so on".[\[99\]](#page-78-1) At the same time, however, they noted that "[i]f any great changes by way of addition, suppression or alteration had been made, controversy would almost certainly have arisen; but of that there is little trace." They also note that "Modern study of the Quran has not in fact raised any serious question of its authenticity. The style varies, but is almost unmistakable."[\[100\]](#page-78-2)

## **Questions about the text**

### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Criticism_of_the_Quran&action=edit§ion=12)

The Quran itself states that its revelations are themselves "miraculous 'signs'["\[8\]—](#page-69-0)inimitable ([I'jaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I%27jaz)) in their eloquence and perfectio[n\[101\]](#page-78-3) and proof of the authenticity of Muhammad's prophethood. (For example [2:2,](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D2%3Averse%3D2) [17:88-89,](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D17%3Averse%3D88-89) [29:47,](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D29%3Averse%3D47) [28:49\)](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D28%3Averse%3D49) \[Note [14\]](#page-66-4) Several verses remark on how the verses of the book set clear or make things clear[,\[Note 15\]](#page-67-0) and are in "pure and clear" [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic) language [\[Note 16\]](#page-67-1) At the same time, (most Muslims believe) some verses of the Quran have been abrogated ([naskh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naskh_(tafsir))) by others and these and other verses have sometimes been revealed in response or answer to questions by followers or opponents[.\[11\]](#page-69-3)[\[103\]](#page-78-4)[\[104\]](#page-78-5)

Not all early Muslims agreed with this consensus. Muslimturned-skeptic [Ibn al-Rawandi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_al-Rawandi) (d.911) dismissed the Quran as "not the speech of someone with wisdom, contain[ing] contradictions, errors and absurdities"[.\[105\]](#page-78-6) In response to claims that the Quran is a miracle, 10th-century physician and polymath [Muhammad ibn Zakariya al-Razi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_ibn_Zakariya_al-Razi) wrote (according to his opponent [Abu Hatim Ahmad ibn Hamdan al-Razi\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Hatim_Ahmad_ibn_Hamdan_al-Razi),

You claim that the evidentiary miracle is present and available, namely, the Koran. You say: "Whoever denies it, let him produce a similar one." Indeed, we shall produce a thousand similar, from the works of rhetoricians, eloquent speakers and valiant poets, which are more appropriately phrased and state the issues more succinctly. They convey the meaning better and their rhymed prose is in better meter. ... By God what you say astonishes us! You are talking about a work which recounts ancient myths, and which at the same time is full of contradictions and does not contain any useful information or explanation. Then you say: "Produce something like it"?![\[106\]](#page-78-7) [\[107\]](#page-78-8)

Early Western scholars also often attacked the literary merit of the Quran. Orientalist [Thomas Carlyle,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Carlyle) [\[Note 17\]](#page-67-2) called the Quran "toilsome reading and a wearisome confused jumble, crude, incondite" with "endless iterations, long-windedness, entanglement" and "insupportable stupidity"[.\[109\]](#page-78-9) [Salomon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salomon_Reinach) [Reinach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salomon_Reinach) wrote that this book warrants "little merit ... from a literary point of view"[.\[Note 18\]](#page-67-3)

More specifically, "peculiarities" in the text have been alleged.

[\[111\]](#page-79-0) Iranian rationalist and scholar [Ali Dashti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_Dashti) points out that before its perfection became an issue of Islamic doctrine, early Muslim scholar Ibrahim an-Nazzam "openly acknowledged that the arrangement and syntax" of the Quran was less than "miraculous"[.\[112\]](#page-79-1)

Ali Dashti states that "more than one hundred" aberrations from "the normal rules and structure of Arabic have been noted" in the Quran[.\[113\]](#page-79-2)

sentences which are incomplete and not fully intelligible without the aid or commentaries; foreign words, unfamiliar Arabic words, and words used with other than the normal meaning; adjectives and verbs inflected without observance of the concords of gender and number; illogically and ungrammatically applied pronouns which sometimes have no referent; and predicates which in rhymed passages are often remote from the subjects[.\[114\]](#page-79-3)

Scholar [Gerd R. Puin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerd_R._Puin) puts the number of unclear verses much higher:

The Koran claims for itself that it is 'mubeen,' or 'clear,' but if you look at it, you will notice that every fifth sentence or so simply doesn't make sense. Many Muslims—and Orientalists will tell you otherwise, of course, but the fact is that a fifth of the Koranic text is just incomprehensible. This is what has caused the traditional anxiety regarding translation. If the Koran is not comprehensible—if it can't even be understood in Arabic—then it's not translatable. People fear that. And since the Koran claims repeatedly to be clear but obviously is not—

as even speakers of Arabic will tell you—there is a contradiction. Something else must be going on.[\[1\]](#page-68-0)

Scholar of the Semitic languages Theodor Noldeke collected a large quantity of morphological and syntactic grammatical forms in the Qura[n\[115\]](#page-79-4) that "do not enter into the general linguistic system of Arabic"[.\[116\]](#page-79-5) Alan Dundes points out the Quran itself denies that there can be errors within it, "If it were from other than Allah, they would surely have found in it many contradictions". (Q.4:82[\)\[117\]](#page-79-6)

According to the Muslim translator and expositor [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Asad) [Asad:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Asad)

"The significance of these letter-symbols has perplexed the commentators from the earliest times. There is no evidence of the Prophet's having ever referred to them in any of his recorded utterances, nor any of his [Companions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Companions_of_the_Prophet) having ever asked him for an explanation. None the less, it is established beyond any possibility of doubt that all the Companions obviously following the example of the Prophet - regarded the muqatta'at as integral parts of the suras to which they are prefixed, and used to recite them accordingly: a fact which disposes effectively of the suggestion advanced by some Western orientalists that these letters may be no more than the initials of the scribes who wrote down the individual revelations at the Prophet's dictation, or of the Companions who recorded them at the time of the final codification of the Qur'an during the reign of the first three Caliphs.

#### **Narrative voice: Mohammed or God as speakers**

## [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Criticism_of_the_Quran&action=edit§ion=17)

Since the Quran is God's revelation to humanity, critics have wondered why in many verses, God is being addressed by humans, instead of Him addressing human beings. Or as scholars [Richard Bell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Bell_(Arabist)) and [W. Montgomery Watt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._Montgomery_Watt) point out, while it is not unheard of for someone (especially someone very powerful) to speak of himself in the third person, "the extent to which we find the Prophet apparently being addressed and told about God as a third person, is unusual", as is where "God is made to swear by himself"[.\[68\]\)](#page-75-2)

Folklorist Alan Dundes notes how one "formula" or phrase ("... acquit thou/you/them/him of us/your/their/his evil deeds") is repeated with a variety of voices both divine and human, singular and plural:

- `Our Lord, forgive Thou our sins and acquit us of our evil deeds` [3:193;](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D3%3Averse%3D193)

- `We will acquit you of your evil deeds`, [4:31;](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D4%3Averse%3D31)
- `I will acquit you of your evil deeds`, [5:12;](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D5%3Averse%3D12)
- `He will acquit them of their evil deeds`, [47:2;](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D47%3Averse%3D2)
- `Allah will acquit him of his evil deeds`, [64:9;](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D64%3Averse%3D9) [\[69\]](#page-75-3)

The point-of-view of God changes from third person ("He" and "His" in Exalted is He who took His Servant by night from al-Masjid al-Haram to al-Masjid al- Aqsa), to first person ("We" and "Our" in We have blessed, to show him of Our signs), and back again to third ("He" in Indeed, He is the Hearing) all in the same verse. (In Arabic there is no capitalization to indicate divinity.) Q.33:37 also starts by referring to God in the third person, is followed by a sentence with God speaking in first person (we gave her in marriage ...) before returning to third person (and God's commandment must be performed)[.\[70\]](#page-75-4) Again in [48:1](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D48%3Averse%3D1) [48:2](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D48%3Averse%3D2) God is both first (We) and third person (God, His) within one sentence[.\[136\]](#page-81-0)

The [Jewish Encyclopedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_Encyclopedia), for example, writes: "For example, critics note that a sentence in which something is said concerning Allah is sometimes followed immediately by another in which Allah is the speaker (examples of this are Q.16.81, 27:61, 31:9, 43:10) Many peculiarities in the positions of words are due to the necessities of rhyme (lxix. 31, lxxiv. 3).["\[111\]](#page-79-0) The verse [6:114](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D6%3Averse%3D114) starts out with Muhammad talking in first person (I) and switches to third (you).

• [6:114](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D6%3Averse%3D114) Shall I seek other than Allah for judge, when He it is Who hath revealed unto you [this] Scripture, fully explained? Those unto whom We gave the Scripture [aforetime] know that it is revealed from thy Lord in truth. So be not thou [O Muhammad] of the waverers.

While some (Muhammad Abdel Haleem) have argued that "such grammatical shifts are a traditional aspect of Arabic rhetorical style"[,\[Note 20\]](#page-67-5) [Ali Dashti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_Dashti) (also quoted by critic [Ibn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Warraq) [Warraq\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Warraq) notes that in many verses "the speaker cannot have been God". The opening surah [Al-Fatiha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Fatiha)[\[140\]](#page-81-1) which contains such lines as

Praise to God, the Lord of the Worlds, ... You [alone] we worship and from You [alone] we seek help. ...

is "clearly addressed to God, in the form of a prayer."[\[141\]](#page-81-2)[\[140\]](#page-81-1) [\[142\]](#page-81-3) Other verses (the beginning of [27:91,](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D27%3Averse%3D91) "I have been commanded to serve the Lord of this city ..."; [19:64,](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D19%3Averse%3D64) "We come not down save by commandment of thy Lord") also makes no sense as a statement of an all-powerful God.

(qul) -- but it does not occur in [Al-Fatiha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Fatiha) and many other similar verses. [Al-Suyuti,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Suyuti) the noted medieval philologist and commentator of the Quran thought five verses had questionable "attribution to God" and were likely spoken by either Muhammad or Gabriel. [\[140\]](#page-81-1)

## **Similarities with Jewish and Christian Narratives**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Criticism_of_the_Quran&action=edit§ion=20)

In dealing with the question of the origins of the Quran, non-Muslim historians have often focused on Christian and Jewish sources.

The [Quran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quran) contains references to [more than fifty people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_people_in_both_the_Bible_and_the_Quran) and events also found in the [Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible) (including [Adam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam) and [Eve,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eve) [Cain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cain_and_Abel) [and Abel,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cain_and_Abel) [Noah,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noah) [Abraham,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham) [Joseph,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_(Genesis)) [Lot,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lot_(biblical_person)) [Moses,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses) [Saul,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saul) David and [Goliath,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goliath) Jonah, Jesus, Mary). [Moses,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses_in_Islam) is mentioned 135 time[s\[155\]](#page-82-7)[\[156\]](#page-82-8) Moses is mentioned in 502 verses in 36 surahs, [\[157\]](#page-82-9) Abraham in 245 verses, Noah in 131[.\[158\]](#page-83-0)

Legends, [parables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parable) or pieces of [folklore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folklore) that appear in the Quran, with similar motifs to [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaism) traditions include [Cain and](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cain_and_Abel) [Abel,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cain_and_Abel) Abraham destroying idols, [Solomon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomon) conversing with a talking ant. [Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity) traditions include the [Seven Sleepers,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Sleepers) the naming of [Mary, mother of Jesus,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary,_mother_of_Jesus) the selection of Mary's guardian by lottery, how a palm tree obeyed the commands of the child Jesus.

The Quran and Bible differ on a number of narrative and theological issues. There is no [original sin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Original_sin) in the Quran; it specifically and repeatedly denies the Christian [Trinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity) of three persons in one God, and denies that Jesus is the son of God (9:30), was crucified (4:157) and died, or rose from the dead. It holds that the Holy Spirit is actually the angel Gabriel (2:97; 16:102). The Devil, Satan (Shaitan), is regarded as a jinn not a fallen angel in most contemporary scholarship [159] (2:34; 7:12; 15:27; 55:15). [160]

Muslims believe the Quran refers to figures, prophets, and events in the Hebrew Bible and the Christian New Testament because these books are predecessors of the Quran, also revealed by the one true omnipotent God. The differences between these books and the Quran can be explained (Muslims believed) by the flawed processes of transmission and interpretation, distorting revelation that the Quran provides free from any distortions and corruptions.

Non-Muslim historians – secular but also Jewish and Christian – in keeping with Occam's razor, have looked for simpler, non-divine/non-supernatural explanations for the connection [Note 21] (In Islamic language, dealing only with *shahada*, i.e. what can be perceived, described, and studied; and not with the unseen *al-Ghaib*, made known only by divine revelation). Many stories of Muhammad hearing about Christianity from Christians and Judaism from Jews come from Muslim sources.

Western academic scholars who have studied "the relationship between the Quran and the Judeo-Christian scriptural tradition" [162] include Abraham Geiger, [163] Tor Andræ, [164]

Richard Bell, [165] and Charles Cutler Torrey. [166]

## **Influence of heretical Christian sects**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Criticism_of_the_Quran&action=edit§ion=21)

The Quran maintains that Jesus was not actually crucified and did not die on the cross. The general Islamic view supporting the denial of crucifixion may have been influenced by [Manichaeism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manichaeism) [\(Docetism\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Docetism), which holds that someone else was crucified instead of Jesus, while concluding that Jesus will return during the end times[.\[187\]](#page-85-5) However, the general consensus is that Manichaeism was not prevalent in Mecca during the 6th and 7th centuries when Islam developed.[\[188\]](#page-85-6) [\[189\]](#page-85-7)[\[190\]](#page-85-8)

That they said (in boast), "We killed [Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ) Jesus the son of [Mary,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_(mother_of_Jesus)) the [Messenger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messengers_of_Islam) of [Allah"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allah);- but they killed him not, nor crucified him, but so it was made to appear to them, and those who differ therein are full of doubts, with no (certain) knowledge, but only conjecture to follow, for of a surety they killed him not:-

Nay, Allah raised him up unto Himself; and Allah is Exalted in Power, Wise;-

Despite these views and no eyewitness accounts, most modern scholars have maintained that the [Crucifixion of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crucifixion_of_Jesus) is indisputable[.\[192\]](#page-85-9)

The view that Jesus only appeared to be crucified and did not actually die predates Islam, and is found in several apocryphal gospels[.\[187\]](#page-85-5)

[Irenaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irenaeus) in his book Against Heresies describes [Gnostic beliefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnostic_beliefs) that bear remarkable resemblance with the Islamic view:

He did not himself suffer death, but Simon, a certain man of Cyrene, being compelled, bore the cross in his stead; so that this latter being transfigured by him, that he might be thought to be Jesus, was crucified, through ignorance and error, while Jesus himself received the form of Simon, and, standing by, laughed at them. For since he was an incorporeal power, and the Nous (mind) of the unborn father, he transfigured himself as he pleased, and thus ascended to him who had sent him, deriding them, inasmuch as he could not be laid hold of, and was invisible to all.-

A Gnostic writing, found in the Nag Hammadi library, [Second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Treatise_of_the_Great_Seth) [Treatise of the Great Seth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Treatise_of_the_Great_Seth) has a similar view of Jesus' death:

I was not afflicted at all, yet I did not die in solid reality but in what appears, in order that I not be put to shame by them and also:

Another, their father, was the one who drank the gall and the vinegar; it was not I. Another was the one who lifted up the cross on his shoulder, who was Simon. Another was the one on whom they put the crown of thorns. But I was rejoicing in the height over all the riches of the archons and the offspring of their error and their conceit, and I was laughing at their ignorance

The [Collyridians,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collyridianism) an early Christian heretical sect in [pre-Islamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Islamic_Arabia) [Arabia,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Islamic_Arabia) whose adherents apparently worshipped [Mary, mother](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary,_mother_of_Jesus) [of Jesus,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary,_mother_of_Jesus) as a [goddess,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goddess) [\[193\]](#page-86-0)[\[194\]](#page-86-1) have garnered interest in some recent Christian–Muslim religious discussions in reference to the [Islamic concept of the Christian Trinity.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_in_Islam) The debate hinges on some verses in the [Qur'an,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qur%27an) primarily [5:73,](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D5%3Averse%3D73) [5:75,](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D5%3Averse%3D75) and [5:116](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D5%3Averse%3D116) in the [sura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sura) [Al-Ma'ida,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ma%27ida) which have been taken to imply that Muhammad believed Christians considered Mary to be part of the Trinity. That idea has never been part of mainstream Christian doctrine and is not clearly and unambiguously attested among any ancient Christian group, including the Collyridians.

#### **Contradictions and abrogation**

## [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Criticism_of_the_Quran&action=edit§ion=24)

The Quran contains divine commands or policies that are ignored in Islamic law [\(sharia\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharia), including [Q24:2,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Q24:2) [\[195\]](#page-86-2) which prescribes a penalty of "100 lashes" for [zina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zina) (sex outside of marriage), while sharia law—based on hadith of Muhammad orders adulterers to be stoned to death, not lashed.[\[196\]](#page-86-3) This seeming disregard of the founding work of revelation of Islam has been explained by the concept of abrogation ([naskh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naskh_(tafsir))), whereby God sometimes abrogates one (sometimes more) revelation(s) with another—not only in the Quran but also among [hadith.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadith) Naskh also holds that are Islamic laws based on verses once part of the Quran but no longer found in presentday [Mus'haf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mus%27haf) (written copies of the Quran)[,\[197\]](#page-86-4) which is the case with the stoning penalty for adultery. A number of verses mention the issue of abrogation, the central one being:

- [Quran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quran) [2:106:](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D2%3Averse%3D106) "We do not abrogate a verse or cause it to be forgotten except that We bring forth [one] better than it or similar to it. Do you not know that Allah is over all things competent?["\[198\]](#page-86-5)

Besides [24:2,](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D24%3Averse%3D2) some other examples of naskh cited by scholars are:

- [2:219,](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D2%3Averse%3D219) which allows but discourages Muslims from drinking alcohol; [4:43,](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D4%3Averse%3D43) which forbids Muslims from praying while drunk, and [5:90](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D5%3Averse%3D90) which commands Muslims not to drink alcohol. These seeming contradictory commands are explained by the first verse being abrogated by the second, and the second by the last, as part of a gradual process of weaning early Muslims from alcohol consumption[.\[199\]](#page-86-6)
- • The revelation of a verse criticizing Muslim slackers in the waging of jihad, prompted a blind Muslim ('Abd Allah ibn Umm Maktum) to protest that his lack of vision prevented him from fighting. "Almost instantaneously" a revelation [\(4:95\)](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D4%3Averse%3D95) was sent down partially abrogating the earlier on[e\[Note 22\]](#page-68-7) by adding the qualifier "except the disabled".

- [8:65](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D8%3Averse%3D65) tells Muslim warriors, "If there be of you twenty patient believers, they will overcome two hundred" enemy. It is thought to be abrogated by [8:66](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D8%3Averse%3D66) which lowers the number of enemies each Muslim warrior is expected to overcome in battle from ten to only two: "Now God has alleviated your burden, knowing that there is weakness in you. If there should be of you one hundred, they will overcome two hundred;[.\[201\]](#page-86-7)
- • Verses such as [43:89](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D43%3Averse%3D89) urging followers to "turn away" from mocking unbelievers "and say, 'Peace'", when Muslims were few in number, were replaced with the "Sword verse" [9:29](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D9%3Averse%3D29) commanding "Fight those who (do) not believe in Allah and not in the Day the Last ... ", as Muhammad's followers grew stronger[.\[202\]](#page-86-8)

Among the criticisms made of the concept of abrogation is that it was developed to "remove" contradictions found in the Quran, which "abounds in repetitions and contradictions, which are not removed by the convenient theory of abrogation" [\(Philip Schaff\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Schaff)[;\[203\]](#page-87-0) that it "poses a difficult theological problem" because it seems to suggest God was changing His mind[,\[204\]](#page-87-1) or has realized something He was unaware of when revealing the original verse, which is logically absurd for an eternally all-knowing deity (David S. Powers and John Burton); [\[205\]](#page-87-2)[\[206\]](#page-87-3)[\[207\]](#page-87-4) and that it is suspiciously similar to the human process of "revising ... past decisions or plans" after "learning from experience and recognising mistakes" [\(Ali Dashti\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_Dashti)[.\[208\]](#page-87-5) [\[209\]](#page-87-6)

Muslim scholars such as [Muhammad Husayn Tabatabaei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Husayn_Tabatabaei) argue abrogation in Quranic verses is not an indication of contradiction but of addition and supplementation. An example of the mention of impermanent commands in the Quran is Q.2:10[9\[210\]](#page-87-7) where — according to Tabatabaei — it clearly states the forgiveness is not permanent and soon there will be another command (through another verse) on this subject that completes the matter. Verse Q.4:1[5\[210\]](#page-87-7) also indicates its temporariness[.\[211\]](#page-87-8)

The question of why a perfect and unchangeable divine revelation would need to be abrogated, however, has led other scholars to interpret verse Q.2:106 differently than the mainstream. [Ghulam Ahmed Parwez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghulam_Ahmed_Parwez) in his Exposition of the Quran writes that the abrogation Q.2:106 refers to is of the Bible/Torah, not the Quran:

The Ahl-ul-Kitab (People of the Book) also question the need for a new revelation (Qur'an) when previous revelations from Allah exist. They further ask why the Qur'an contains injunctions contrary to the earlier Revelation (the Torah) if it is from Allah? Tell them that Our way of sending Revelation to successive anbiya (prophets) is that: Injunctions given in earlier revelations, which were meant only for a particular time, are replaced by other injunctions, and injunctions which were to remain in force permanently but were abandoned, forgotten or adulterated by the followers of previous anbiya are given again in their original form (22:52). And all this happens in accordance with Our laid down standards, over which We have complete control. Now this last code of life which contains the truth of all previous revelations (5:48), is complete in every respect (6:116), and will always be preserved (15:9), has been given [to mankind][.\[212\]](#page-88-0)

Some criticism of the Quran has revolved around two verses known as the ["Satanic Verses"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satanic_Verses). Some early Islamic [histories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satanic_Verses#Tabar%C4%AB) [recount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satanic_Verses#Tabar%C4%AB) that as Muhammad was reciting Sūra Al-Najm (Q.53), as revealed to him by the angel Gabriel, [Satan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satan) deceived him to utter the following lines after verses 19 and 20: "Have you thought of [Al-l](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-l%C4%81t)āt and [al-'Uzz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-%27Uzz%C4%81)ā and [Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Man%C4%81t)āt the third, the other; These are the exalted [Gharaniq,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gharaniq) whose intercession is hoped for." The Allāt, al-'Uzzā and Manāt were three goddesses worshiped by the Meccans. These histories then say that these 'Satanic Verses' were repudiated shortly afterward by Muhammad at the behest of Gabriel[.\[213\]](#page-88-1)

There are numerous accounts reporting the alleged incident, which differ in the construction and detail of the narrative, but they may be broadly collated to produce a basic account.[\[214\]](#page-88-2)

The different versions of the story are all traceable to one single narrator Muhammad ibn Ka'b, who was two generations removed from biographer [Ibn Ishaq.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Ishaq) [\[215\]](#page-88-3) In its essential form, the story reports that Muhammad longed to convert his kinsmen and neighbors of [Mecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecca) to [Islam.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam) As he was reciting Sū[ra an-Najm,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An-Najm) [\[216\]](#page-88-4) considered a revelation by the angel [Gabriel,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriel) [Satan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shaitan) tempted him to utter the following lines after [verses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayat) 19 and 20:

Have ye thought upon [Al-Lat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-l%C4%81t) and [Al-'Uzzá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-%27Uzz%C3%A1) and [Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Man%C4%81t)āt, the third, the other? These are the exalted [ghar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gharaniq)āniq, whose intercession is hoped for.

Allāt, al-'Uzzā and Manāt were three goddesses worshipped by the Meccans. Discerning the meaning of "gharāniq" is difficult, as it is a [hapax legomenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hapax_legomenon) (i.e. used only once in the text). Commentators wrote that it meant the [cranes.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crane_(bird)) The Arabic word does generally mean a "crane" – appearing in the singular as ghirnīq, ghurnūq, ghirnawq and ghurnayq, and the word has cousin forms in other words for birds, including "raven, crow" and "eagle"[.\[217\]](#page-88-5)

The subtext to the event is that Muhammad was backing away from his otherwise uncompromising [monotheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism) by saying that these goddesses were real and their intercession effective. The Meccans were overjoyed to hear this and joined Muhammad in ritual prostration at the end of the sūrah. The Meccan refugees who had fled to Abyssinia heard of the end of persecution and started to return home. Islamic tradition holds that Gabriel chastised Muhammad for adulterating the revelation, at which point [\[Quran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quran) [22:52\]](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2002.02.0006%3Asura%3D22%3Averse%3D52) is revealed to comfort him,

Never sent We a messenger or a prophet before thee but when He recited (the message) Satan proposed (opposition) in respect of that which he recited thereof. But Allah abolisheth that which Satan proposeth. Then Allah establisheth His revelations. Allah is Knower, Wise.

Muhammad took back his words and the [persecution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitna_(word)) of the Meccans resumed. Verses [53:21-23](https://quran.com/53?starting Verse=21) were given, in which the goddesses are belittled. The passage in question, from 53:19, reads:

## Have ye thought upon Al-Lat and Al-'Uzza

And Manat, the third, the other? Are yours the males and His the females? That indeed were an unfair division!

They are but names which ye have named, ye and your fathers, for which Allah hath revealed no warrant. They follow but a guess and that which (they) themselves desire. And now the guidance from their Lord hath come unto them.

The incident of the Satanic Verses is put forward by some critics as evidence of the Quran's origins as a human work of Muhammad. [Maxime Rodinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxime_Rodinson) describes this as a conscious attempt to achieve a consensus with pagan Arabs, which was then consciously rejected as incompatible with Muhammad's attempts to answer the criticism of contemporary Arab Jews and Christians[,\[218\]](#page-88-6) linking it with the moment at which Muhammad felt able to adopt a "hostile attitude" towards the pagan Arabs[.\[219\]](#page-88-7) Rodinson writes that the story of the Satanic Verses is unlikely to be false because it was "one incident, in fact, which may be reasonably accepted as true because the makers of Muslim tradition would not have invented a story with such damaging implications for the revelation as a whole". [\[220\]](#page-88-8) In a caveat to his acceptance of the incident, William Montgomery Watt, states: "Thus it was not for any worldly motive that Muhammad eventually turned down the offer of the Meccans, but for a genuinely religious reason; not for example, because he could not trust these men nor because any personal ambition would remain unsatisfied, but because acknowledgment of the goddesses would lead to the failure of the cause, of the mission he had been given by God."[\[221\]](#page-88-9) Academic scholars such as [William Montgomery Watt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Montgomery_Watt) and [Alfred Guillaume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Guillaume) argued for its authenticity based upon the implausibility of Muslims fabricating a story so unflattering to their prophet. Watt says that "the story is so strange that it must be true in essentials.["\[222\]](#page-88-10) On the other hand, John Burton rejected the tradition.

In an inverted culmination of Watt's approach, Burton argued the narrative of the "satanic verses" was forged, based upon a demonstration of its actual utility to certain elements of the Muslim community – namely, those elite sections of society seeking an ["occasion of revelation"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asbab_al-nuzul#Narratological) for [eradicatory modes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naskh_(exegesis)#Modes) of [abrogation.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naskh_(exegesis)) [\[223\]](#page-89-0) Burton's argument is that such stories served the vested interests of the status quo, allowing them to dilute the radical messages of the Quran. The rulers used such narratives to build their own set of laws which contradicted the Quran, and justified it by arguing that not all of the Quran is binding on Muslims. Burton also sides with [Leone Caetani,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leone_Caetani) who wrote that the story of the "satanic verses" should be rejected not only on the basis of isnad, but because "had these hadiths even a degree of historical basis, Muhammad's reported conduct on this occasion would have given the lie to the whole of his previous prophetic activity.["\[224\]](#page-89-1) Eerik Dickinson also pointed out that the Quran's challenge to its opponents to prove any inconsistency in its content was pronounced in a hostile environment, also indicating that such an incident did not occur or it would have greatly damaged the Muslims.[\[225\]](#page-89-2)

Some verses of the Quran are assumed to be directed towards all of Muhammad's followers while other verses are directed more specifically towards Muhammad and his wives, yet others are directed towards the whole of humanity [\(33:28,](https://web.archive.org/web/20140606204827/http://www.usc.edu/org/cmje/religious-texts/quran/verses/033-qmt.php#033.028) [33:50,](https://web.archive.org/web/20140606204827/http://www.usc.edu/org/cmje/religious-texts/quran/verses/033-qmt.php#033.050) [49:2,](https://web.archive.org/web/20160527105235/http://www.usc.edu/org/cmje/religious-texts/quran/verses/049-qmt.php#049.002) [58:1,](https://web.archive.org/web/20160527111014/http://www.usc.edu/org/cmje/religious-texts/quran/verses/058-qmt.php#058.001) [58:9](https://web.archive.org/web/20160527111014/http://www.usc.edu/org/cmje/religious-texts/quran/verses/058-qmt.php#058.009) [66:3\)](https://web.archive.org/web/20160414060718/http://www.usc.edu/org/cmje/religious-texts/quran/verses/066-qmt.php#066.003).

Other scholars argue that variances in the Quran's explicitly intended audience are irrelevant to claims of divine origin – and, for example, that Muhammad's wives "specific divine guidance, occasioned by their proximity to the Prophet (Muhammad)" where "Numerous divine reprimands addressed to Muhammad's wives in the Quran establish their special responsibility to overcome their human frailties and ensure their individual worthiness"[,\[226\]](#page-89-3) or argue that the Quran must be interpreted on more than one leve[l\[227\]](#page-89-4) (See[:\[228\]\)](#page-89-5).

British-German professor of Arabic and Islam [Joseph Schacht,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Schacht) in his work [The Origins of Muhammadan Jurisprudence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Origins_of_Muhammadan_Jurisprudence) (1950), regarding the subject of law derived from the Quran, wrote:

Muhammadan [Islamic] law did not derive directly from the Koran but developed... out of popular and administrative practice under the Umaiyads, and this practice often diverged from the intentions and even the explicit wording of the Koran... Norms derived from the Koran were introduced into Muhammadan law almost invariably at a secondary stage.[\[229\]](#page-89-6)

Schacht further states that every legal tradition from Muhammad must be taken as an inauthentic and fictitious expression of a legal doctrine formulated at a later date:

... We shall not meet any legal tradition from the Prophet which can positively be considered authentic[.\[230\]](#page-89-7)

What is evident regarding the compilation of the Quran is the disagreement between the [companions of Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahabah) (earliest supporters of Muhammad), as evidenced by their several disagreements regarding interpretation and particular versions of the Quran and their interpretative Hadith and Sunna, namely the [mutawatir mushaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadith_terminology) having come into present form after Muhammad's death[.\[231\]](#page-89-8) [John Burton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Burton_(scholar))'s work The Collection of the Quran further explores how certain Quranic texts were altered to adjust interpretation, in regards to controversy between [fiqh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiqh) (human understanding of Sharia) and [madhahib.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madhhab) [\[232\]](#page-89-9)

## **Science in the Quran**

## [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Criticism_of_the_Quran&action=edit§ion=28)

Some scientists among Muslim commentators, notably [al-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Biruni)[Biruni,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Biruni) assigned to the Quran a separate and autonomous realm of its own and held that the Quran "does not interfere in the business of science nor does it infringe on the realm of science.["\[214\]](#page-88-2) These medieval scholars argued for the possibility of multiple [scientific explanations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Models_of_scientific_inquiry) of the natural phenomena, and refused to subordinate the Quran to an everchanging science[.\[214\]](#page-88-2) However, there are factual contradictions between the Quran and contemporary science as shown below.

Muslims and non-Muslims have disputed the presence of scientific miracles in the Quran. According to author [Ziauddin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ziauddin_Sardar) [Sardar,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ziauddin_Sardar) "popular literature known as ijaz" (miracle) has created a "global craze in Muslim societies", starting the 1970s and 1980s and now found in Muslim bookstores, spread by websites and television preachers[.\[233\]](#page-89-10)

An example is the verse: "So verily I swear by the stars that run and hide ..." [\(Q81:15–16\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Q81:15%E2%80%9316)[,\[234\]](#page-89-11) which proponents claim demonstrates the Quran's knowledge of the existence of [black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_hole) [holes;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_hole) or: "[I swear by] the Moon in her fullness that ye shall journey on from stage to stage" [\(Q84:18–19\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Q84:18%E2%80%9319) refers, according to proponents, to human [flight into outer space.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaceflight) [\[233\]](#page-89-10)

Critics argue that verses which allegedly explain modern scientific facts about subjects such as [biology,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology) the [history of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Earth) [Earth,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Earth) and [evolution of human life,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acceptance_of_evolution_by_religious_groups#Islam) contain fallacies and are unscientific[.\[235\]](#page-89-12)[\[236\]](#page-90-0)[\[237\]](#page-90-1)

Ijaz literature tends to follow a pattern of finding some possible agreement between a scientific result and a verse in the Quran. "So verily I swear by the stars that run and hide ..." (Q.81:15-16) or "So, I swear by the setting places of the stars, and this, if only you knew, is indeed a great oath".[\[238\]\(](#page-90-2)Quran, 56:75-76[\)\[234\]](#page-89-11) is declared to refer to black holes; "[I swear by] the Moon in her fullness; that ye shall journey on from stage to stage" (Q.84:18-19) refers to space travel[,\[233\]](#page-89-10) and thus evidence the Quran has miraculously predicted this phenomenon centuries before scientists.

While it is generally agreed the Quran contains many verses proclaiming the wonders of nature — "Travel throughout the earth and see how He brings life into being" (Q.29:20) "Behold in the creation of the heavens and the earth, and the alternation of night and day, there are indeed signs for men of understanding ..." (Q.3:190) — it is strongly doubted by [Ziauddin Sardar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ziauddin_Sardar) that "everything, from relativity, quantum mechanics, Big Bang theory, black holes and pulsars, genetics, embryology, modern geology, thermodynamics, even the laser and hydrogen fuel cells, have been 'found' in the Quran".[\[233\]](#page-89-10) [\[239\]](#page-90-3)
