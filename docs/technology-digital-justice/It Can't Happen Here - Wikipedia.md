**It Can't Happen Here** is a 1935 [dystopian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dystopia) [political novel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_novel) by the American author [Sinclair Lewis.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinclair_Lewis) [\[1\]](#page-13-0) Set in a fictionalized version of the [1930s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1930s) [United States,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States) it follows an American politician, Berzelius "Buzz" Windrip, who quickly rises to power to become the country's first outright [dictator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dictator) (in allusion to [Adolf Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler)'s rise to power in [Nazi Germany\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Germany), and Doremus Jessup, a newspaper editor who sees Windrip's [fascist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fascism) policies for what they are ahead of time and who becomes Windrip's most ardent critic.

The novel was adapted into a play by Lewis and John C. Moffitt in 1936[.\[2\]](#page-13-1)

It Can't Happen Here was published during the heyday of [fascism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fascism) in Europe, which was reported on by [Dorothy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorothy_Thompson) [Thompson,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorothy_Thompson) Lewis's wife[.\[3\]](#page-13-2) The novel describes the rise of Berzelius "Buzz" Windrip, a [demagogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demagogue) who is elected [President of the United States,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_United_States) after fomenting fear and promising drastic economic and social reforms while promoting a return to patriotism and "traditional" values. After his election, Windrip takes complete control of the government via

[self-coup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-coup) and imposes [totalitarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totalitarianism) rule with the help of a ruthless paramilitary force, in the manner of European fascists such as [Adolf Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler) and [Benito Mussolini.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benito_Mussolini) The novel's plot centers on journalist Doremus Jessup's opposition to the new regime and his subsequent struggle against it as part of a liberal rebellion.

In 1936, American Senator Berzelius "Buzz" Windrip enters the [presidential election campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1936_United_States_presidential_election) on a [populist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Populist) platform, promising to restore the country to prosperity and greatness, and promising each citizen \$5,000 per year. Portraying himself as a champion of "the [forgotten man"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forgotten_man) and "traditional" American values, Windrip defeats incumbent President [Franklin D. Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt) for the Democratic nomination, and then beats his Republican opponent, Senator Walt Trowbridge, in the November election.

Having previously foreshadowed some [authoritarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authoritarian) measures to reorganize the government, Windrip outlaws dissent, incarcerates political enemies in [concentration camps,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentration_camps) and trains and arms a [paramilitary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paramilitary) force called the "Minute Men" (named after [the Revolutionary War militias of the same](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minutemen) [name\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minutemen), who terrorize citizens and enforce the policies of a [corporatist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporatism#Fascist_corporatism) regime. One of Windrip's first acts as president is to eliminate the influence of [Congress,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Congress) which draws the ire of many citizens as well as the legislators themselves. The Minute Men respond to protests harshly, attacking demonstrators with bayonets. In addition to these actions, Windrip's administration, known as the Corpo government, curtails women's and minority rights, and eliminates [individual states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U. S._state) by subdividing the country into administrative sectors. The government of these sectors is managed by Corpo authorities, usually prominent businessmen or Minute Men officers. Those accused of crimes against the government appear before [kangaroo courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kangaroo_court) presided over by military judges. A majority of Americans approve of these dictatorial measures, seeing them as painful but necessary steps to restore American power.

Open opponents of Windrip, led by Senator Trowbridge, form an organization called the New Underground (named after the [Underground Railroad\)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underground_Railroad), helping dissidents escape to Canada and distributing anti-Windrip propaganda. One recruit to the New Underground is Doremus Jessup, a traditional [liberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberalism_in_the_United_States) and an opponent of both corporatism and communism, the latter being suppressed by Windrip's administration. Jessup's participation in the organization results in the publication of The Vermont Vigilance, a periodical in which he writes editorials decrying Windrip's abuses of power.

Shad Ledue, the local district commissioner and Jessup's former hired man, resents his old employer. After discovering Jessup's actions, Ledue has him sent to a concentration camp. Ledue terrorizes Jessup's family and particularly his daughter Sissy, who he unsuccessfully attempts to seduce. Sissy discovers evidence of corrupt dealings on the part of Ledue which she exposes to Francis Tasbrough, a one-time friend of Jessup and Ledue's superior in the administrative hierarchy. Tasbrough has Ledue imprisoned in the same camp as Jessup, where inmates sent there by Ledue organize his murder. Jessup eventually escapes when his friends bribe one of the camp guards. He flees to Canada and rejoins the New Underground. He later serves the organization as a spy, passing along information and urging locals to resist Windrip.

In time, Windrip's hold on power weakens as his promised economic prosperity fails to materialize, and increased numbers of disillusioned Americans, including Vice President Perley Beecroft, flee the country. Windrip also angers his [Secretary of State,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Secretary_of_State) Lee Sarason, who had served earlier as his chief political operative and adviser. Sarason and Windrip's other lieutenants, including General Dewey Haik, seize power and exile the president to France. Sarason succeeds Windrip, but his extravagant and relatively weak rule creates a power vacuum in which Haik and others vie for power. In a [putsch,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Putsch) Haik leads a party of military supporters into the [White House,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_House) kills Sarason and his associates, and proclaims himself president. The two coups cause a slow erosion of Corpo power. Haik's government tries to arouse patriotism by launching an invasion of Mexico, slandering the country in state-run newspapers and ordering a mass [conscription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conscription_in_the_United_States) of young American men, infuriating many who had until then been Corpo loyalists. Riots and rebellions break out across the country, with many realizing the Corpos have misled them.

General Emmanuel Coon, one of Haik's senior officers, defects to the opposition with a large portion of his army, giving strength to the resistance movement. Although Haik remains in control of much of the country, a new [civil war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_war) soon breaks out as the resistance tries to consolidate its grasp on the [Midwest.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midwestern_United_States) As the conflict begins, Jessup works as an agent for the New Underground in Corpo-occupied portions of southern [Minnesota.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minnesota)

Reviewers at the time[,\[4\]](#page-13-3) and historians and literary critics ever since, have emphasized the resemblance to Louisiana politician [Huey Long,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huey_Long) who used strong-arm political tactics and who was building a nationwide ["Share Our Wealth"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Share_Our_Wealth) organization in preparing to run for president in the [1936](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1936_United_States_presidential_election) [election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1936_United_States_presidential_election). Long was assassinated in 1935 just prior to the novel's publication[.\[5\]](#page-13-4)[\[6\]](#page-14-0)[\[7\]](#page-14-1)[\[8\]](#page-14-2) The inspiration for Windrip backer Bishop Prang was renowned radio priest [Charles Coughlin,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Coughlin) [\[9\]](#page-14-3) [\[10\]](#page-14-4) who in real life conspired with Long to oust Roosevelt in the 1936 U. S. presidential election[.\[11\]](#page-14-5)[\[10\]](#page-14-4)

According to Boulard (1998), "the most chilling and uncanny treatment of Huey by a writer came with Sinclair Lewis's It Can't Happen Here.["\[12\]](#page-14-6) Lewis portrayed a genuine U. S. dictator on the Hitler model. Starting in 1936, the [Works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Works_Progress_Administration) [Progress Administration,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Works_Progress_Administration) a [New Deal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Deal) agency, [performed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Theater_Project) the stage adaptation across the country; Lewis had the goal of hurting Long's chances in the 1936 election[.\[5\]](#page-13-4)

Keith Perry argues that the key weakness of the novel is not that he decks out U. S. politicians with sinister European touches, but that he finally conceives of fascism and totalitarianism in terms of traditional U. S. political models rather than seeing them as introducing a new kind of society and a new kind of regime[.\[13\]](#page-14-7) Windrip is less a [Nazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi) than a conman-plus[-Rotarian,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotary_Club) a manipulator who knows how to appeal to people's desperation, but neither he nor his followers are in the grip of the kind of world-transforming ideology like Hitler's Nazism[.\[14\]](#page-14-8)

In 1936, Lewis and John C. Moffitt wrote a stage version, also titled It Can't Happen Here, [\[15\]](#page-14-9) which is still produced. The stage version premiered on October 27, 1936, in 21 U. S. theaters in 17 state[s\[16\]](#page-15-0) simultaneously, in productions sponsored by the [Federal Theatre Project.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Theatre_Project)

The Z Collective, a San Francisco theater company, adapted the novel for the stage, producing it both in 1989 and 1992. In 2004, [Z Space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z_Space) adapted the Collective's script into a radio drama that was broadcast on the [Pacifica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacifica_Foundation) radio network on the anniversary of the Federal Theatre Project's original premiere. [\[17\]](#page-15-1)

A new stage adaptation by [Tony Taccone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Taccone) and Bennett S. Cohen premiered at the [Berkeley Repertory Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berkeley_Repertory_Theatre) in September 2016[.\[18\]](#page-15-2)

[Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer) (MGM) purchased the rights in late 1935 for a reported \$200,00[0\[19\]](#page-15-3) from seeing the [galley proofs,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galley_proof) [\[20\]](#page-15-4) with [Lucien Hubbard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucien_Hubbard) ([Wings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wings_(1927_film))) as the producer. By early 1936, screenwriter [Sidney Howard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidney_Howard) completed an [adaptation,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_adaptation) his third of Lewis's novels. [J. Walter Ruben](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Walter_Ruben) was named to direct the film with the cast headed by [Lionel Barrymore,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lionel_Barrymore) [Walter Connolly,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Connolly) [Virginia Bruce,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia_Bruce) and [Basil Rathbone.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basil_Rathbone) [\[21\]](#page-15-5) Studio head [Louis B.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_B._Mayer) [Mayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_B._Mayer) indefinitely postponed production, citing costs, to the publicly announced pleasure of the [Nazi regime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_regime) in Germany. Lewis and Howard countered that financial reason with information pointing to Berlin's and Rome's influence on movies. [Will H. Hays,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Will_H._Hays) responsible for the enforcement of the [Motion Picture Production Code,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_Picture_Production_Code) had notified Mayer of potential problems in the German market. [Joseph Breen,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Breen) head of the [Production Code Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Production_Code_Administration) department under Hays, thought the script was too "anti-fascist" and "so filled with dangerous material"[.\[22\]](#page-15-6)[\[23\]](#page-15-7)[\[24\]](#page-15-8)

In December 1938, [Charlie Chaplin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlie_Chaplin) announced his next movie would satirize Hitler ([The Great Dictator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Great_Dictator))[.\[25\]](#page-15-9) MGM's Hubbard

"dusted off the script["\[26\]](#page-15-10) in January, but the "idea of a dictator ruling America" had now been discussed in public for years. Hubbard rewrote a new climax, "showing a dictatorship in Washington and showing it being kicked out by disgruntled Americans as soon as they realized what had happened." The film was placed back on the production schedule for the third time with shooting starting in June and [Lewis Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_Stone) playing Doremus Jessup[.\[27\]](#page-15-11) By July 1939, MGM "admitted it would not make the movie after all["\[28\]](#page-16-0) to some criticism[.\[29\]](#page-16-1)

The 1968 [television movie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_movie) [Shadow on the Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadow_on_the_Land), which also went by the title United States: It Can't Happen Here, was produced by [Screen Gems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screen_Gems) as a [backdoor pilot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_pilot) for a [series.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_series) The TV movie, a thriller which takes place following the fascist takeover, is often cited as an adaptation of Lewis's novel but does not credit the novel.

Inspired by the book, director–producer [Kenneth Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Johnson_(producer)) in 1982 scripted a miniseries entitled Storm Warnings. NBC executives, to whom Johnson presented the script, rejected the original, which they considered too cerebral for the average American viewer. In order to make the script more marketable, Storm Warnings was revised into a far less subtle [alien invasion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alien_invasion) story in which the invaders initially pose as humanity's friends, but prove to be a genocidal militaristic power with thinly disguised [Nazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi) trappings intent on draining the planet of its water and using humanity predominantly as a food source. The new script formed the basis for the popular miniseries [V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V_(1983_miniseries)), which premiered May 3, 1983[.\[30\]](#page-16-2)

A four-part radio adaptation of the 2016 Berkeley Repertory

Theatre stage version aired on public radio stations and on You Tube from October 13 through November 8, 2020, during the run-up to the Presidential election[.\[31\]](#page-16-3) Among the cast members were [David Strathairn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Strathairn) as Doremus Jessup. The intent of the project was "to encourage dialogue and motivate citizens to exercise their civic power and vote."

A six-part radio play version based on the book titled "It Happened Here 2024" started airing on October 2, 2024 in the United States, produced by WNYC Studios, a New York City NPR affiliate. Starring the voice talents of [Tony Shalhoub,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Shalhoub) [Edie Falco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edie_Falco) and [John Turturro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Turturro) it adapts both the original book by Lewis and [Richard Dresser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Dresser)'s novel, "It Happened Here.["\[32\]](#page-16-4) The story revolves around the impacts of the rise of a fascist dictator in America, who is called "The Great Leader," and how the family and country were torn asunder by white nationalist violence and extremism[.\[33\]](#page-16-5) Episodes 1 and 2 were released on October 2 and the series plays on the NPR [On the Media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Media) podcast.

A British adaptation of the book for [BBC Radio 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_4) aired on October 5, 2024. It stars [Ben Miles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Miles) as Jessup and Nathan Osgood as Windrip[.\[34\]](#page-16-6)

Since its publication, It Can't Happen Here has been seen as a [cautionary tale,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cautionary_tale) starting with the 1936 presidential election and potential candidate Huey Long.

In retrospect, Franklin D. Roosevelt's [internment of Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internment_of_Japanese_Americans) [Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internment_of_Japanese_Americans) during [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) has been used as an example of "It can happen here," despite nominally being a measure against the fascistic [Empire of Japan.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_of_Japan) [\[35\]](#page-16-7)

[Frank Zappa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Zappa) and [The Mothers of Invention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mothers_of_Invention) released their first album [Freak Out!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freak_Out!) in 1966 with the song "It Can't Happen Here". [\[36\]](#page-16-8)[\[37\]](#page-16-9)

In May 1973, in the middle of the [Watergate scandal,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watergate_scandal) [Knight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight_Newspapers) [Newspapers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight_Newspapers) published an ad in their own and other publications, headlined "It Can't Happen Here" and emphasizing the importance of free press: "There is a struggle going on in this country. It is not just a fight by reporters and editors to protect their sources. It is a fight to protect the public's right to know. [...] It can't happen here as long as the press remains an open conduit through which public information flows.["\[38\]](#page-16-10) [Herbert Mitgang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Mitgang) in his [op-ed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Op-ed) piece said "The headline of this ad is the title of a novel that keeps insinuating itself these days, not because of its literary qualities but because of its prescience." And that Lewis's point was "that home‐grown hypocrisy leads to a nice brand of home‐grown authoritarianism.["\[38\]](#page-16-10)

[Joe Conason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Conason)'s non-fiction book [It Can Happen Here:](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It_Can_Happen_Here_(2007_book)) [Authoritarian Peril in the Age of Bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It_Can_Happen_Here_(2007_book)) (2007) frequently quotes Lewis's book in relation to the [presidency of George W. # **Presidency of Donald Trump**

#### [\[edit\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=It_Can%27t_Happen_Here&action=edit§ion=10)

Several writers have compared the [demagogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demagogue) Buzz Windrip to [Donald Trump.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Trump) [Michael Paulson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Paulson) wrote in [The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times) that the Berkeley Repertory Theatre's 2016 rendition of the play aimed to provoke discussion about [Trump's presidential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Trump_presidential_campaign,_2016)

[candidacy.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Trump_presidential_campaign,_2016) [\[40\]](#page-17-1) Writing for [The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian), Jules Stewart discussed the similarities between Trump's America with the country as depicted in the book[.\[41\]](#page-17-2) In [Salon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salon_(website)), Malcolm Harris stated: "Like Trump, Windrip uses a lack of tact as a way to distinguish himself" and "The social forces that Windrip and Trump invoke aren't funny, they're murderous.["\[42\]](#page-17-3) In [The](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Washington_Post) [Washington Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Washington_Post), [Carlos Lozada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlos_Lozada_(journalist)) compared Trump to Windrip, opining that "it is impossible to miss the similarities between Trump and totalitarian figures in American literature."[\[43\]](#page-17-4) Jacob Weisberg wrote in [Slate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slate_(website)) that one "can't read Lewis's novel today without flashes of Trumpian recognition."[\[44\]](#page-17-5) Following the results of the [2016 United States presidential election,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_United_States_presidential_election) sales of It Can't Happen Here surged significantly, and it appeared on [Amazon.com](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon.com)'s list of bestselling books[.\[45\]](#page-17-6) [Penguin Modern Classics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penguin_Modern_Classics) released a new edition of the novel on January 20, 2017, the same day as the [inauguration of Donald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_inauguration_of_Donald_Trump) [Trump.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_inauguration_of_Donald_Trump) [\[46\]](#page-17-7)

In 2018, [Harper Collins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harper Collins) published Can It Happen Here?: Authoritarianism in America, a collection of essays about the prospect of authoritarianism in the United States, edited by [Cass Sunstein.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cass_Sunstein) [\[47\]](#page-17-8)

In 2019, [Robert Evans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Evans_(journalist)) produced the podcast series It Could Happen Here, which speculated on the causes and consequences of a hypothetical second American Civil War.[\[48\]](#page-17-9) In 2021, [New York University Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_University_Press) published a book [It Can](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It_Can_Happen_Here_(2021_book)) [Happen Here: White Power and the Rising Threat of Genocide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It_Can_Happen_Here_(2021_book)) [in the US](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It_Can_Happen_Here_(2021_book)) by genocide scholar [Alexander Laban Hinton.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Laban_Hinton) Hinton argued that "there is a real risk of violent atrocities happening in the United States"[.\[49\]](#page-18-0)
