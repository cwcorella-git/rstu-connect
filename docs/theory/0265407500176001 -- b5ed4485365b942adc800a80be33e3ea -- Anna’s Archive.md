# Measuring attitudes toward adult sibling relationships: The Lifespan Sibling Relationship Scale

# Heidi R. Riggio

California State University, Fullerton and Claremont Graduate University

- ABSTRACT -Based on research noting the importance of sibling relationships throughout the lifespan and grounded in basic research on attitudes structure, a self-report instrument called the Lifespan Sibling Relationship Scale (LSRS) was developed. The current study examined the psychometric properties of the LSRS. A sample of 711 participants completed the LSRS, which measures three dimensions of the sibling relationship in childhood and adulthood: frequency and positivity of behavior toward the sibling, affect toward the sibling, and beliefs about the sibling and the sibling relationship. Results suggested that the LSRS possesses good psychometric properties, including high internal consistency of all six subscales and the total instrument, as well as evidence supporting a coherent factor structure and stability of responses over time. Significant correlations with social support, overall relationship satisfaction, closeness of the sibling relationship, the degree to which the sibling served as a role model, and a measure of various sibling relationship qualities supported the construct validity of the LSRS. Lack of correlations between the LSRS, social desirability, and theoretically unrelated scales demonstrated discriminant validity. Correlations with personality traits and psychological well-being indicated that satisfactory sibling relationships were associated with emotional adjustment. The utility of the LSRS for use in future research is discussed.

KEY WORDS: adult sibling relationships • attitudes toward siblings • lifespan development • relationship satisfaction

Research indicates that sibling relationships have a strong influence on individual behavior and development throughout the entire lifespan (Cicirelli, 1985, 1995a). Siblings are believed to play a large part in the development of interpersonal and social skills, language skills, skills in conflict management and resolution, and skills in maintaining social support networks (Cicirelli, 1995a; Hoff-Ginsberg & Krueger, 1991; Lamb & Sutton-Smith, 1982). Differences in how siblings behave toward one another may be associated with individual personality, overall adjustment, and with different aspects of sociocognitive development (Daniels, Dunn, Furstenberg, & Plomin, 1985; Dunn, 1988; McHale & Gamble, 1987). The sibling relationship has a longer duration than most other relationships, continuing from the birth of the youngest sibling until the end of the lifespan (Cicirelli, 1991). In addition, the presence of siblings in the family of origin has commonly been assumed to be essential for the normal development of children (Falbo & Poston, 1993), with the only child often described as self-centered, unlikable, and maladjusted (Thompson, 1974).

The majority of research investigations concerned with the quality of sibling relationships have focused on children, commonly examining variations in sibling relationship quality due to numerous factors such as child and sibling temperament, family constellation variables such as birth order, and the parent-child relationship. Variations in relationship quality have been linked to differences in individual development and psychosocial adjustment (Brody, Stoneman, & Burke, 1987; Dunn & Kendrick, 1982; Furman & Giberson, 1995; Stormshak, Bellanti, & Bierman, 1996). Of research examining the adult sibling relationship, most has examined sibling relationship quality in older adulthood and involves investigations of caretaking behaviors, social support, and siblings as friends in later life (e.g., Cicirelli, 1980b, 1989; Connidis, 1994; Goetting, 1986; Gold, 1989; Wilson, Calsyn, & Orlofsky, 1994). The general findings of these studies indicate that elderly siblings are viewed as a source of aid in times of crisis (Cicirelli, 1979; Connidis, 1994; Hoyt & Babchuk, 1983), that elderly people who experience more frequent interactions with siblings have a greater sense of control in life (Cicirelli, 1980a), and that elderly adults view siblings as significant sources of psychological support and companionship (Cicirelli, 1990; Scott, 1983). Studies of siblings in middle and late adulthood indicate that siblings generally feel close and accepting of each other (Bedford, 1989; Cicirelli, 1982; Gold, 1989). This research supports the meaningfulness of the sibling relationship to the individual throughout the lifespan.

The sibling relationship in childhood and adulthood is undoubtedly an important area of study, and self-report instruments may aid researchers in understanding the influence of the quality of the sibling relationship on individual development. Reliable self-report instruments may be particularly valuable in assessing adults' perceptions and attitudes regarding the quality of their sibling relationships, and how those attitudes influence behavior and relate to various areas of adult functioning. Because adult siblings may be limited in their degree of interaction with each other by geographic distance (Bedford, 1989), perceptions of relationship quality may be

particularly important in describing and understanding the nature of this family relationship.

Many studies examining the nature of adult sibling relationships have relied on brief questionnaires designed to assess the overall quality of the sibling relationship with a few self-report items (e.g., Newman, 1991; Pulakos, 1990). Researchers have, however, recognized the need for comprehensive self-report instruments designed to measure the wide range of feelings, beliefs, and behaviors associated with adult sibling relationships. There are two validated self-report measures of sibling relationship quality that are currently widely used. Many studies examining the quality of children's sibling relationships have used a measure developed by Furman and Buhrmester (1985) called the Sibling Relationship Questionnaire (SRQ) (e.g., Brody, Stoneman, & McCoy, 1994a, 1994b; Buhrmester & Furman, 1990; Stoneman & Brody, 1993). The SRQ, which was designed to assess individual perceptions of the qualities of sibling relationships of children in later and middle childhood and adolescence, contains 17 subscales, consisting of three items each. Principal components analysis revealed that variation among these scales was accounted for by three general dimensions of warmth/closeness, perceived relative status/power, and perceptions of conflict, along with a fourth weakly defined dimension involving perceptions of rivalry. The SRQ has served as a useful tool in studying children's sibling relationships.

Based on the conceptualization of the sibling relationship yielded by the SRQ, Stocker, Lanthier, and Furman (1997) developed the Adult Sibling Relationship Questionnaire (ASRQ). The ASRQ assesses respondents' current perceptions of their behavior and feelings toward their sibling, as well as their perceptions of their sibling's behavior and feelings toward them. The ASRQ includes 81 items that are grouped into 14 scales: Intimacy, Affection, Emotional Support, Instrumental Support, Knowledge, Similarity, Admiration, Acceptance, Dominance, Competition, Antagonism, Quarreling, Maternal Rivalry, and Paternal Rivalry. Rivalry items refer specifically to parental partiality in the treatment of siblings. Factor analysis of the ASRQ suggested three factors underlying responses to the ASRQ, namely warmth, conflict, and rivalry. The ASRQ demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency and test–retest reliability, and has been used in investigations of adult sibling relationship quality (e.g., Stewart, Verbrugge, & Beilfuss, 1998).

The purpose of the present investigation was the development of an alternative self-report measure of adult sibling relationship quality, the Lifespan Sibling Relationship Scale (LSRS). The LSRS differs from the ASRQ in several ways. First, while the authors of the ASRQ were attempting to assess dimensions identified in the childhood sibling relationship, the LSRS was designed as a measure of individuals' overall attitudes toward the adult sibling relationship. Toward this end, the LSRS was designed based on the common conceptualization of attitudes as being composed of affective, cognitive, and behavioral components, traditionally referred to as the 'tricomponential' view of attitudes (Eagly & Chaiken, 1998; Krech, Crutchfield,

& Ballachey, 1962). Other researchers have also conceptualized the adult sibling relationship as involving emotions, beliefs, and behaviors (Cicirelli, 1991). Furthermore, measurement of emotions toward the sibling appears to be particularly warranted based on the assertion that emotions are the bases of all types of relationship (Bedford & Avioli, 1996; Duck, 1986), and research findings indicating that warmth or emotional positivity is a major component of adult sibling relationship quality (Stocker et al., 1997). The affective component of adult sibling relationships was measured by assessing emotions toward the sibling and the sibling relationship. The cognitive component was operationalized by focusing on beliefs about the sibling and the sibling relationship. Finally, the behavioral component of adult sibling relationships was operationalized by assessing the degree of interaction with the sibling in a variety of activities, and the positivity of that interaction.

The LSRS also differs from the ASRQ by including items assessing attitudes toward the sibling relationship in childhood (hence the inclusion of 'lifespan' in the name). Overall quality of the childhood sibling relationship has been found to influence quality of the sibling relationship in adulthood. Events experienced early in life are viewed as persistently influencing aspects of the sibling relationship during the later half of adulthood (Bedford, 1995). For example, early patterns of cooperation are thought to account for sharing the responsibilities of elderly parent care (Tonti, 1988), and reactivation of early conflicts is thought to account for conflicts that arise over elderly parent care (Matthews & Rosner, 1988; Suitor & Pillemer, 1993). Furthermore, attachment to siblings in middle and old age has been found to be strongly predicted by differential parental affection in childhood, early established attachment styles, and early interaction patterns associated with birth order (Bedford, 1993). These findings have been explained as being caused by the establishment of expectations of family relationships, including the sibling relationship, early in life, such that these expectations become difficult to change once they are established, repeatedly accessed, and reinforced over time (Levitt, 1991). Thus, expectations developed in early childhood serve to guide interactions with siblings and other family members throughout the lifespan. Based on this perspective, attitudes toward the childhood sibling relationship may be seen as a meaningful component of attitudes toward the sibling relationship in adulthood. The importance of early childhood experiences supports the necessity of retrospective reports in investigations of attitudes toward the adult sibling relationship.

A third difference between the ASRQ and the LSRS involves the exclusion of items directly assessing rivalry. During middle childhood and adolescence, there tends to be a rapid decline in reported conflict with siblings (Buhrmester, 1992), and sibling rivalry also tends to decrease into adulthood (Cicirelli, 1991; Ross & Milgram, 1982). Research indicates that the vast majority of adults report rarely or never arguing with their sibling, and rarely or never feeling competitive with their sibling (Cicirelli, 1982). In addition, only about 10% of adult sibling relationships may be characterized as

'hostile' (Gold, 1989). Cicirelli (1995b) asserts that rivalry not only appears to be low throughout adulthood but to decline throughout adulthood to old age. This decrease in rivalry may be attributed to the large contribution of parent-child interactions and parental partiality to sibling rivalry (Leung & Robson, 1991) – effects that are less influential when siblings reach adulthood and no longer reside with each other and parents. Other research indicates that sibling role relations tend to become more symmetrical with age, which has been attributed to the decreasing discrepancy between competency levels of older and younger siblings as the children mature (Buhrmester & Furman, 1990; Stoneman, Brody, Davis, & Crapps, 1989). By the beginning of middle childhood, most siblings develop the egalitarian, role-sharing relationships that appear to characterize sibling relationships during adolescence and adulthood (Cicirelli, 1982; Stoneman et al., 1989). In addition, rivalry and conflict between siblings may be difficult to measure through self-report, as indicated by significant correlations found between sibling conflict items and social desirability (Stocker et al., 1997). Based on this research, the LSRS does not contain items directly assessing rivalry, but rather measures effects of rivalry and negative sibling interactions on individual emotions and beliefs about the sibling.

In sum, the purpose of the present investigation was to develop an alternative self-report measure of the quality of adult sibling relationships, focusing on individual attitudes toward the adult sibling relationship. This article presents initial validation work, particularly focusing on the instrument's measurement properties, reliability, factor structure, evidence of convergent and discriminant validity, and group differences consistent with previous research findings.

## Method

# **Development of the Lifespan Sibling Relationship Scale**

The Lifespan Sibling Relationship Scale (LSRS) was created to measure individual attitudes toward the adult sibling relationship. The original form of the LSRS contained 135 items, all of which were written by the author. Successive review of items by the author and groups of other judges (graduate students and faculty members in psychology) resulted in the deletion of 39 items due to unclear wording, redundancy, or disagreement between judges regarding the construct reflected by the item (i.e., emotion, belief, or behavior). Although the resulting 96-item version of the LSRS was found to be psychometrically sound and served as a useful measurement tool in research (e.g., Riggio, 1999; Weller, 1996), in order to further reduce redundancy among items, reduce the length of the instrument, and increase its parsimony, an additional 48 items were eliminated, resulting in a 48-item scale.

The LSRS is composed of six subscales, eight items each, that assess emotions toward the sibling and the sibling relationship as a child (Child Affect; CA) and as an adult (Adult Affect; AA); beliefs about the sibling and the sibling relationship as a child (Child Cognitions; CC) and as an adult (Adult Cognitions; AC); and behavioral interactions with the sibling and the positivity of those interactions

as a child (Child Behavior; CB) and as an adult (Adult Behavior; AB). Eight items are reverse-scored. Retrospective childhood items were included because sibling relationships typically begin in childhood and last throughout the life-span. Positive responses to items reflect relatively more frequent and more positive behaviors with the sibling (e.g., telephoning, visiting, sharing secrets), positive beliefs about the sibling (e.g., beliefs about closeness and the importance of the relationship), and positive affect toward the sibling. The total LSRS score may be seen as reflective of overall attitudes toward the adult sibling relationship, and individual satisfaction with the relationship. LSRS items are included in the Appendix.

Respondents are required to complete the LSRS with only one sibling relationship in mind. Respondents answer each item using a 5-point Likert scale indicating the degree to which they agreed or disagreed with the statement concerning their sibling relationship (Strongly Agree/Disagree (5/1); Agree/Disagree (4/2); Neither Agree nor Disagree (3)).

## Participants and procedure

A total of 711 undergraduate and graduate students (62% women, 38% men), with a mean age of 23.5 years, completed the LSRS and other measures either for extra course credit or required research participation credit. The total sample was composed of six particular subsamples of participants who completed a variety of different measures. All 711 participants were asked to provide information regarding whether or not they currently resided with their chosen sibling, their birth position in the family, the ages and birth positions of their siblings, and their biological relation to each of their siblings (i.e., 'full', 'half', or 'step/adopted'). Over 90% of respondents described their relationship with a full biological sibling (n = 642), while relatively few described relationships with half siblings (n = 51) and adopted siblings (n = 18). Most participants were asked to complete the LSRS regarding one particular sibling relationship, and were instructed to report on the sibling that had had the 'greatest impact on your life, positive or negative.' Participants with two or more siblings in one subsample were asked to complete the LSRS for each of their sibling relationships. One sibling relationship was randomly selected for each of these participants for inclusion in the total sample. Unless otherwise indicated, results reported are based on the responses of the total sample of 711 participants.

**Sample 1.** A total of 146 participants (59 men, 87 women) with a mean age of 26.3 years completed the LSRS to describe one sibling relationship. Participants were also administered the 90-item, self-report Eysenck Personality Questionnaire (EPQ; Eysenck & Eysenck, 1975), which measures the three basic personality dimensions of Extraversion (E), Neuroticism (N), and Psychoticism (P).

**Sample 2.** A second group of participants completed the LSRS in order to determine the ability of the LSRS to discriminate between positive and negative sibling relationships. Participants in the second sample were 97 undergraduate and graduate students (45 men, 52 women) with a mean age of 24.8 years, 54 of whom had two or more siblings. These 54 participants were asked to complete an LSRS for each sibling in their family. Each of the 97 participants also completed five semantic differential items that indicated on a 9-point scale their overall satisfaction with each of their sibling relationships; the degree of

closeness involved in each of their sibling relationships; how each individual sibling relationship compared to all other sibling relationships in terms of overall satisfaction; the overall degree of rivalry involved in each of their sibling relationships; and the degree to which each sibling had served as a good/poor role model.

**Sample 3.** Participants in the third sample were 127 undergraduate and graduate students (56 men, 71 women) with a mean age of 24 years, who completed the LSRS as well as the 40-item Inventory of Socially Supportive Behaviors (ISSB; Barrera & Ainlay, 1983; Barrera, Sandler, & Ramsay, 1981). Participants were asked to complete both instruments with only one sibling in mind.

**Sample 4.** A total of 60 undergraduate students (21 men, 39 women) with a mean age of 21.4 years completed the LSRS for one sibling as well as the Marlowe-Crowne Social Desirability Scale (SDS; Crowne & Marlowe, 1964) in order to examine the relation between the LSRS and a social desirability response bias.

**Sample 5.** A total of 242 undergraduate and graduate students (80 men, 162 women) with a mean age of 21.2 years completed the LSRS for one sibling as well as the Rand Well-Being Scale (Ware, Johnston, Davies-Avery, & Brook, 1979) in order to examine relations between psychological well-being and attitudes toward the adult sibling relationship.

**Sample 6.** A total of 54 undergraduate students (19 men, 35 women) with a mean age of 22.8 years completed the LSRS and the Adult Sibling Relationship Questionnaire (ASRQ; Stocker et al., 1997) in order to examine relations between the LSRS subscale and total scores and the 14 scales of the ASRQ. Participants were instructed to complete each instrument in regard to a single sibling relationship. Thirty of these individuals completed the LSRS again one month later.

## Discriminability of the LSRS

Fifty-four participants (24 men, 30 women) from Sample 2 completed the LSRS for each of their siblings, along with five 9-point rating scales indicating how 'positive' or 'negative' overall their relationship was with each sibling. Thirty of the 54 participants had a total of two siblings; for these cases, the sibling relationship that was rated the highest on the five 9-point scales was selected as 'positive,' and the other was designated as 'negative,' regardless of the closeness of the ratings. For each participant with more than two siblings, the most positive and the most negative sibling relationships were selected according to the scores on the five 9-point scales.

A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) on the total LSRS score using the positive/negative grouping variable revealed that the LSRS did indeed discriminate between the most positive and most negative sibling relationships (means = 178.5. Similarly, (SD = 31.6) and 140.3 (SD = 35.6), respectively, F(1, 106) = 34.75, p < .001). Similarly, multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) indicated a significant multivariate difference between the two groups on the LSRS subscales, F(6, 101) = 6.36, p < .001. Univariate tests revealed significant differences (all ps < .001) between positive and negative sibling relationships on each of the six LSRS subscales, with F(1, 106) values ranging from 15.09 to 35.07.

# Participant age

In order to examine the utility of the LSRS in measuring attitudes toward the sibling relationship across the span of adulthood, participants were divided into three groups: age 17–24 years (N = 530); age 25–34 years (N = 128); and age 35 years or older (N = 53). Results indicated that the groups did not differ significantly in their responses to LSRS subscale and total scores.

## **Discussion**

The present study reports on the development of a comprehensive selfreport measure of general attitudes toward the adult sibling relationship. This initial validation work suggests that the LSRS has good psychometric properties, including high internal consistency of the six subscales and total LSRS, ability to reliably discriminate between positive and negative sibling relationships, and a coherent factor structure. In addition, the lack of differences in LSRS scores between older and younger participants supports the utility of the instrument in assessing general attitudes toward the sibling relationship across the span of adulthood.

The results support the construct validity of the LSRS by offering evidence of convergent and discriminant validity. The LSRS correlated positively with a measure of social support, suggesting that persons reporting positive attitudes toward a sibling relationship also perceived a high degree of social support from that sibling. The lack of correlation between LSRS scores and the Marlowe-Crowne SDS indicates that social desirability as a response bias was not associated with reports of sibling relationship attitudes. Scores on the LSRS also correlated with independent measures of sibling relationship quality. Ratings of overall satisfaction and closeness, satisfaction with the relationship compared to other sibling relationships, and the degree to which the sibling had served as a positive role model were significantly positively correlated with LSRS scores, demonstrating that higher LSRS scores are indicative of more satisfying, close sibling relationships involving a sibling who has served as a positive role model for the individual.

Significant correlations between the LSRS, the Eysenck Personality Questionnaire, and psychological well-being were also found. Results indicate that individuals with high scores on EPQ Neuroticism (i.e., emotionally unstable, anxious, moody, depressed) and EPQ Psychoticism (i.e., solitary, insensitive, lacking in feeling and empathy; Eysenck & Eysenck, 1975) report fewer positive feelings about the sibling in childhood and adulthood, and more negative beliefs about the sibling relationship in adulthood compared to individuals who are emotionally stable. Obviously, individuals exhibiting a substantial degree of Neuroticism and/or Psychoticism are likely to have tumultuous and unstable interpersonal relationships with anyone, including siblings. Significant negative correlations between EPQ Neuroticism, Psychoticism, and the total LSRS score, as well as significant positive correlations between LSRS scores and psychological well-being, indicate that more positive attitudes toward the adult sibling relationship are associated with emotional stability and psychological adjustment.

Previous research examining the influence of sibling dyad sex composition has reported a higher degree of closeness and emotional intimacy between sisters. Studies of siblings in middle childhood and early adolescence indicate that older sisters are more intimate and affectionate with their younger siblings than are older brothers (Buhrmester, 1992; Dunn, Slomkowski, & Beardsall, 1994). Stoneman and Brody (1993) found that male sibling pairs engaged in more negative interactions than female sibling pairs. Results of investigations of elderly siblings are consistent, with sister-to-sister relationships being the closest and most satisfying, followed by sisters and brothers, with brothers being least close (Wilson, Calsyn, & Orlofsky, 1994). The validity of the LSRS was supported by results indicating predictable sex differences in LSRS responses. Women in the present

study reported significantly more positive emotions toward the sibling, more frequent and more positive behavioral interactions with the adult sibling, and more positive overall attitudes toward the adult sibling relationship than men. In addition, respondents reporting on their relationships with sisters exhibited significantly more positive feelings and beliefs about the adult sibling, more frequent interactions with the adult sibling, and more positive overall attitudes toward the sibling relationship than respondents reporting on brothers.

Research on sibling relationships in childhood indicates that same-sex siblings report a higher degree of childhood companionship than siblings of opposite sex (Furman & Buhrmester, 1985). In addition, same-sex sibling pairs in early childhood direct more positive behavior toward each other than children in mixed-sex pairs (Dunn & Kendrick, 1982). The interaction between participant sex and sibling sex in the present study is consistent with previous research, highlighting relations between beliefs about the childhood sibling relationship and sibling dyad sex composition. Sisters reported more positive beliefs about sisters in childhood than about brothers, and brothers reported more positive beliefs about brothers in childhood than about sisters. Respondents belonging to mixed-sex sibling dyads reported significantly fewer positive beliefs about the childhood sibling relationship than those belonging to same-sex dyads.

The construct validity of the LSRS was also supported by the highly significant positive correlations between LSRS responses and ASRQ scales assessing warmth between siblings. LSRS scores were positively correlated with ASRQ responses concerning acceptance, admiration, affection, intimacy, emotional and instrument support, and similarity between siblings, as well as responses regarding amount of knowledge about the sibling. Conflict factors such as antagonism and quarreling between siblings were reflected in more negative feelings and beliefs about the sibling relationship in childhood and adulthood, and more negative overall attitudes toward the adult sibling relationship, but were not related to the frequency and positivity of behaviors between siblings in childhood and adulthood. ASRQ items assessing competition and dominance between siblings had little relation to LSRS responses, possibly because conflict between siblings is difficult to measure with self-report instruments (Stocker et al., 1997).

Rivalry between siblings commonly involves feelings of anger and jealousy toward the sibling, and thus a high degree of rivalry may negatively affect the positivity of emotions toward the sibling (Newman, 1994). Ratings of overall rivalry between siblings were significantly negatively related only to feelings about the adult sibling, suggesting that perceptions of current rivalry are only marginally related to overall attitudes toward the sibling relationship in childhood and adulthood, and that the impact of sibling rivalry in adulthood is likely to be emotional. In contrast, ASRQ Maternal Rivalry scores were unrelated to Adult Affect, but were instead significantly negatively correlated with frequency of interaction with the adult sibling and the overall attitudes toward the childhood sibling relationship. ASRQ Paternal Rivalry scores were unrelated to all LSRS scores. These differences may have occurred because ASRQ Maternal and Paternal Rivalry items do not refer to rivalry between siblings per se, but specifically to perceptions of parents' behavior involving parental partiality toward siblings (e.g., 'Do you think your mother favors you or this sibling more?' and 'Does this sibling think your father supports him/her or you more?'). In this way, the ASRQ includes aspects of the parent-child relationship in measuring the adult sibling relationship.

The significant negative relation between ASRQ Maternal Rivalry and LSRS Adult Behavior may have occurred because parental partiality by mother negatively affects the frequency of contact between siblings, or because siblings may avoid seeing each other if that contact also entails contact with the mother. In other words, maternal partiality may relate to frequency of contact between siblings only for individuals who are still living in the family home, or when the sibling still resides with the mother. Although this question cannot be answered with the current data, a hypothesis for future research may be that maternal partiality is unrelated to frequency of contact between siblings when both siblings live away from their mother. The significant negative relations between perceptions of maternal partiality and overall attitudes toward the childhood sibling relationship are intuitively sensible. Research indicates that parental partiality negatively influences the quality of sibling relationships between children (Brody et al., 1987; Stocker, Dunn, & Plomin, 1989). However, rivalry caused by parental partiality may be less salient in adulthood than in childhood because adult siblings typically no longer live with each other or with their parents (Stocker et al., 1997). The current findings suggest that perceptions of parental partiality in the treatment of siblings are likely to be reflected in more negative feelings, beliefs, and behaviors involving the sibling in childhood, while they have less influence on feelings and beliefs about the sibling in adulthood.

An important focus of the current research was the distinction between the LSRS and the ASRQ. The current findings indicate that the LSRS and the ASRQ are very different instruments. Rather than seeking to measure specific relationship dimensions identified in childhood sibling relationships, the LSRS takes a more general approach and assesses general attitudes toward the sibling relationship in childhood and adulthood, as well as current overall satisfaction with the sibling relationship. In addition, current rivalry between siblings is viewed as reflected in negative affect toward the adult sibling, while past rivalry (including rivalry caused by parental partiality) is seen as reflected in more negative overall attitudes toward the childhood sibling relationship. The LSRS does not measure aspects of the parent-child relationship or parental partiality, which may not only increase the precision of the scale in assessing attitudes toward the sibling relationship, but may also make the scale more appropriate for use with older adults. Furthermore, while the ASRQ relies substantially on individual perceptions of the sibling's perceptions (e.g., 'How much does this sibling feel proud of you?' and 'How much does this sibling feel jealous of you?'), the LSRS is more straightforward, asking respondents about their own feelings,

beliefs, and behaviors toward the sibling. Perceptions of the sibling's feelings are described as beliefs about the sibling relationship.

A final distinction between the LSRS and the ASRQ involves the summative nature of the scales. The LSRS was designed to be flexible; the items use Likert scaling and reverse-scoring such that they may be summed to indicate overall attitudinal components or overall attitudes. Although the current research focused on the LSRS in terms of six subscales and a total score, the items may be summed in other ways as well. For instance, LSRS items may be summed to indicate overall attitudinal dimensions toward the sibling relationship (i.e., affect items, belief items, and behavior items). In addition, items may be summed to indicate overall attitudes toward the adult sibling relationship (the sum of all adulthood items) and overall attitudes toward the childhood sibling relationship (the sum of all childhood items). It is unclear whether ASRQ items may be summed into an overall score or composite score.

The LSRS is presented as a useful self-report instrument in measuring attitudes toward the adult sibling relationship that is based on experiences with the sibling across the lifespan. It is in relatively recent years that significant research attention has been given to adulthood, as opposed to childhood, sibling relationships. Yet, the sibling relationship in young and middle adulthood is at an intersection of influence; childhood feelings, beliefs, and interactions influence current relationship attitudes, while current attitudes are likely to influence the sibling relationship in older age. Siblings play an important role throughout adulthood, providing social support and meaningful friendships, and influencing psychological wellbeing and adjustment. It is hoped that the LSRS will serve as a useful tool in helping to gain understanding of sibling relationships as they change across the lifespan.
