# COMMODIFICATION OF HEALTHCARE AND ITS CONSEQUENCES

## Isaac Christiansen

Isaac Christiansen is an Assistant Professor of Sociology at Midwestern State University. His dissertation "Resource Distribution, Accessibility and Health Equity: From Global Analyses to the Cuban Case" examines various facets of health and development. His primary research interests are social, economic and health inequalities, social change and development, and the dynamics of economic crises. He has taught various classes in sociology including Introduction to Sociology, Social Problems, Race and Ethnic Relations, and Sociology of Work. He is a member of the Midwest Sociological Society, the American Sociological Association and Jewish Voice for Peace. Email: isaacc@iastate.edu

Abstract: This article examines the political economy of healthcare financing and provision from a Marxist perspective. The article argues that the nature of a country's health financing system results in part as a result of the balance of class forces within a society. The commodification of healthcare is seen as not only creating barriers to access that are deleterious to society's most vulnerable members, but also distorting the dynamics of healthcare provision itself. The article closes with a typology of healthcare financing and provision and a brief analysis of essential elements of a rights-based approach to healthcare financing and delivery.

**Key words:** political economy of healthcare; capitalism and healthcare; health financing; privatization

Within a system of capitalist relations of production, those spheres that promote rapid capital accumulation, whether through enterprises engaged in the "productive" or "unproductive" financial spheres of capitalism, are given precedence over sectors that do not favor capital accumulation, such as public education and healthcare for the poor. Thus, effective demand and the expected realization of all the value components contained within a commodity influence not only its distribution, but also what is produced, researched (cosmetic surgery for the wealthy versus basic medicine for communicable diseases that often disproportionately affect poorer countries and individuals), and where services and medical personnel are located.

Leys (2009) addresses the claim that capitalism (due to improved nutrition originating from increases in population income) was responsible for the large decline in mortality in England from around 1850 to 1950,3 and the conclusion emanating from this: that there is a causal relation between economic growth under capitalism and public health. While Leys acknowledges that some modest improvements in income and nutrition occurred during this period, he suggests that this was not the primary force responsible for the improvement in population health. Furthermore, Mc Kinlay and Mc Kinlay (1977) found that "medical measures" also had a limited impact on the decrease in mortality since 1900. Rather, improved public sanitation and sewer systems (measures opposed by the large business magnates in that period), scientific discoveries as to the causes of disease, and preventative public health measures were largely responsible for the drop in mortality and increased life expectancy (Matcha 2000, 46; Leys 2009, 3–6). Thus, while the coercive laws of competition promoted constant revolutionizing of the means of production to lower the average socially necessary labor time, it was public pressure and working class resistance to capitalist power that played a larger share in advances in public health and the decline in mortality.

The sanitation movement that sparked the mortality revolution in England was then hardly an achievement, in the sense of an intended effect, of capitalism, any more than the Russian revolution was an achievement of the Czarist autocracy, or Indian independence an achievement of the British Raj. The sanitation movement that produced the mortality revolution was a reaction against the social costs of capitalism, not a benefit the capitalist class sought to confer, while on the other hand non-capitalist countries have matched and sometimes surpassed the achievement of many capitalist countries in raising life expectancy. (Leys 2009, 5)

It is noteworthy here that under capitalism, capitalists seek to both augment (and realize) surplus value, as well as to increase that portion of surplus value that becomes their profit (versus the portion that is paid to the government as taxes and becomes part of the public dollar), while simultaneously offering only those "benefits" necessary for retaining their workforce. Furthermore, due to the difficulties and intense competition in finding profitable outlets for investment as both increases in productivity and global competition have magnified overproduction and the surplus absorption problem, there has been an increased pressure on capital to penetrate economic sectors that were previously off-limits to private ownership and investment. While it should be noted that neoliberal austerity measures might arise independently of the pressures created by difficulties in surplus absorption, these pressures may intensify the desires of investors to open up new sectors. Thus, we have seen that the "capitalist class" has often acted through their respective political apparatuses to reduce the amounts of social services publicly available to its workforce (since this is viewed by the capitalist as a deduction from their profits) and has sought to privatize and commodify those services not vet privatized (Hermann 2009).

In countries where the dominant mode of production is capitalist, the balance of social power between the working and capitalist classes varies, leading to various degrees in which social services are privatized. Hence, social-democratic countries that retain public healthcare systems that are either government funded and publicly provided or government funded and privately provided (often through systems of progressive taxation) represent a balance of class forces where working classes have relatively greater control than in countries where healthcare is privatized and access is mediated by insurance companies and market imperatives. The latter group of nations represents countries in which working class power has been significantly eroded and where the imperatives of profit maximization are less fettered by working class demands.4 So, it becomes clear that the more unrestrained the power of the capitalist class in its pursuit of profit, the greater the push for more privatized healthcare, the greater the extent that the logic of capital operates in the field of healthcare provision resulting in greater inequality in healthcare distribution (accessibility).

#### Healthcare Financing in the United States

When a healthcare system is privatized, the goal becomes not only the provision of healthcare and the advancement of science and technology to better that end, but also the maximization of profit for shareholders. In the United States, healthcare is generally treated as a commodity with a large portion of healthcare expenditure managed by private insurance companies that have an economic incentive to deny coverage or care for those well positioned enough to have coverage. The system of insurance gradually replaced earlier systems of direct customer payment for care in the early twentieth century as a way of being able to better handle large and sudden costs. From here we have seen pre-paid plans and managed care models (such as Health Maintenance Organizations [HMOs]) develop in the United States to (ideally) address the issue of rising healthcare costs and protect quality, cutting out presumably unnecessary, ineffective or wasteful procedures. Nevertheless, managed care models and healthcare in the United States are treated more and more as commodities; "the managed care movement has become increasingly for-profit. As a result, the balance between offering access to care runs counter to the bottom line of corporate profits for the organization" (Matcha 2000, 375–76).

The US healthcare system is highly commodified. According to the WHO (World Health Organization) Global Observatory (2016), private insurance accounted for 40.7% of all health expenditures while direct out of pocket expenditures accounted for an additional 11.5%. By 2013, the indicators were roughly identical, with all private expenditure on health (insurance + out of pocket) totaling 52.9% of all US health expenditure.

In spite of neoliberal claims that the private sector exhibits greater efficiency, the cost of insurance companies' excess administration, profit, and additional costs resulting from uninsured and underinsured people postponing or forgoing care have led to the United States having inferior health outcomes in key indicators in spite of spending more on healthcare than other wealthy nations. In 2011, according to World Bank data, the United States ranked number one in the world in terms of GDP (gross domestic product), at roughly 15 trillion dollars. However, contrasted with other wealthy nations, the United States "spends more than twice as much per capita on healthcare, yet nearly 50 million people are uninsured, and tens of millions more are grossly uninsured" (Gottschalk 2009, 103). Far from improving under the Affordable Care Act (ACA), the proportion of GDP represented by health expenditure is projected to "increase cumulative health spending by roughly \$621 billion" (Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services n.d.).

Lamentably, the threat of high medical bills, particularly for the poor and working classes, serves as a barrier to obtaining care, often leading to the postponement of care and the exacerbation of medical conditions that could either be prevented or less expensively treated if caught earlier. In a recent comparative study of healthcare systems of 11 developed countries funded by The Commonwealth Fund, Davis et al. (2014) found that 39% of people surveyed in the United States did not go to the doctor in spite of a medical problem because of cost, compared with 7% in Norway and Canada, 5% in Sweden and just 1% in the UK. Hence, while the United States ranked fifth in terms of quality and timeliness of care, it nevertheless was ranked with the worst health system overall due largely to significant problems with accessibility and outcomes.

In addition, the privatized nature of healthcare provision in the United States encourages excessive medication, testing and procedures for those adequately insured, as these "sales" help the bottom line of pharmaceutical industries and other interested parties, while serving as a barrier to care to those underinsured (Applbaum 2009; Gottschalk 2009; Leys 2009; Farmer 2005). A further complication of the commodified healthcare model has been the growing power of the pharmaceutical industry and its control and influence over the research process (Leys 2009; Applbaum 2009; Suarez-Villa 2012). These phenomena raise further ethical concerns regarding a conflict of interest between private profit and societal well-being.

The Obama administration has attempted to balance the needs of private insurers with the goal of universal insurance coverage through the ACA. The law ostensibly aims at increasing access to care for poorer and less healthy segments of the US population through the expansion of Medicaid eligibility and not allowing insurers to deny coverage to individuals based on pre-existing conditions. The law also imposes a tax penalty on individuals who do not have coverage through Medicare, Medicaid or private insurance. With the intention of avoiding a "death spiral" increase in insurance premiums due to the "adverse selection" phenomenon (where those who are sick select more "generous" insurance policies to gain access to needed care while healthier individuals seek out cheaper less "generous" policies) and to prevent insurance companies from creating disincentives for sicker populations to obtain insurance, the US Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) created a three-prong system of pooling risk.

The first prong is risk adjustment, where lower risk insurance plans contribute to higher risk plans and is a permanent feature of the ACA. The second prong is "reinsurance," a pooled-risk system for insurers administered by HHS where all insurance companies contribute funds and those whose costs exceed a threshold up to a certain amount receive payments. The third prong is the creation of risk corridors where plans with claims at 3% below the 80% medical loss ratio make payments to those insurance companies that paid a higher percentage of their premium revenue to medical claims (Cox et al. 2016). The latter two features are temporary and are due to only last for three years.

Unfortunately, the ACA has not weakened the distortion of US healthcare provision caused by the profit motive. To the contrary, while the ACA once implemented is expected to reduce the number of uninsured to around 27 million, it has exacerbated "underinsurance" and increased administrative costs. The ACA has increased financial barriers to care for the poor in the form of undermining public hospitals and safety nets, high deductibles, premiums, and coverage limitations that vary according to the actuarial value of the specific tiered plan (Waitzkin and Hellander 2016; Andrews 2015).

Early reports indicated that insurance companies, in spite of their voiced opposition to the law, might have benefited. Andrews (2015) presented data indicating that United Health Group, Aetna, Anthem, Cigna and Humana reported large stock price gains from July 2013 to July 2014. However, more recent reports indicate that several large insurance companies (Aetna, United Health Group, and Humana) have reported large losses on ACA plans and are contemplating opting out of the ACA all together (*Wall Street Journal* 2016). The potential exodus of insurers from the ACA ultimately emanating from the modest requirement of requiring insurance companies to cover those with pre-existing conditions reflects the inherent difficulty in attempting to erect a policy that seeks to extend access to care within a framework of privatized healthcare financing.

### The Political Economy of Healthcare

In the Global South, privatization and a rolling back of state services (promoted often through loan conditionality clauses from international financial institutions) have increasingly led to many non-governmental organizations (NGOs) trying to fill the void in healthcare provision (Structural Adjustment Participatory Review International Network [SAPRIN] 2004; Schoepf, Schoepf, and Millen 2000; Smith-Nonini 2000; Pfiefer et al. 2008). While many NGOs are filled with dedicated people who are sincerely intent on alleviating poverty, illness or other social ills, others actively promote a neoliberal agenda (Gareau 2012). "In some instances, however, they are actively neoliberal, engaging in privatization of state welfare functions or fostering institutional reforms to facilitate market integration of marginalized populations" (Harvey 2010, 253).

Arguments made by right-wing think tanks, neoliberals and supporters of privatized healthcare (and neoclassical economists in general) are based on the assumptions that the public sector is notoriously inefficient and that government ownership (and even regulation) stifles innovation and undermines quality in general. Thus, the reasoning goes, private ownership and control are preferable to public ownership and control across the economy in general and in healthcare in particular (The Galen Institute n.d.; The Heritage Foundation 2016; Shleifer 1998). These organizations promote a "consumer choice model" that conceptualizes patients as consumers and relies on the invisible hand of the market to eradicate both inefficiency and poor quality. "Consumer-centered health care reform is to transform the health care market into one that maximizes value, meaning that the system's operational dynamic is competition among participants to produce better results at lower cost for patients and consumers" (Hais Imaier 2008). In spite of these overly confident affirmations, significant cracks in the support for the forprofit healthcare model have appeared even from commentators within the business press itself, at times going as far as advocating a 100% single payer healthcare plan for the United States, arguing that privatized healthcare is neither good for society nor sustainable for capitalists (Unger 2011).

Since the 2007–8 global financial crash, there have been significant pushes for austerity measures across Europe (and in the United States), with many of the cuts in social spending leading to partial privatizations of the health sector (Kentikelenis et al. 2014; Hermann 2009). Much of the ideological support for austerity is based on the neoliberal/conservative positions outlined above, particularly on those that claim that public healthcare systems are unsustainable. Their central claim is that unlike government/public healthcare systems, private (for-profit) systems are self-sustaining.

To examine this claim it is necessary to delve into some concepts neglected by neoclassical economics. The neoclassical economic paradigm would have us believe that there is no such thing as *value*, that *exchange value* has no intrinsic substance (i.e., socially necessary labor time in the production of a given commodity), and that *price* is all that is real, which is formed solely according to marginal utility of the goods in a market. This convenient obfuscation of human labor as the essence of value and the erasure of value as the central element around which price hovers due to imbalances in supply, demand and competition has both a distinct class character and real impacts concerning long-term economic sustainability.

Karl Marx ([1894] 1981) in Volume III of *Capital* demonstrated how the surplus value produced by social labor is divided up between the industrial capitalist, merchant, financial sector (through the extension of credit and the collection of interest and speculative activities) and the landowning class. Since Marx's time, as capitalism has progressed, the relative strength of the financial sector has increased significantly, leading to their capturing of a greater proportion of societies produced value and the further expansion of the illusion that value creation arises from where it does not; i.e., that it arises from circulation or financial ingenuity. This illusion is fortified since the financial sector has grown wealthy relative to the industrial sector (R. Brenner 2003, 81–89; Hossein-Zadeh 2014, 67–81; Foster and Magdoff 2014, 49–63).6 In reality, value is merely siphoned from the sector where it is produced to the commercial and financial sectors.

So, where does healthcare fit into the picture? Healthcare forms part of the "necessary means of subsistence" of society and the worker and, like education, helps provide society with labor power, in addition to being a human right in and on its own terms (Mandel 1978, 50). Healthcare provision is essential for the development of labor power, social reproduction, and the functioning of capitalism. It is a part of a set of investments (such as roads and expensive infrastructure) that individual capitalists prefer to externalize to other firms (when profit can potentially be made) or to the state (if it cannot). This reflects a tendency within capital to first bring all costs to a minimum and deal with the resulting problems later. Since taxes are also viewed as an infringement on capital available for valorization, the corporate sector lobbyists are avid to keep those to a minimum as well, and fear that any form of socialized healthcare will lead to an increase in taxes. Hence, the existence of socialized healthcare in a given society is due to societal compulsion resulting from past and present class struggles/balance of class forces.

What experience generally shows to the capitalist is a constant excess of population, i.e., an excess in relation to capital's need for valorization at a given moment, although this throng of people is made up of generations stunted, short-lived and rapidly replaced human beings, plucked, so to speak before they were ripe . . . Capital which has such "good reasons" for denying the sufferings of the legions of workers surrounding it, allows its actual movement to be determined as much and as little by the sight of the coming degradation and final depopulation of the human race, as by the probable fall of the earth into the sun. In every stock-jobbing swindle everyone knows that some time or other the crash must come, but everyone hopes that it may fall on the head of his neighbor, after he has caught the shower of gold and placed it in secure hands. Aprés moi le déluge! is the watchword of every individual capitalist and of every capitalist nation. Capital therefore takes no account of the health and the length of life of the worker unless society forces it to do so. (Marx [1867] 1976, 380–81)

Marx ([1867] 1976, 638) concluded Chapter 15 of Vol. I stating that "Capitalist production, therefore, only develops the techniques and the degree of combination of the social processes of production by simultaneously undermining the sources of all wealth—the soil and the worker." Hence, when capitalist logic is applied to healthcare systems (the sphere of mental and physical labor whose services are essential to social reproduction and to the capacity of humanity to labor), the tendency is for these services to be provided in inverse proportion to need (Navarro 1976, 41–43; Hart 1971). This is all the more true since both the efficiency of industry and the global reach of capital have made access to cheap labor power abundant. In spite of Keynesian warnings of undermining effective demand, capital does not shy away from the immiseration of the working classes globally (since profit can be supply-side driven as well as demand-side driven, particularly so in the case of export-driven production). Capital risks undermining the essence of the very source of value creation: human labor power.

Thus, the health of the working class as a whole is essential to the production of profit itself by protecting the actual value-producing substance of society itself (i.e., the life of the worker). That this reality has rendered itself invisible to the individual capitalist and to neoclassical economics should not come as a surprise. This is a consequence of the denial of the category of value itself, and of living, alienated, value-producing labor (both mental and physical) as the creator of exchange value. The confusion is deepened since surplus value not only does not accrue to the direct producers (workers), but also is siphoned from the industrial capitalist by the commercial (i.e., the relation between Wal-Mart and textile workers in Bangladesh or the relation between the Ethiopian coffee growers and Starbucks), and financial sectors.

So, what is the result of the privatization put forth as the solution to the "sustainability" crisis of socialized healthcare? When healthcare is treated as a commodity and becomes part of the private sector it becomes progressively more subject to the instabilities inherent within the logic of capital. It is through the application of this logic that we have seen healthcare costs in the United States reach dizzying heights, differing greatly from their necessary costs. This arises from the fact that this is a sector with "high utility" for the consumer with low elasticity of demand (since the patient sees much "utility" in the preservation of his/her own existence), thus providing fertile ground for insurance companies and the health industry to capture greater portions of the total surplus value produced in society as well as greater portions of worker's incomes (through insurance premiums and medical bills not covered by insurance).8

Thus, we see the incentive to transform healthcare from a human right into a commodity in those areas where it has been socialized as a result of collective struggles and where, by law, healthcare represents a form of worker protection—a part of the workers social wage as part of his/her necessary means of subsistence. In the United States, many people of low to mid incomes have incurred some form of healthcare debt and are burdened with big expenses for necessary operations (while many others forgo needed care due to prohibitive costs). It behooves us to recall here that debt often becomes the source of speculative financing bubbles.

Now, having identified some fundamental political-economic problems in the capitalist model of healthcare provision, what are the characteristics of a genuine alternative? What should the public payer/public provider model include to ensure its financial sustainability? Here, what is necessary, particularly in hybrid economies, is sufficient working class power to ensure that the health sector receives *its rightful share of societies total surplus* coupled with correct use of preventative healthcare strategies. Thus, while administrative costs and high healthcare costs are reduced in public models (compared with the exorbitant salaries of insurance Corporate Executive Officers and other healthcare profiteers) and high-end treatment costs are avoided due to the promotion of primary and preventative care, the working classes must exercise their will over the state, to the end of ensuring adequate funding for the provision of healthcare as a human right.

In US society, although healthcare expenditures are greater than in many other industrialized nations, the vast majority of tax dollars feeds the military/industrial complex. This excessive military spending deleteriously crowds out other uses for the public monies (Hossein-Zadeh 2006). This is more than a question of political will; it is the expression of entrenched economic interests that defend their disproportionate share of US tax dollars. While there are genuine financial and ethical concerns about the high costs of end of life treatment, and the extension of expensive healthcare technologies, many of the problems of public healthcare finance are the result of losses of class battles, capitalist economic crises (due to a plethora of causes among them such as the massive growth of fictitious capital relative to real economic investment, and overcapacity), and the resulting neoliberal austerity measures such as those that have swept through Europe. These austerity measures have sought to define many social protections as "unsustainable," when in fact *it*

is the austerity measures themselves that further exacerbate crisis and lead to unconscionable suffering among the population.

## **Some Brief Comparisons**

A society can deal with the challenge of providing ample universal care adequately only when it has the liberty to allocate funds (i.e., to allocate a certain amount of the total social product and social labor) to societies' real needs. For example, although Cuba faces genuine economic challenges, Cuba recently allocated record proportions of its national budget (22%) to healthcare with 54% of the 2014 budget going to social services (Agencia Venezolana de Noticias [AVN] 2013). This is quite unlike the distribution of society's surplus value in capitalist and advanced capitalist societies where it is divided between shareholders of the financial, industrial and commercial sectors for private enrichment; in a socialist public payer/public provider model, the surplus helps sustain national priorities.

In Cuba, where state allocations of the public funds are not determined by the needs of capital (financial, productive or any combination), society's surplus 10 can be moved around to where it is needed according to the plan. Thus, since the major means of production remain in the hands of the Cuban state, the created surplus may be placed in those areas that favor social reproduction or human capital to lead toward (at least in theory) fuller human development (in this case, in terms of education and healthcare). This contrasts with the division of the greater portion of the surplus product being divided among various capitalists with the minimum amount socially necessary for the reproduction of capitalist relations, and the public portion (tax dollars) being allocated with significant portions going to those who have greater influence, i.e., to the wealthy in the form of bank bailouts, tax breaks or military contracts with private firms.11

Even though the US healthcare system does not provide universal or free healthcare, the country spends far more on healthcare than Cuba, in large part due to the US's focus on curative approaches, system of pharmaceutical patents, high technology costs, insurance, and doctor's fees12 (Feinsilver 1993, 97). In Cuba, by contrast, there is no profit motive to push toward an overall curative approach; on the contrary, a guiding preventative approach makes wise use of scarce dollars and resources.

While infant and under-five mortality are low in the United States, 6.0 and 7.1, respectively, those numbers are higher than in many other industrialized nations (Farmer 2005, 152–53). Although the social democracies of Northern Europe may fall short of a "healthcare for all" perspective, as they limit the provision of socialized medicine to their own citizens, they have certainly made notable achievements in healthcare provision to those who they count among their citizenry.

Countries that have some form of socialized healthcare such as Norway, Denmark and Finland tend to have rates of infant and child mortality substantially lower than the United States. As Table 1 shows, countries that provide more universal care have healthcare systems with private expenditure accounting for a far smaller share of total health expenditure.

In addition, the US healthcare system hides within it internal inequalities in outcomes as well as access. Even though the US does not report mortality data according to socioeconomic status, it does do so according to race and ethnicity (Farmer 2005). Infant mortality among non-Hispanic African-Americans was 13.31 per 1,000 live births while it was 5.63 for non-Hispanic whites, with the average of 6.75 (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [CDC] 2011). In the Global South, commodification of healthcare is a more poignant issue, given the paucity of economic resources to serve as effective demand among the poorer sectors of the population. Without effective demand, that is, demand backed by purchasing power (in spite of the presence of overabundant absolute need), those populations can only rely on the state, missionary organizations, foreign medical aid (as in the case of Cuba's collaborative health missions) or perhaps a few NGOs such as Partners in Health in the case of Haiti to provide healthcare where the private sector is unmotivated to do so. Paul Farmer, an advocate of a "preferential option for the poor," argues

[w]hether you are sitting in a clinic in rural Haiti, or sitting in an emergency room in any US city, and thus the provider of first resort for forty million uninsured, you must acknowledge that the commodification of medicine invariably punishes the vulnerable. (Farmer 2005, 152)

# Approaches to Reduce Health Inequality: Components of a Rights-Based Approach

These approaches may overlap and include efforts from civil society and government as well as a greater taxing of the private sector. The WHO (2010) has emphasized the importance of developing healthcare finance structures that encourage vulnerable and impoverished populations to receive care by reducing or eliminating the proportion of direct patient payment for services. They identified three main barriers to the extension of universal coverage in impoverished nations. Approaches to reducing health inequality often vary based on the theoretically identified sources of inequality. Whitehead (2007) highlighted four main types of measures that may be taken to reduce inequality in health. *Strengthening individuals* includes those strategies that are designed to educate low-income individuals about health risks, hygiene, nutrition and exercise. These approaches are often based on "a perceived personal deficit in some respect, whether that is a deficiency in an individual's knowledge, belief, self-esteem, practical competence in life skills or powerlessness", i.e., in and of themselves they may be targeted to remedy the impact of a larger socioeconomic system that produces highly unequal outcomes along class and racial lines (Whitehead 2007, 474). Education of individuals is clearly an essential component in reducing inequality and in health promotion, but it is insufficient to resolve health inequality in the absence of measures that address inequality's structural foundations.

Strengthening communities is a strategy that corresponds to the view that the problem of health inequality arises from forms of social exclusion and powerlessness (Whitehead 2007). These types of efforts may focus on building social cohesion among marginalized populations and creating a space in which the communities are able to effect changes that will have positive health impacts (such as organizing the community to deal with sanitation issues) on their community and are "horizontal" in nature. Also, these efforts may focus on linking the community to a higher political authority to promote a more participatory and inclusive political system, and through the increased weight of the voices of the poor and historically marginalized, disparities begin to get addressed. The strategies of strengthening the community have been at the base of many changes to address health inequality in both Cuba and Venezuela, and one can legitimately view the efforts of Bolivarian Circles, the Federation of Cuban Women and other Cuban and Venezuelan mass organizations to involve the population directly in health efforts as particular cases of the community strengthening approach to address health inequalities.

The next strategy identified by Whitehead (2007) is "improving living and working conditions." This strategy derives from the dangers that poor and working class people face on the job and on their living environment. A glance at the literature of various labor movements and the material history of capitalist society lends much credence to this strategy. The historically harsh conditions of factory workers, miners, packing plant workers and agricultural laborers testify to the validity of such a strategy (Stormquist 1993; Halpern 1997).

Benavides et al. (2000) in a study of 15,146 individuals in 15 European Union countries found positive associations between precarious employment and reported backache, fatigue and muscular pain, and worse health results for fulltime workers than part-time employees. This is consistent with the findings of Marmot and colleagues (Marmot et al. 1978) who found a robust inverse relationship between employment grade and coronary heart disease mortality after controlling for various risk factors. The UK's (then) Department of Health and Social Security (DHSS) in 1977 commissioned "The Black Report" (DHSS 1980), which famously documented steep class-based inequalities in health and health system utilization. The report also documented that the infant mortality rate was double for children born in families of unskilled workers than to families of professional classes, and that mortality (particularly from respiratory illness) and overall morbidity were significantly higher among unskilled workers than their professional counterparts. Ultimately, the types of policies pursued in the political realm (that reflect the balance of class power) impact labor relations, the degree of social protections available for workers, and ultimately the level of health inequality in a given society (Muntaner et al. 2010; Coburn 2004; Navarro and Shi 2001).

In addition, many poor populations live in environments that are less desirable, are more unsanitary and increase the risk of disease. For example, as viable agricultural and other employment opportunities disappeared in Mexico during the debt crisis of the 1980s and the government adopted a neoliberal strategy designed to attract foreign direct investment, the country saw the growth of maquiladoras (work plants in export processing zones) with a steady supply of desperate and displaced workers from elsewhere in Mexico. These maquiladoras caused significant damage to the environment that has had serious negative health impacts on the poor residents who inhabit the border areas (J. Brenner et al. 2000). Improving the living and working conditions of the maquiladora workers would thus be a step toward health inequality reduction by addressing the health threats to some of society's more vulnerable members.

The final strategy suggested by Whitehead (2007) is to *promote healthy macro policies*. "Healthy macro policies" are those that reduce inequality, poverty, and unemployment; augment socioeconomic stability; and promote a healthy standard of living. These "healthy macro policies" invariably will come into conflict with global monopoly capitalism that is subject to recurring crises and stagnation and uses forms of global labor arbitrage to help foment a race to the bottom in wages, labor conditions and environmental protections (Wolff 2012; Foster and Mc Chesney 2012). Certainly, these macro polices extend to the nature of health financing and provision themselves. Below, I present a brief typology of state versus private healthcare system financing and provision to help identify which macro finance structure could best be described as one that is intended to reduce or eliminate unequal access to healthcare systems.

Examining a typology of "pure" healthcare payment and provision structures, 13 one can see that the private payer/private provider model is the most commodified and, from our above analysis, would be the most likely to lead to disparities in access arising from market imperatives (see Figure 1). While the private payer and public provider models may lead to a more even geographic distribution of institutions than private-payer/private-provider models due to a greater degree of public control, they fail to qualify as rights-based, since they place a greater portion of the burden of payment remains on the patient creating a barrier for poor populations to obtain necessary care through direct user fees.

While it is theoretically possible that any model based primarily on private insurance could provide protection for wealthier sectors of society allowing government to target its efforts toward the poor, "issues of political economy mean that the regulations of governing private insurance cannot ensure that this will be a 'pro-poor' financing mechanism" (Bennett and Gilson 2001, 10). Furthermore, the high cost of emergency and necessary care may lead to the financial ruin of poor and non-poor families, as insurance companies try to evade responsibility for claims.

I would argue that the greater the portion of healthcare financing that is private, the further we move away from a rights-based approach.

It should be noted that the targeted, state-funded, private provision model of Medicare and Medicaid in the United States has had some success in furnishing access to care to those who are in the greatest need, *without* simultaneously crippling them with hefty medical bills and debt. Unfortunately, while Medicare and Medicaid have increased access to healthcare for portions of the poor and the elderly, these increases in coverage have been insufficient. For example, in 1991 Medicaid covered only 57.5% of the poor who lacked private insurance, while Medicare coverage was insufficient to protect the elderly completely against high medical bills (Matcha 2000, 342–43). In addition, up until 2014, the eligibility guidelines for Medicaid were tied to 100% of the Federal poverty level, a level that is notoriously set too low; the amount needed for the average family of four to survive may be about twice the \$23,550 stipulated "just to afford basic expenses" (National Council for Children in Poverty [NCCP] 2014; Medicaid n.d.). While the reforms pursued under President Obama's administration have increased those eligible to 133% of the federal poverty level, and are undoubtedly a step in the right direction, the US healthcare system remains privatized and falls far short of a rights-based approach. Matcha's (2000, 45) words continue to ring true.

Although some changes in the health care system have taken place [for example, passage of Medicare and Medicaid, and the emergence of health maintenance organizations (HMOs)], the American health-care system has maintained its primary feature—that health care is a commodity and not a right. (Matcha 2000, 45; emphasis added)

Three interrelated aspects of equity-promoting healthcare structures are the relative emphasis placed on primary care, the degree to which the healthcare system is human resource centered and financial sustainability. Primary healthcare (PHC) and its role within a healthcare system tend to reduce inequalities in access to healthcare and health outcomes. PHC approaches occur at the local level, but they may be emphasized and exercised by governments or NGOs at the municipal, state or national levels. PHC emphasizes prevention and early detection of disease. PHC approaches ideally are "underpinned by universal accessibility and coverage on the basis of need, with emphasis on disease prevention and health promotion, community participation, self-reliance and intersectoral collaboration" (Magnussen, Ehiri, and Jolly 2004, 168). Supporters of a more limited approach focused on targeting mothers, infants and specific diseases promoted selective PHC arguing that the goals put forth at Alma Ata were lofty but unreachable (Basu 2009; Magnussen, Ehiri, and Jolly 2004). While PHC delivery has suffered from various problems, many difficulties had insufficient investment as their root cause (Basu 2009, 296; Hill et al. 2000).14

Strategies that emphasize PHC tend to be human resource centered. Human resource-centered approaches to healthcare delivery take into consideration that healthcare systems need more than investment in advanced technologies to be successful, but depend upon the people working within the system to be effective and sustainable (WHO 2006). Measurements of the degree to which a given country follows a human resource approach include ratios of health personnel (physicians, nurses, dentists) to the population as well as assessments of the distribution of those personnel.

For a healthcare system to be sustainable, there must be a sufficient budget to cover the maintenance and necessary expansions of the healthcare system according to the costs of medical technology, population growth and strengthening human resource capacity. Sustainability implies using cost-effective strategies in healthcare (WHO 2010). These lead to an emphasis on preventive and PHC strategies discussed above that seek to avoid high treatment costs. By this criterion, an optimal rights-based healthcare system would be publicly funded, provide universal coverage, and would be human resource-centered with an emphasis on primary care. Another central element of sustainability is ensuring an adequate stream of revenue to the state so that it has the funds with which to provide services, infrastructure, conduct research, and obtain all other necessary inputs to the healthcare system such as medical equipment and pharmaceuticals.

As implied throughout this article, Cuba's healthcare system provides a model of a human resource centered, rights-based approach to healthcare. Regarding the challenges of sustainability of health financing in Cuba, state workers contribute to the social surplus directly through their labor time and do not pay taxes. Nevertheless, a progressive income tax has been established as the number of people working in small business or cooperatives has increased. Although tourism and remittances have led to a higher level of inequality on the island, it should be noted that the increased state revenue resulting from some of the foreign direct investment and higher taxes that apply to workers who have access to foreign currency are intended to protect Cuba's rights-based approach to healthcare provision by providing more resources to the health sector (Saney 2004, 92–93, 180–85). Recent changes in Cuba (including the new foreign investment law recently passed) have the financial sustainability of public services as one of their putative goals (Gaceta Oficial de Cuba 2014).
