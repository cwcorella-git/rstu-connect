## ![](_page_0_Picture_2.jpeg)

The **Arts and Crafts movement** was an international trend in the [decorative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decorative_arts) and fi[ne arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine_arts) that developed earliest and most fully in the [British](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Isles) [Isles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Isles)[\[1\]](#page-19-0) and subsequently spread across the [British](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire) [Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire) and to the rest of Europe and North America.[\[2\]](#page-19-1)

Initiated in reaction against the perceived impoverishment of the decorative arts and the conditions in which they were produced[,\[3\]](#page-19-2) the movement flourished in Europe and North America between about 1880 and 1920. Some consider that it is the root of the [Modern Style,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Style_(British_Art_Nouveau_style)) a British expression of what later came to be called the [Art Nouveau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_Nouveau) movement[.\[4\]](#page-19-3) Others consider that it is the incarnation of Art Nouveau in England[.\[5\]](#page-19-4)

Others consider Art and Crafts to be in opposition to Art Nouveau[.\[6\]](#page-19-5) Arts and Crafts indeed criticised Art Nouveau for its use of industrial materials such as iron.

In Japan, it emerged in the 1920s as the [Mingei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mingei) movement. It stood for traditional craftsmanship, and often used [medieval,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medievalism) [romantic,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanticism) or [folk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_art) styles of decoration. It advocated economic and social reform and was antiindustrial in its orientation[.\[3\]](#page-19-2)[\[7\]](#page-20-0) It had a strong influence on the arts in Europe until it was displaced by [Modernism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernism) in the 1930s[,\[1\]](#page-19-0) and its influence continued among craft makers, designers, and town planners long afterwards[.\[8\]](#page-20-1)

The term was first used by [T. J. Cobden-Sanderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._J._Cobden-Sanderson) at a meeting of the [Arts and Crafts Exhibition Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arts_and_Crafts_Exhibition_Society) in 1887[,\[9\]](#page-20-2) although the principles and style on which it was based had been developing in England for at least 20 years. It was inspired by the ideas of historian [Thomas Carlyle,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Carlyle) art critic [John Ruskin,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Ruskin) and designer [William Morris.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Morris)[\[10\]](#page-20-3) In Scotland, it is associated with key figures such as [Charles Rennie Mackintosh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Rennie_Mackintosh)[.\[11\]](#page-20-4) Viollet le Duc's books on nature and Gothique art also play an essential part in the aesthetics of the Arts and Crafts movement.

### **Origins and influences**

#### **Design reform**

The Arts and Crafts movement emerged from the attempt to reform design and decoration in mid-19th century Britain. It was a reaction against a perceived decline in standards that the reformers associated with machinery and factory production. Their critique was sharpened by the items that they saw in [the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Great_Exhibition_of_1851) [Exhibition of 1851,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Great_Exhibition_of_1851) which they considered to be excessively [ornate,](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/ornate) artificial, and ignorant of the qualities of the materials used. Art historian [Nikolaus Pevsner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolaus_Pevsner) writes that the exhibits showed "ignorance of that basic need in creating patterns, the integrity of the surface", as well as displaying "vulgarity in detail".[\[12\]](#page-20-5)

Design reform began with Exhibition organisers [Henry Cole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Cole_(inventor)) (1808–1882), [Owen Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Owen_Jones_(architect)) (1809–1874), [Matthew Digby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_Digby_Wyatt) [Wyatt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_Digby_Wyatt) (1820–1877), and [Richard Redgrave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Redgrave) (1804–1888),[\[13\]](#page-20-6) all of whom deprecated excessive ornament and impractical or badly-made things.[\[14\]](#page-20-7) The organisers were "unanimous in their condemnation of the exhibits."[\[15\]](#page-20-8) Owen Jones, for example, complained that "the architect, the upholsterer, the paper-stainer, the weaver, the calico-printer, and the potter" produced "novelty without beauty, or beauty without intelligence."[\[15\]](#page-20-8) From these criticisms of manufactured goods emerged several publications that set out what the writers considered to be the correct principles of design. Richard Redgrave's *Supplementary Report on Design* (1852) analysed the principles of design and ornament and pleaded for "more logic in the application of decoration."[\[14\]](#page-20-7)

Other works followed in a similar vein, such as Wyatt's *Industrial Arts of the Nineteenth Century* (1853), [Gottfried](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Semper) [Semper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Semper)'s *Wissenschaft, Industrie und Kunst* ("Science, Industry and Art") (1852), [Ralph Wornum'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Wornum)s *Analysis of Ornament* (1856), Redgrave's *Manual of Design* (1876), and Jones's *Grammar of Ornament* (1856).[\[14\]](#page-20-7) The *Grammar of Ornament* was particularly influential, liberally distributed as a student prize and running into nine reprints by 1910[.\[14\]](#page-20-7)

#### **John Ruskin**

The Arts and Crafts philosophy was derived in large measure from [John Ruskin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Ruskin)'s social criticism, deeply influenced by the work of [Thomas Carlyle.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Carlyle)[\[20\]](#page-20-13) Ruskin related the moral and social health of a nation to the qualities of its architecture and to the nature of its work. Ruskin considered the sort of mechanised production and division of labour that had been created in the [industrial revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_revolution) to be "servile labour", and he thought that a healthy and moral society required independent workers who designed the things that they made. He believed factory-made works to be "dishonest," and that handwork and craftsmanship merged dignity with labour.[\[21\]](#page-20-14)

On the artistic side Ruskin was influenced by his contemporary [Viollet le Duc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eug%C3%A8ne_Viollet-le-Duc) whom he taught to all of his pupils. In a letter to one of his pupils Ruskin writes : "There is only one book of any value and that is the Dictionnary of Viollet le Duc. Everyone should learn French". And according to some Ruskin's influence on Arts and Crafts was supplanted in 1860 by that of Viollet le Duc[.\[22\]](#page-20-15)

His followers favoured craft production over industrial manufacture and were concerned about the loss of traditional skills, but they were more troubled by the effects of the [factory system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factory_system) than by machinery itself. [\[23\]](#page-20-16) William Morris's idea of "handicraft" was essentially work without any division of labour rather than work without any sort of machinery[.\[24\]](#page-20-17) Morris admired Ruskin's *The Seven Lamps of Architecture* and *The Stones of Venice* and had read *Modern Painters*, but he did not share Ruskin's admiration for [J. M. W.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._M._W._Turner)

[Turner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._M._W._Turner)[\[25\]](#page-20-18) and his writings on art indicate a lack of interest in easel painting as such. On his side, Ruskin dissented firmly from the idea that became Arts-and-Crafts orthodoxy, that decoration should be flat and should not represent three-dimensional forms[.\[26\]](#page-20-19)

#### **William Morris**

[William Morris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Morris) (1834–1896) was the towering figure in late 19th-century design and the main influence on the Arts and Crafts movement. The aesthetic and social vision of the movement grew out of ideas that he developed in the 1850s with the [Birmingham Set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_Set) – a group of students at the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford) including [Edward Burne-Jones,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Burne-Jones) who combined a love of [Romantic literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romantic_literature) with a commitment to social reform.[\[27\]](#page-21-1) [John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_William_Mackail) [William Mackail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_William_Mackail) notes that, for the Set, "Carlyle's *[Past and Present](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Past_and_Present_(book))* stood alongside of [Ruskin's] *[Modern Painters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Painters)* as inspired and absolute truth."[\[28\]](#page-21-2) The medievalism of [Malory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Malory)'s *[Morte d'Arthur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morte_d%27Arthur)* set the standard for their early style.[\[29\]](#page-21-3) In Burne-Jones' words, they intended to "wage Holy warfare against the age".[\[30\]](#page-21-4)

Morris began experimenting with various crafts and designing furniture and interiors.[\[32\]](#page-21-5) He was personally involved in manufacture as well as design[,\[32\]](#page-21-5) which was the hallmark of the Arts and Crafts movement. Ruskin had argued that the separation of the intellectual act of design from the manual act of physical creation was both socially and aesthetically damaging. Morris further developed this idea, insisting that no work should be carried out in his workshops before he had personally mastered the appropriate techniques and materials, arguing that "without dignified, creative human occupation people became disconnected from life"[.\[32\]](#page-21-5)

In 1861, Morris began making furniture and decorative objects commercially, modelling his designs on medieval styles and using bold forms and strong colours. His patterns were based on flora and fauna, and his products were inspired by the vernacular or domestic traditions of the British countryside. Some were deliberately left unfinished in order to display the beauty of the materials and the work of the craftsman, thus creating a rustic appearance. Morris strove to unite all the arts within the decoration of the home, emphasising nature and simplicity of form.[\[33\]](#page-21-6)

## **Social and design principles**

Unlike their counterparts in the United States, most Arts and Crafts practitioners in Britain had strong, slightly incoherent, negative feelings about machinery. They thought of "the craftsman" as free, creative, and working with his hands, "the machine" as soulless, repetitive, and inhuman. These contrasting images derive in part from John Ruskin's (1819–1900) *The Stones of Venice*, an architectural history of Venice that contains a powerful denunciation of modern industrialism to which Arts and Crafts designers returned again and again. Distrust for the machine lay behind the many little workshops that turned their backs on the industrial world around 1900, using preindustrial techniques to create what they called "crafts."

### **Critique of industry**

William Morris shared Ruskin's critique of industrial society and at one time or another attacked the modern factory, the use of machinery, the division of labour, capitalism and the loss of traditional craft methods. But his attitude to machinery was inconsistent. He said at one point that production by machinery was "altogether an evil"[,\[12\]](#page-20-5) but at others times, he was willing to commission work from manufacturers who were able to meet his standards with the aid of machines.[\[35\]](#page-21-8) Morris said that in a "true society", where neither luxuries nor cheap trash were made, machinery could be improved and used to reduce the hours of labour[.\[36\]](#page-21-9) The cultural historian [Fiona Mc Carthy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiona_Mac Carthy) said of Morris that "unlike later zealots like [Gandhi,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahatma_Gandhi) William Morris had no practical objections to the use of machinery *per se* so long as the machines produced the quality he needed."[\[37\]](#page-21-10)

Morris insisted that the artist should be a craftsman-designer working by han[d\[12\]](#page-20-5) and advocated a society of free craftspeople, such as he believed had existed during the Middle Ages. "Because craftsmen took pleasure in their work", he wrote, "the Middle Ages was a period of greatness in the art of the common people. ... The treasures in our museums now are only the common utensils used in households of that age, when hundreds of medieval churches – each one a masterpiece – were built by unsophisticated peasants."[\[38\]](#page-21-11) Medieval art was the model for much of Arts and Crafts design, and medieval life, literature and building was idealised by the movement.

Morris's followers also had differing views about machinery and the factory system. For example, [C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Robert_Ashbee) R. [Ashbee,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Robert_Ashbee) a central figure in the Arts and Crafts movement, said in 1888, that, "We do not reject the machine, we welcome it. But we would desire to see it mastered."[\[12\]](#page-20-5)[\[39\]](#page-21-12) After unsuccessfully pitting his Guild and School of Handicraft guild against modern methods of manufacture, he acknowledged that "Modern civilisation rests on machinery",[\[12\]](#page-20-5) but he continued to criticise the deleterious effects of what he called "mechanism", saying that "the production of certain mechanical commodities is as bad for the national health as is the production of slave-grown cane or child-sweated wares."[\[40\]](#page-21-13) [William Arthur Smith Benson,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Arthur_Smith_Benson) on the other hand, had no qualms about adapting the Arts and Crafts style to metalwork produced under industrial conditions (see Crawford quotation above).

Morris and his followers believed the division of labour on which modern industry depended was undesirable, but the extent to which every design should be carried out by the designer was a matter for debate and disagreement. Not all Arts and Crafts artists carried out every stage in the making of goods themselves, and it was only in the twentieth century that that became essential to the definition of craftsmanship. Although Morris was famous for getting hands-on experience himself of many crafts (including weaving, dying, printing, calligraphy and embroidery), he did not regard the separation of designer and executant in his factory as problematic. Walter Crane, a close political associate of Morris's, took an unsympathetic view of the division of labour on both moral and artistic grounds, and strongly advocated that designing and making should come from the same hand. Lewis Foreman Day, a friend and contemporary of Crane's, as unstinting as Crane in his admiration of Morris, disagreed strongly with Crane. He thought that the separation of design and execution was not only inevitable in the modern world, but also that only that sort of specialisation allowed the best in design and the best in making.[\[41\]](#page-21-14)

Few of the founders of the [Arts and Crafts Exhibition Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arts_and_Crafts_Exhibition_Society) insisted that the designer should also be the maker, although they considered it important that the maker should be credited, which was the practice in the catalogues of their exhibitions. Peter Floud, writing in the 1950s, said that "The founders of the Society ... never executed their own designs, but invariably turned them over to commercial firms."[\[42\]](#page-21-15) The idea that the designer should be the maker and the maker the designer derived "not from Morris or early Arts and Crafts teaching, but rather from the second-generation elaboration doctrine worked out in the first decade of [the twentieth] century by men such as [W. R. Lethaby"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._R._Lethaby)[.\[42\]](#page-21-15)

### **Socialism**

Many of the Arts and Crafts movement designers were [socialists,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist) including Morris, [T. J. Cobden Sanderson,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._J._Cobden_Sanderson) [Walter Crane,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Crane) [C. R. Ashbee,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C. R._Ashbee) [Philip Webb,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Webb) [Charles Faulkner,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Joseph_Faulkner) and [A. H. Mackmurdo.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._H._Mackmurdo)[\[43\]](#page-21-16) In the early 1880s, Morris was spending more of his time on promoting socialism than on designing and making[.\[44\]](#page-21-17) Ashbee established a community of craftsmen called the Guild of Handicraft in east London, later moving to [Chipping Campden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chipping_Campden)[.\[9\]](#page-20-2) Those adherents who were not socialists, such as [Alfred Hoare Powell,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Hoare_Powell)[\[23\]](#page-20-16) advocated a more humane and personal relationship between employer and employee. [Lewis Foreman Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_Foreman_Day) was another successful and influential Arts and Crafts designer who was not a socialist, despite his long friendship with Crane.

### **Association with other reform movements**

In Britain, the movement was associated with [dress reform,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dress_reform)[\[45\]](#page-21-18) [ruralism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruralism) the [garden city movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_city_movement)[\[8\]](#page-20-1) and [the](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecil_Sharp) [folk-song revival.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecil_Sharp) All were linked, in some degree, by the ideal of "the Simple Life"[.\[46\]](#page-21-19) In continental Europe the movement was associated with the preservation of national traditions in building, the applied arts, domestic design and costume.[\[47\]](#page-21-20)

### **Development**

Morris's designs quickly became popular, attracting interest when his company's work was exhibited at the

[1862 International Exhibition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1862_International_Exhibition) in London. Much of this work is directly inspired from the Dictionnaire of Viollet le Duc. Most of Morris & Co's early work was for churches and Morris won important interior design commissions at [St James's Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_James%27s_Palace) and the [South Kensington Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Kensington_Museum) (now the Victoria and Albert Museum). Later his work became popular with the middle and upper classes, despite his wish to create a democratic art, and by the end of the 19th century, Arts and Crafts design in houses and domestic interiors was the dominant style in Britain, copied in products made by conventional industrial methods.

The spread of Arts and Crafts ideas during the late 19th and early 20th centuries resulted in the establishment of many associations and craft communities, although Morris had little to do with them because of his preoccupation with socialism at the time. A hundred and thirty Arts and Crafts organisations were formed in Britain, most between 1895 and 1905[.\[48\]](#page-21-21)

In 1881, [Eglantyne Louisa Jebb,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eglantyne_Louisa_Jebb) [Mary Fraser Tytler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Fraser_Tytler) and others initiated the [Home Arts and Industries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_Arts_and_Industries_Association) [Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_Arts_and_Industries_Association) to encourage the working classes, especially those in rural areas, to take up handicrafts under supervision, not for profit, but in order to provide them with useful occupations and to improve their taste. By 1889 it had 450 classes, 1,000 teachers and 5,000 students.[\[49\]](#page-22-0)

In 1882, architect [A. H. Mackmurdo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A. H. Mackmurdo) formed the [Century Guild,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Century_Guild_of_Artists) a partnership of designers including [Selwyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selwyn_Image) [Image,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selwyn_Image) [Herbert Horne,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Horne) Clement Heaton and [Benjamin Creswick.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Creswick)[\[50\]](#page-22-1)[\[51\]](#page-22-2)

In 1884, the [Art Workers Guild](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_Workers_Guild) was initiated by five young architects, [William Lethaby,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Lethaby) [Edward Prior,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Schroeder_Prior) [Ernest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Newton) [Newton,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Newton) [Mervyn Macartney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mervyn_Macartney) and [Gerald C. Horsley,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerald_C._Horsley) with the goal of bringing together fine and applied arts and raising the status of the latter. It was directed originally by [George Blackall Simonds.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Blackall_Simonds) By 1890 the Guild had 150 members, representing the increasing number of practitioners of the Arts and Crafts style.[\[52\]](#page-22-3) It still exists.

The London department store [Liberty & Co.,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberty_%26_Co.) founded in 1875, was a prominent retailer of goods in the style and of the ["artistic dress"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artistic_dress) favoured by followers of the Arts and Crafts movement.

In 1887, the [Arts and Crafts Exhibition Society,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arts_and_Crafts_Exhibition_Society) which gave its name to the movement, was formed with [Walter Crane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Crane) as president, holding its first exhibition in the [New Gallery,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Gallery_(London)) London, in November 1888[.\[53\]](#page-22-4) It was the first show of contemporary decorative arts in London since the [Grosvenor Gallery'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grosvenor_Gallery)s Winter Exhibition of 1881[.\[54\]](#page-22-5) [Morris & Co.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morris_%26_Co.) was well represented in the exhibition with furniture, fabrics, carpets and embroideries. [Edward Burne-Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Burne-Jones) observed, "here for the first time one can measure a bit the change that has happened in the last twenty years"[.\[55\]](#page-22-6) The society still exists as the Society of Designer Craftsmen. [\[56\]](#page-22-7)

In 1888, [C. R. Ashbee,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Robert_Ashbee) a major late practitioner of the style in England, founded the [Guild and School of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guild_and_School_of_Handicraft) [Handicraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guild_and_School_of_Handicraft) in the East End of London. The guild was a craft co-operative modelled on the medieval guilds and intended to give working men satisfaction in their craftsmanship. Skilled craftsmen, working on the principles of Ruskin and Morris, were to produce hand-crafted goods and manage a school for apprentices. The idea was greeted with enthusiasm by almost everyone except Morris, who was by now involved with promoting [socialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialism) and thought Ashbee's scheme trivial. From 1888 to 1902 the guild prospered, employing about 50 men. In 1902 Ashbee relocated the guild out of London to begin an experimental community in [Chipping Campden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chipping_Campden) in the [Cotswolds.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotswolds) The guild's work is characterised by plain surfaces of hammered silver, flowing wirework and coloured stones in simple settings. Ashbee designed jewellery and silver tableware. The guild flourished at Chipping Camden but did not prosper and was liquidated in 1908. Some craftsmen stayed, contributing to the tradition of modern craftsmanship in the area.[\[18\]](#page-20-11)[\[57\]](#page-22-8)[\[58\]](#page-22-9)

[C. F. A. Voysey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C. F. A._Voysey) (1857–1941) was an Arts and Crafts architect who also designed fabrics, tiles, ceramics, furniture and metalwork. His style combined simplicity with sophistication. His wallpapers and textiles, featuring stylised bird and plant forms in bold outlines with flat colours, were used widely.[\[18\]](#page-20-11)

Morris's thought influenced the [distributism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributism) of [G. K. Chesterton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._K._Chesterton) and [Hilaire Belloc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilaire_Belloc)[\[59\]](#page-22-10)

By the end of the nineteenth century, Arts and Crafts ideals had influenced architecture, painting, sculpture, graphics, illustration, book making and photography, domestic design and the decorative arts, including furniture and woodwork, stained glass, [\[60\]](#page-22-11) leatherwork, lacemaking, embroidery, rug making and weaving, jewellery and metalwork, [enameling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitreous_enamel) and ceramics.[\[61\]](#page-22-12) By 1910, there was a fashion for "Arts and Crafts" and all things hand-made. There was a proliferation of amateur handicrafts of variable quality[\[62\]](#page-22-13) and of incompetent imitators who caused the public to regard Arts and Crafts as "something less, instead of more, competent and fit for purpose than an ordinary mass produced article."[\[63\]](#page-22-14)

## ![](_page_7_Picture_6.jpeg)

[Coleton Fishacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coleton_Fishacre) was designed in 1925 as a holiday home in Kingswear, Devon, England, in the Arts and Crafts tradition.

The Arts and Crafts Exhibition Society held eleven exhibitions between 1888 and 1916. By the outbreak of war in 1914 it was in decline and faced a crisis. Its 1912 exhibition had been a financial failure.[\[64\]](#page-22-15) While designers in continental Europe were making innovations in design and alliances with industry through initiatives such as the [Deutsche Werkbund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutsche_Werkbund) and new initiatives were being taken in Britain by the [Omega Workshops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omega_Workshops) and the [Design](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Design_in_Industries_Association&action=edit&redlink=1) [in Industries Association,](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Design_in_Industries_Association&action=edit&redlink=1) the Arts and Crafts Exhibition Society, now under the control of an old guard, was withdrawing from commerce and collaboration with manufacturers into purist handwork and what Tania Harrod describes as "decommoditisation"[\[64\]](#page-22-15) Its rejection of a commercial role has been seen as a turning point in its fortunes.[\[64\]](#page-22-15) Nikolaus Pevsner in his book *Pioneers of Modern Design* presents the Arts and Crafts movement as design radicals who influenced the modern movement, but failed to change and were eventually superseded by it[.\[12\]](#page-20-5)

### **Later influences**

The British artist potter [Bernard Leach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Leach) brought to England many ideas he had developed in Japan with the social critic [Yanagi Soetsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yanagi_Soetsu) about the moral and social value of simple crafts; both were enthusiastic readers of Ruskin. Leach was an active propagandist for these ideas, which struck a chord with practitioners of the crafts in the inter-war years, and he expounded them in *A Potter's Book*, published in 1940, which denounced industrial society in terms as vehement as those of Ruskin and Morris. Thus the Arts and Crafts philosophy was perpetuated among British craft workers in the 1950s and 1960s, long after the demise of the Arts and Crafts movement and at the high tide of Modernism. British [Utility furniture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utility_furniture) of the 1940s also derived from Arts and Crafts principles[.\[65\]](#page-22-16)

One of its main promoters, [Gordon Russell,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_Gordon_Russell) chairman of the Utility Furniture Design Panel, was imbued with

Arts and Crafts ideas. He manufactured furniture in the Cotswold Hills, a region of Arts and Crafts furnituremaking since Ashbee, and he was a member of the Arts and Crafts Exhibition Society. William Morris's biographer, [Fiona Mac Carthy,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiona_Mac Carthy) detected the Arts and Crafts philosophy even behind the [Festival of Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Festival_of_Britain) (1951), the work of the designer [Terence Conran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terence_Conran) (1931–2020)[\[8\]](#page-20-1) and the founding of the British [Crafts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crafts_Council) [Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crafts_Council) in the 1970s[.\[66\]](#page-22-17)

### **The British Isles**

#### **Scotland**

The beginnings of the Arts and Crafts movement in Scotland were in the stained glass revival of the 1850s, pioneered by [James Ballantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Ballantine) (1806–1877). His major works included the great west window of [Dunfermline Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunfermline_Abbey) and the scheme for [St.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Giles_Cathedral) [Giles Cathedral,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Giles_Cathedral) Edinburgh. In Glasgow it was pioneered by [Daniel Cottier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Cottier) (1838–1891), who had probably studied with Ballantine, and was directly influenced by [William Morris,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Morris) [Ford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford_Madox_Brown) [Madox Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford_Madox_Brown) and [John Ruskin.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Ruskin) His key works included the *Baptism of Christ* in [Paisley Abbey,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paisley_Abbey) (c. 1880). His followers included Stephen Adam and his son of the same name[.\[67\]](#page-22-18)

The Glasgow-born designer and theorist [Christopher Dresser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Dresser) (1834–1904) was one of the first, and most important, independent designers, a pivotal figure in the [Aesthetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetic_Movement) [Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetic_Movement) and a major contributor to the allied [Anglo-Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Japanese) movement[.\[68\]](#page-22-19) The movement had an "extraordinary flowering" in Scotland where it was represented by the development of the '[Glasgow Style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glasgow_Style)' which was based on the talent of the [Glasgow School of Art.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glasgow_School_of_Art) Celtic revival took hold here, and motifs such as the Glasgow rose became popularised. #### **Wales**

The situation in Wales was different from elsewhere in the UK. Insofar as craftsmanship was concerned, Arts and Crafts was a *revivalist* campaign. But in Wales, at least until [World War I,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I) a genuine craft tradition still existed. Local materials, stone or clay, continued to be used as a matter of course.[\[69\]](#page-22-20)

Scotland become known in the Arts and Crafts movement for its stained glass; Wales would become known for its pottery. By the mid 19th century, the heavy, salt glazes used for generations by local craftsmen had gone out of fashion, not least as mass-produced ceramics undercut prices. But the Arts and Crafts Movement brought new appreciation to their work. Horace W Elliot, an English gallerist, visited the [Ewenny Pottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ewenny_Pottery) (which dated back to the 17th century) in 1885, to both find local pieces and encourage a style compatible with the movement.[\[70\]](#page-23-0) The pieces he brought back to London for the next twenty years revivified interest in

Welsh pottery work.

A key promoter of the Arts and Crafts movement in Wales was [Owen Morgan Edwards.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Owen_Morgan_Edwards) Edwards was a reforming politician dedicated to renewing Welsh pride by exposing its people to their own language and history. For Edwards, "There is nothing that Wales requires more than an education in the arts and crafts."[\[71\]](#page-23-1) – though Edwards was more inclined to resurrecting Welsh Nationalism than admiring glazes or rustic integrity[.\[72\]](#page-23-2)

In architecture, [Clough Williams-Ellis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clough_Williams-Ellis) sought to renew interest in ancient building, reviving "rammed earth" or [pisé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rammed_earth)[\[73\]](#page-23-3) construction in Britain.

The movement spread to Ireland, representing an important time for the nation's cultural development, a visual counterpart to the literary revival of the same time[\[74\]](#page-23-4) and was a publication of Irish nationalism. The Arts and Crafts use of stained glass was popular in Ireland, with [Harry Clarke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Clarke) the best-known artist and also with [Evie Hone.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evie_Hone) The architecture of the style is represented by the [Honan Chapel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honan_Chapel) (1916) in [Cork city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cork_(city)) in the grounds of [University College Cork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_Cork)[.\[75\]](#page-23-5) Other architects practising in Ireland included Sir [Edwin Lutyens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Lutyens) (Heywood House in Co. ### **Continental Europe**

In continental Europe, the revival and preservation of national styles was an important motive of Arts and Crafts designers; for example, in Germany, after unification in 1871 under the encouragement of the *Bund für Heimatschutz* (1897)[\[76\]](#page-23-6) and the *Vereinigte Werkstätten für Kunst im Handwerk* founded in 1898 by Karl Schmidt; and in Hungary [Károly Kós](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%A1roly_K%C3%B3s) revived the vernacular style of [Transylvanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transylvania) building. In central Europe, where several diverse nationalities lived under powerful empires (Germany, Austria-Hungary and Russia), the discovery of the vernacular was associated with the assertion of national pride and the striving for independence, and, whereas for Arts and Crafts practitioners in Britain the ideal style was to be found in the medieval, in central Europe it was sought in remote peasant villages[.\[77\]](#page-23-7)

Widely exhibited in Europe, the Arts and Crafts style's simplicity inspired designers like [Henry van de Velde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_van_de_Velde) and styles such as [Art Nouveau,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_Nouveau) the Dutch [De Stijl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Stijl) group, [Vienna Secession,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna_Secession) and eventually the [Bauhaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bauhaus) style. Pevsner regarded the style as a prelude to [Modernism,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernism) which used simple forms without ornamentation. [\[12\]](#page-20-5)

The earliest Arts and Crafts activity in continental Europe was in [Belgium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgium) in about 1890, where the English style inspired artists and architects including [Henry Van de Velde,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Van_de_Velde) [Gabriel Van Dievoet,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriel_Van_Dievoet) [Gustave Serrurier-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustave_Serrurier-Bovy)[Bovy,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustave_Serrurier-Bovy) and a group known as *[La Libre Esthétique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Libre_Esth%C3%A9tique)* (Free Aesthetic).

### **North America**

In the United States, the Arts and Crafts style initiated a variety of attempts to reinterpret European Arts and Crafts ideals for Americans. These included the "Craftsman"-style architecture, furniture, and other decorative arts such as designs promoted by [Gustav Stickley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Stickley) in his magazine, *The Craftsman* and designs produced on the Roycroft campus as publicised in Elbert Hubbard's *The Fra*. Both men used their magazines as a vehicle to promote the goods produced with the Craftsman workshop in Eastwood, New York, and Elbert Hubbard's [Roycroft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roycroft) campus in [East Aurora, New York.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Aurora,_New_York) A host of imitators of Stickley's furniture (the designs of which are often mislabelled the ["Mission Style"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Craftsman)) included three companies established by his brothers.

The terms *[American Craftsman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Craftsman)* or *Craftsman style* are often used to denote the style of architecture, interior design, and decorative arts that prevailed between the dominant eras of [Art Nouveau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_Nouveau) and [Art Deco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_Deco) in the US, or approximately the period from 1910 to 1925. The movement was particularly notable for the professional opportunities it opened up for women as artisans, designers and entrepreneurs who founded and ran, or were employed by, such successful enterprises as the [Kalo Shops,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalo_Shops) [Pewabic Pottery,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pewabic_Pottery) [Rookwood Pottery,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rookwood_Pottery) and Tiff[any Studios.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiffany_Studios) In Canada, the term *Arts and Crafts* predominates, but *Craftsman* is also recognised.[\[81\]](#page-23-11)

While the Europeans tried to recreate the virtuous crafts being replaced by industrialisation, Americans tried to establish a new type of virtue to replace heroic craft production: well-decorated middle-class homes. They claimed that the simple but refined aesthetics of Arts and Crafts decorative arts would ennoble the new experience of industrial consumerism, making individuals more rational and society more harmonious. The American Arts and Crafts movement was the aesthetic counterpart of its contemporary political philosophy, [progressivism.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressivism) Characteristically, when the Arts and Crafts Society began in October 1897 in Chicago, it was at [Hull House,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hull_House) one of the first American [settlement houses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Settlement_house) for social reform[.\[81\]](#page-23-11)

Arts and Crafts ideals disseminated in America through journal and newspaper writing were supplemented by societies that sponsored lectures[.\[81\]](#page-23-11) The first was organised in Boston in the late 1890s, when a group of influential architects, designers, and educators determined to bring to America the design reforms begun in Britain by William Morris; they met to organise an exhibition of contemporary craft objects. The first meeting was held on 4 January 1897, at the [Museum of Fine Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Fine_Arts,_Boston) (MFA) in Boston to organise an exhibition of contemporary crafts. When craftsmen, consumers, and manufacturers realised the aesthetic and technical potential of the applied arts, the process of design reform in Boston started. Present at this meeting were General Charles Loring, Chairman of the Trustees of the MFA; [William](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Sturgis_Bigelow) [Sturgis Bigelow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Sturgis_Bigelow) and [Denman Ross,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denman_Ross) collectors, writers and MFA trustees; Ross Turner, painter; [Sylvester Baxter,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylvester_Baxter) art critic for the *Boston Transcript*; Howard Baker, [A. W. Longfellow Jr.;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Wadsworth_Longfellow_Jr.) and Ralph Clipson Sturgis, architect.

#### **Architecture and art**

The ["Prairie School"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prairie_School) of [Frank Lloyd Wright,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Lloyd_Wright) [George Washington Maher,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington_Maher) and other architects in Chicago, the [Country Day School movement,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Country_Day_School_movement) the [bungalow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bungalow) and [ultimate bungalow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultimate_bungalow) style of houses popularised by [Greene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greene_and_Greene) [and Greene,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greene_and_Greene) [Julia Morgan,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_Morgan) and [Bernard Maybeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Maybeck) are some examples of the American Arts and Crafts and [American Craftsman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Craftsman) style of architecture. Restored and landmark-protected examples are still present in America, especially in California in [Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berkeley,_California) and [Pasadena,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasadena,_California) and the sections of other towns originally developed during the era and not experiencing post-war urban renewal. [Mission Revival,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mission_Revival_Style_architecture) Prairie School, and the '[California bungalow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_bungalow)' styles of residential building remain popular in the United States today.

As theoreticians, educators, and prolific artists in mediums from printmaking to pottery and pastel, two of the most influential figures were [Arthur Wesley Dow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Wesley_Dow) (1857–1922) on the East Coast and [Pedro Joseph de Lemos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedro_Joseph_de_Lemos) (1882–1954) in California. Dow, who taught at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University) and founded the Ipswich Summer School of Art, published in 1899 his landmark *Composition*, which distilled into a distinctly American approach the essence of Japanese composition, combining into a decorative harmonious amalgam three elements: simplicity of line, "notan" (the balance of light and dark areas), and symmetry of colour.[\[85\]](#page-23-15)

His purpose was to create objects that were finely crafted and beautifully rendered. His student de Lemos, who became head of the [San Francisco Art Institute,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_Art_Institute) Director of the [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University) Museum and Art Gallery, and Editor-in-Chief of the *School Arts Magazine*, expanded and substantially revised Dow's ideas in over 150 monographs and articles for art schools in the United States and Britain.[\[86\]](#page-23-16) Among his many unorthodox teachings was his belief that manufactured products could express "the sublime beauty" and that great insight was to be found in the abstract "design forms" of pre-Columbian civilisations.

The [Museum of the American Arts and Crafts Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_the_American_Arts_and_Crafts_Movement) in [St. Petersburg, Florida,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Petersburg,_Florida) opened its doors in 2021.[\[87\]](#page-24-0)[\[88\]](#page-24-1)

#### **Asia**

In Japan, [Yanagi S](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yanagi_S%C5%8Detsu)ōetsu, creator of the [Mingei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mingei) movement which promoted folk art from the 1920s onwards, was influenced by the writings of Morris and Ruskin.[\[38\]](#page-21-11) Like the Arts and Crafts movement in Europe, Mingei sought to preserve traditional crafts in the face of modernising industry.

## **Architecture**

The movement ... represents in some sense a revolt against the hard mechanical conventional life and its insensibility to beauty (quite another thing to ornament). It is a protest against that so-called industrial progress which produces shoddy wares, the cheapness of which is paid for by the lives of their producers and the degradation of their users. It is a protest against the turning of men into machines, against artificial distinctions in art, and against making the immediate market value, or possibility of profit, the chief test of artistic merit. It also advances the claim of all and each to the common possession of beauty in things common and familiar, and would awaken the sense of this beauty, deadened and depressed as it now too often is, either on the one hand by luxurious superfluities, or on the other by the absence of the commonest necessities and the gnawing anxiety for the means of livelihood; not to speak of the everyday uglinesses to which we have accustomed our eyes, confused by the flood of false taste, or darkened by the hurried life of modern towns in which huge aggregations of humanity exist, equally removed from both art and nature and their kindly and refining influences.

—Walter Crane, ["Of The Revival of Design and Handicraft",](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Arts_and_Crafts_Essays/Of_the_Revival_of_Design_and_Handicraft) *Arts and Crafts Essays* (1893)[\[89\]](#page-24-2)

Many of the leaders of the Arts and Crafts movement were trained as architects (e.g. William Morris, A. H. Mackmurdo, C. R. Ashbee, W. R. Lethaby) and it was on building that the movement had its most visible and lasting influence.

[Red House,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_House,_Bexleyheath) in [Bexleyheath,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bexleyheath) London, designed for Morris in 1859 by architect [Philip Webb,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Webb) exemplifies the early Arts and Crafts style, with its well-proportioned solid forms, wide porches, steep roof, pointed window arches, brick fireplaces and wooden fittings. Webb rejected classical and other revivals of historical styles based on grand buildings, and based his design on British [vernacular architecture,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vernacular_architecture) expressing the texture of ordinary materials, such as stone and tiles, with an asymmetrical and picturesque building composition.[\[18\]](#page-20-11)

The London suburb of [Bedford Park,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bedford_Park,_London) built mainly in the 1880s and 1890s, has about 360 Arts and Crafts style houses and was once famous for its [Aesthetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aestheticism) residents. Several [Almshouses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almshouse) were built in the Arts and Crafts style, for example, [Whiteley Village,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whiteley_Village) Surrey, built between 1914 and 1917, with over 280 buildings, and the [Dyers Almshouses,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyers_Almshouses) Sussex, built between 1939 and 1971. [Letchworth Garden City,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letchworth_Garden_City) the first garden city, was inspired by Arts and Crafts ideals[.\[8\]](#page-20-1)

The first houses were designed by [Barry Parker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Barry_Parker) and [Raymond Unwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_Unwin) in the [vernacular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vernacular) style popularised by the movement and the town became associated with high-mindedness and simple living. The sandal-making workshop set up by [Edward Carpenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Carpenter) moved from Yorkshire to Letchworth Garden City and [George](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Orwell) [Orwell'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Orwell)s jibe about "every fruit-juice drinker, nudist, sandal-wearer, sex-maniac, Quaker, 'Nature Cure' quack, pacifist, and feminist in England" going to a socialist conference in Letchworth has become famous[.\[90\]](#page-24-3)

## **Garden design**

[Gertrude Jekyll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gertrude_Jekyll) applied Arts and Crafts principles to garden design. She worked with the English architect, Sir [Edwin Lutyens,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Lutyens) for whose projects she created numerous landscapes, and who designed her home [Munstead Wood,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munstead_Wood) near Godalming in Surrey[.\[91\]](#page-24-4) Jekyll created the gardens for [Bishopsbarns,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishopsbarns)[\[92\]](#page-24-5) the home of York architect [Walter Brierley,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Brierley) an exponent of the Arts and Crafts movement and known as the "Lutyens of the North".[\[93\]](#page-24-6) The garden for Brierley's final project, [Goddards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goddards_House_and_Garden) in York, was the work of George Dillistone, a gardener who worked with Lutyens and Jekyll at [Castle Drogo.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castle_Drogo)[\[94\]](#page-24-7) At [Goddards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goddards_House_and_Garden) the garden incorporated a number of features that reflected the arts and crafts style of the house, such as the use of hedges and [herbaceous borders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbaceous_border) to divide the garden into a series of outdoor rooms[.\[95\]](#page-24-8)

Another notable Arts and Crafts garden is [Hidcote Manor Garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hidcote_Manor_Garden) designed by [Lawrence Johnston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Waterbury_Johnston) which is also laid out in a series of outdoor rooms and where, like Goddards, the landscaping becomes less formal further away from the house.[\[96\]](#page-24-9) Other examples of Arts and Crafts gardens include [Hestercombe Gardens,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hestercombe_House) [Lytes Cary Manor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lytes_Cary) and the gardens of some of the architectural examples of arts and crafts buildings (listed above).

## **Art education**

Morris's ideas were adopted by the [New Education Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Education_Movement) in the late 1880s, which incorporated handicraft teaching in schools at Abbotsholme (1889) and [Bedales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bedales) (1892), and his influence has been noted in the social experiments of [Dartington Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartington_Hall) during the mid-20th century.[\[66\]](#page-22-17)

Arts and Crafts practitioners in Britain were critical of the government system of art education based on design in the abstract with little teaching of practical craft. This lack of craft training also caused concern in industrial and official circles, and in 1884 a Royal Commission (accepting the advice of William Morris) recommended that art education should pay more attention to the suitability of design to the material in which it was to be executed[.\[97\]](#page-24-10) The first school to make this change was the [Birmingham School of Arts and](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_Art_School) [Crafts,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_Art_School) which "led the way in introducing executed design to the teaching of art and design nationally (working in the material for which the design was intended rather than designing on paper). In his external examiner's report of 1889, Walter Crane praised Birmingham School of Art in that it 'considered design in relationship to materials and usage.' "[\[98\]](#page-24-11) Under the direction of Edward Taylor, its headmaster from 1877 to 1903, and with the help of [Henry Payne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Payne_(artist)) and [Joseph Southall,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Southall) the Birmingham School became a leading Artsand-Crafts centre[.\[99\]](#page-25-0)

Other local authority schools also began to introduce more practical teaching of crafts, and by the 1890s Arts and Crafts ideals were being disseminated by members of the Art Workers Guild into art schools throughout the country. Members of the Guild held influential positions: Walter Crane was director of the [Manchester School of Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_School_of_Art) and subsequently the [Royal College of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_College_of_Art) [Art;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_College_of_Art) F.
