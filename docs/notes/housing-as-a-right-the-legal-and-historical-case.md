## A Foundational Document for the Reno-Sparks Tenants Union

---

The housing crisis isn't a crisis. A crisis is something that happens to you. This was done to us.

For ninety years, the federal government built a system that treats shelter—the most basic human need after food and water—as a casino chip. The returns have been spectacular, if you own the casino. For everyone else, the math is simple: pay half your income to someone who bought the building with borrowed money, or sleep outside and get arrested for it.

This document makes one argument: **We are responsible for the unhoused.** Not as charity. Not as compassion. As obligation—because we made the system that put them there, and we keep it running.

The evidence isn't hidden. It's in congressional testimony and FHA manuals and exposed court records. It's in the exposed text of laws we passed and the exposed minutes of meetings where officials decided, on purpose, that some people's homes would be taken and others' would be subsidized. Every person sleeping in a tent along the Truckee is there because of choices made in Washington and Carson City and Reno City Hall.

If that sounds like an exaggeration, keep reading.

---

## Congress said housing was a right. Then walked away.

In 1949, Congress passed the Housing Act. Section 2 reads like something a tenant organizer would write:

> "The Congress declares that the general welfare and security of the Nation and the health and living standards of its people require... the realization as soon as feasible of the goal of a decent home and a suitable living environment for every American family."

Not "some families." Not "families who can afford it." Every American family. Truman called it a "national objective." Congress reaffirmed it in 1968. The law is still on the books. 42 U.S.C. § 1441. You can look it up.

So what happened?

The same Congress that declared housing a national goal funded highways that destroyed Black neighborhoods. The same federal government that said everyone deserves a decent home required segregation as a condition of mortgage insurance. The law said one thing; the money did another.

The Fair Housing Act of 1968 tried to fix this. It didn't just ban discrimination—it created an *affirmative* duty. Section 3608(d) says federal agencies "shall administer their programs and activities relating to housing and urban development... in a manner affirmatively to further" fair housing. Courts have enforced this. In *Shannon v. HUD*, the Third Circuit told HUD it had to "utilize some institutionalized method" to actually make fair housing happen. In *NAACP v. HUD*, the First Circuit ruled that "HUD's obligation to affirmatively further fair housing requires that HUD do more than simply not discriminate itself."

The government doesn't just have to stop hurting people. It has to help. That's what the law says.

New York's constitution goes further. Article XVII: "The aid, care and support of the needy are public concerns and shall be provided by the state." Not "may be." Shall be. In 1981, advocates used that language to win *Callahan v. Carey*, which created the only enforceable right to shelter in the country. New York City must provide a bed to anyone who asks. The city fought it for decades. Lost every time.

Montana's constitution says "the dignity of the human being is inviolable." It guarantees "the rights of pursuing life's basic necessities." These aren't suggestions. They're law.

New Jersey proved that courts can make housing happen. In 1975, the state Supreme Court ruled in *Mount Laurel* that every municipality has a constitutional obligation—not option, obligation—to provide its "fair share" of affordable housing. Towns that zoned out poor people had to zone them back in. The case has been enforced for fifty years.

None of this is radical. It's already in the code. The question is whether we mean it.

## The government built segregation on purpose

Here is the sentence from the 1938 FHA Underwriting Manual:

> "If a neighborhood is to retain stability it is necessary that properties shall continue to be occupied by the same social and racial classes."

Not a memo. Not a suggestion. The official manual. Federal policy, in writing, required that Black and white Americans live separately or the government wouldn't insure the mortgages.

The manual called "infiltration of inharmonious racial or nationality groups" an "adverse influence" that would lower property values. Housing scholars have compared the language to the Nuremberg Laws. That's not hyperbole—read them side by side.

Here's how it worked in practice. In 1941, a developer in Detroit wanted FHA backing for a white subdivision. Problem: a Black neighborhood sat nearby. FHA refused to approve the loans. So the developer built a six-foot concrete wall—half a mile long—between the white development and the Black community. Then FHA approved the mortgages. The wall still stands.

Levittown, the symbol of postwar suburban prosperity, excluded Black buyers as a condition of federal loan guarantees. The contract literally required it. From 1934 to 1968, more than 98% of FHA-insured loans went to white borrowers.

The Home Owners' Loan Corporation made it visual. HOLC drew maps of 239 American cities, grading neighborhoods A through D. Grade D—marked in red, which is where "redlining" comes from—went to any neighborhood with Black residents. The condition of the housing didn't matter. The finances of the residents didn't matter. If Black people lived there, the neighborhood was "hazardous."

A 2020 Federal Reserve study found that 74% of neighborhoods HOLC marked "hazardous" in the 1930s remain low-to-moderate income today. Ninety years later. The maps worked exactly as designed—they locked Black Americans out of the wealth-building machine and kept them locked out across generations.

Before 1948, courts enforced racial covenants—deed restrictions that prohibited selling property to Black buyers. The Supreme Court heard *Shelley v. Kraemer* that year and ruled that judicial enforcement of private discrimination counts as state action. Three justices had to recuse themselves because they personally owned property with racial covenants.

The GI Bill—sold as reward for service, opportunity for all—operated the same way. The VA adopted FHA's racial exclusion rules. In Mississippi in 1947, out of 3,229 VA home loans, two went to Black veterans. Two. In New York and northern New Jersey, of 67,000 GI Bill home purchases, fewer than 100 were non-white.

Historian Ira Katznelson calculated what this theft cost. A Black World War II veteran's family is owed roughly $180,000—the gap between the benefits they received and what white veterans got. Multiply that by millions of veterans. The number gets very large.

This wasn't the market. This wasn't individual prejudice. This was policy.

## Then they tore down what Black communities built anyway

"Urban renewal means Negro removal." James Baldwin said it first. The data proves it.

Between 1949 and 1971, federally funded urban renewal displaced approximately 1.36 million people. Sixty percent were not white. The government spent $13 billion demolishing neighborhoods.

In Newark, 77% of those displaced by urban renewal were Black—in a city that was only 34% Black at the time. Not proportional. Targeted. Cincinnati's Kenyon Barr project displaced at least 4,953 families—97% Black—to build a light-industrial park. Pittsburgh destroyed the Hill District for a sports arena. San Francisco's Western Addition wiped out over 20,000 people of color.

The chancellor of the University of Chicago explained urban renewal's purpose in exposed documents: "an effective screening tool" and a way of "cutting down the number of Negroes" near the university. Not subtext. Text.

Public housing was supposed to be different. It wasn't. In Chicago, between 1954 and 1967, the Housing Authority built more than 10,300 units. Exactly 63 were outside poor, racially segregated areas. A federal judge in *Gautreaux v. Chicago Housing Authority* called it what it was: "no criterion, other than race, can plausibly explain the veto of over 99½% of the housing units located on the White sites."

The Supreme Court agreed. In 1976, *Hills v. Gautreaux* ruled that HUD violated the Constitution and the Fair Housing Act by funding Chicago's housing authority despite knowing about the discrimination. "It is unlawful for public housing either to encourage or perpetuate segregation."

Then came HOPE VI, the program that was supposed to fix everything. Between 1992 and 2010, HOPE VI demolished 98,592 public housing units and built 55,318 replacements. Simple subtraction: 43,000 units gone. Less than 12% of displaced residents moved into replacement housing. Ninety-five percent of those displaced in early HOPE VI funding were families of color.

Congress repealed the requirement for one-for-one replacement in 1998. From the early 1990s to the late 2010s, the country lost 370,000 public housing units. The program designed to help poor people into better housing mostly helped poor people into the street.

## Dignity is already in the law—we just don't apply it to housing

The Supreme Court keeps using the word "dignity." It used the word in *Lawrence v. Texas*, striking down sodomy laws: adults may make intimate choices "and still retain their dignity as free persons." The state cannot "demean their existence or control their destiny." In *Obergefell v. Hodges*, on marriage equality: "They ask for equal dignity in the eyes of the law. The Constitution grants them that right."

The Eighth Amendment runs on dignity. In *Trop v. Dulles*, Chief Justice Warren wrote: "The basic concept underlying the Eighth Amendment is nothing less than the dignity of man." The case involved stripping someone's citizenship—making them stateless. The Court called it "a form of punishment more primitive than torture" because it destroys "the individual's status in organized society."

Think about that phrase. "Status in organized society." What destroys status more completely than having no address? You can't vote without one in most states. Can't get a job. Can't open a bank account. Can't receive mail. The unhoused exist outside organized society exactly the way a stateless person does.

*Lawrence* explicitly tied dignity to "the confines of their homes." Without a home, the entire framework collapses. Privacy requires walls. Autonomy requires a door you can close. Dignity—as the Court uses the word—requires shelter.

Montana's constitution connects these dots: dignity is inviolable, and everyone has the right to pursue life's basic necessities. Scholars call this "substantive dignity"—the idea that dignity isn't just about treatment, it's about the material conditions required for human flourishing. You can't flourish without somewhere to sleep.

## The Court just made it legal to punish people for being homeless

In 2024, the Supreme Court decided *City of Grants Pass v. Johnson*. The question: can a city criminalize sleeping outside when there's nowhere else to go?

The Ninth Circuit had said no. In *Martin v. Boise* (2018), the appeals court ruled that "the Eighth Amendment's prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment bars a city from prosecuting people criminally for sleeping outside on public property when those people have no home or other shelter to go to." The reasoning was simple: "whether sitting, lying, and sleeping are defined as acts or conditions, they are universal and unavoidable consequences of being human."

The Supreme Court reversed. Cities can now fine and jail people for sleeping outside even when no shelter exists. Justice Sotomayor dissented: the decision "leaves the most vulnerable in our society with an impossible choice: Either stay awake or be arrested."

So. The federal government built a system that locked Black Americans out of housing wealth. It demolished their neighborhoods when they built wealth anyway. It concentrated poverty in underfunded public housing then tore that housing down too. It subsidizes homeowners through the mortgage interest deduction—$30 billion a year, mostly to people who don't need it—while cutting rental assistance. And now it's constitutional to arrest people for the predictable result.

State constitutions become essential. Hawaii's Supreme Court recently ruled it unconstitutional for Maui County to confiscate and destroy homeless people's property without a hearing. Washington advocates are pursuing state constitutional claims. Nevada organizers should examine what our constitution might protect.

## The U.S. helped write the international standard—then ignored it

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights, Article 25: "Everyone has the right to a standard of living adequate for the health and well-being of himself and of his family, including food, clothing, housing."

Eleanor Roosevelt chaired the drafting committee. The Declaration emerged from FDR's Four Freedoms, especially "freedom from want." The United States voted yes.

The International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights requires nations to "recognize the right of everyone to an adequate standard of living...including adequate food, clothing and housing." President Carter signed it in 1977. The Senate never ratified it. The U.S. stands virtually alone among developed nations in refusing to recognize housing as a legal right.

UN Special Rapporteurs have visited and documented what they found. Raquel Rolnik's 2010 mission report identified significant gaps between U.S. practice and international human rights standards. The current Special Rapporteur, Balakrishnan Rajagopal, continues to scrutinize American housing policy. The findings aren't flattering.

South Africa shows what's possible. Section 26 of its 1996 Constitution: "Everyone has a right to have access to adequate housing." In *Government v. Grootboom*, the Constitutional Court found the government's housing policy unconstitutional because it failed to provide emergency housing for people in desperate need. The right has teeth.

UN-HABITAT estimates 75% of countries have constitutions or national laws promoting housing rights. The U.S. is the exception.

## Tenants have changed the law before

In 1918, New York City tenants started refusing to pay rent. Tens of thousands joined. The strikes created the first rent control in American history.

During the Depression, Communist-led tenant councils organized eviction defense. When marshals put furniture on the sidewalk, neighbors put it back. The movement returned an estimated 77,000 families to their homes in New York alone.

In 1963, Jesse Gray organized a rent strike in Harlem. Tenants brought dead rats to court to show the conditions they lived in. The strike led to New York's "Rent Strike Law"—giving organized tenants the power to petition for court-appointed building administrators when landlords won't maintain the property.

The legal doctrines protecting tenants today came from these fights.

*Block v. Hirsh* (1921): The Supreme Court upheld rent control. The opinion stated that "housing is a necessary of life." Government can regulate it. The case remains good law.

*Javins v. First National Realty Corp.* (1970): The D.C. Circuit established the implied warranty of habitability. Landlords must provide livable housing; tenants can withhold rent when they don't. The case started with a rent strike at Clifton Terrace Apartments, where organizers documented over 1,000 code violations.

*Edwards v. Habib* (1968): Landlords can't evict tenants for reporting code violations or organizing. The retaliatory eviction defense exists because tenants fought for it.

The current tenant movement is winning. KC Tenants passed a citywide Tenants Bill of Rights, won right to counsel in eviction court, and elected four City Council members. They held a 248-day rent strike and won a collectively bargained lease with a corporate landlord. California, Oregon, and Washington passed statewide rent protections in recent years. New York enacted Good Cause Eviction in 2024, covering millions of market-rate tenants.

Nineteen cities, two counties, and five states passed right-to-counsel laws in the last decade. The tenant movement is building power.

## What we're responsible for

The record shows:

- The federal government required racial segregation in housing from 1934 to 1968.
- HOLC maps denied investment to Black neighborhoods—and 74% remain low-income ninety years later.
- The GI Bill, administered through racist lending, transferred wealth exclusively to white veterans.
- Urban renewal displaced over a million people, 60% of them non-white, to make room for highways and stadiums and universities.
- Public housing was sited to concentrate and segregate poverty.
- Exclusionary zoning spread after racial zoning was outlawed—a workaround that achieved the same ends.
- The mortgage interest deduction sends $30 billion annually to homeowners, disproportionately wealthy, while rental assistance gets cut.
- State preemption laws prevent cities from protecting their own tenants.

This is causation. The government did these things. We are the government. We are responsible for the results.

Richard Rothstein, in *The Color of Law*: "Racial segregation does not just happen; it is made." The same is true for housing scarcity. For unaffordability. For homelessness. Vienna—where 60% of residents live in social housing and renters spend on average 21% of income on rent—proves different policies produce different outcomes.

Every person sleeping outside in Reno tonight is evidence that our system works exactly as designed. They're not market failures. They're policy successes. The policy was to treat housing as an investment vehicle rather than a human need, and on those terms, it's working perfectly.

## Where this leaves Nevada tenants

RSTU exists because tenants in Reno-Sparks decided to build power instead of waiting for permission. This research provides the legal and historical foundation for what you're already doing.

**The law is on your side—more than you might think.** Nevada tenants have the implied warranty of habitability. Landlords must maintain livable conditions; tenants can act when they don't. Retaliation for organizing is illegal. The Fair Housing Act applies here. These rights exist regardless of whether Nevada ever passes rent control.

**Every legal victory in tenant history started with organizing.** Javins came from a rent strike. The Harlem rent strike produced the Rent Strike Law. KC Tenants' wins came from years of building-by-building work before they ran candidates or negotiated leases. The law follows power.

**State constitutional arguments may protect what federal courts won't.** After *Grants Pass*, the Eighth Amendment won't stop cities from criminalizing homelessness. But Nevada's constitution might offer independent grounds. The research is worth doing.

**You're not asking for handouts. You're demanding what the government already promised.** Congress declared a decent home for every American family as national policy in 1949. HUD has an affirmative duty to further fair housing. The government caused this crisis through documented, exposed, admitted policy choices. Demanding remedy isn't charity. It's accountability.

**The tenant movement is winning across the country.** Statewide protections in Oregon and California started with local fights. Municipal victories build toward state legislation. What RSTU builds in Washoe County becomes a model for Nevada.

## The conclusion is a beginning

Housing as commodity is exploitation. We didn't invent that system, but we inherited it, and we benefit from it or suffer under it depending on which side of the deed we stand.

The law already says housing matters. It says dignity is inviolable. It says the government must affirmatively further fair housing. It says the aid and care of the needy shall be provided.

The question isn't whether we have legal and moral grounds to demand housing as a right. We do. The question is whether we'll build enough power to make the law mean what it says.

The Reno-Sparks Tenants Union is Nevada's first tenant organization. The first of anything is hard. But tenant unions have changed the law before—through rent strikes, through eviction defense, through showing up at city council until the council members got tired of seeing the same faces. There's no reason to believe Nevada is different.

Human dignity demands that no person be left without shelter. The law agrees, when we make it. History shows that organized tenants have made it before.

**Now it's our turn.**
